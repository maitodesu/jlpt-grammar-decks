Volitional form - ～(よ)う

Meaning · let's do it; I'll do it - the form a verb wears when it carries a will

The volitional is the plain-form counterpart of ましょう. It does two jobs that English keeps apart: proposing something to the people you are with ("let's go"), and announcing a decision to yourself ("right - I'll go"). It is also the base that a whole family of N4 patterns is built on, so the table below is worth more than the page around it: ～(よ)うと思(おも)う, ～(よ)うとする and ～(よ)うか all need this form and nothing else will do.

Note - verbs come in three classes: う-verbs (五段(ごだん) / godan / Group 1), る-verbs (一段(いちだん) / ichidan / Group 2), and the two irregulars する and 来(く)る. This page says う-verb and る-verb throughout.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · Formation

う-verbs swap the final -u vowel for -o and add う; る-verbs drop る and add よう. That is the whole rule, and unlike the て-form there are no sound changes to memorise.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · Nuance & register

The plain volitional is casual. Used as a proposal it belongs with friends, family, classmates and people below you at work; said to a customer or a 部長(ぶちょう) it is startling. Upward, use ましょう - and even ましょう can presume too much, so ～ましょうか ("shall I / shall we?") and ～ませんか ("won't you?") are the genuinely safe openers.

Intonation splits the two main uses more reliably than grammar does. Flat or falling, 行(い)こう is a decision you are handing to the group. With か on the end, 行(い)こうか hands the decision back to them. Adding よ - 行(い)こうよ - pushes, and can sound like coaxing or whining depending on who is speaking.

Two colloquial tails worth recognising:

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · Nuance & register (cont.)

  • ～(よ)うっと is a private mutter, not a proposal. Nobody is being invited:
    「あー、もう寝(ね)ようっと。」 It sounds relaxed and slightly childlike, and it is common on social media.
  • ～(よ)うか迷(まよ)っている ("I can't decide whether to…") is the standard
    way to say you are torn: 買(か)おうか迷(まよ)っています。

In writing, the volitional turns up as the essayist's "let us": 次(つぎ)に、この問題(もんだい)について考(かんが)えよう。 That is a register away from the friendly spoken use, but it is the same form. The stiff literary ～であろう ("it may well be that…") is something to recognise in print and never to say out loud.

ましょう has one job the plain form cannot do: it is polite. It cannot, however, be embedded - ～ましょうと思(おも)う is not Japanese. Whenever the volitional is followed by something, it goes in plain.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · ～(よ)う vs ～ましょう

Rule of thumb - same meaning, different politeness. ましょう is the polite sentence-final form; ～(よ)う is the plain one, and it is the only one that can have something attached after it.

みんなで歌(うた)おう。
Let's all sing.

みんなで歌(うた)いましょう。
Let's all sing. (polite / addressed to a group you are leading)

The moment another clause follows, politeness moves to the end of the sentence and the volitional reverts to plain:

来週(らいしゅう)から走(はし)ろうと思(おも)っています。
I'm thinking of taking up running from next week.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · ～(よ)う vs ～ましょう (cont.)

❌走(はし)りましょうと思(おも)っています is impossible. Politeness is marked once, at the end.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · ～(よ)う vs ～ませんか

Rule of thumb - ～(よ)う and ましょう assume the answer is yes. ～ませんか genuinely asks, so it is the polite way to invite someone who is free to refuse.

昼(ひる)ごはん、いっしょに食(た)べよう。
Let's have lunch together. (we're already doing this)

昼(ひる)ごはん、いっしょに食(た)べませんか。
Would you like to have lunch together? (a real invitation)

Inviting someone for the first time, ませんか is almost always the better choice; ましょう after they have agreed is what settles the details. A useful ordering from most to least presumptuous: 行(い)こう → 行(い)きましょう → 行(い)きましょうか → 行(い)きませんか. Treat that as a tendency, not a rulebook - tone of voice moves any of them.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · ～(よ)う vs the plain non-past

Rule of thumb - English "will" is usually not the volitional. A plain future statement is just the dictionary form or ます; the volitional adds the feeling of a will being exercised at this moment.

明日(あした)、病院(びょういん)へ行(い)きます。
I'm going to the hospital tomorrow. (a fact about my schedule)

やっぱり明日(あした)、病院(びょういん)へ行(い)こう。
You know what - I'll go to the hospital tomorrow. (deciding as I speak)

This is why the volitional cannot report the weather, someone else's plans, or anything that happens without a decision. Those all take the plain form, with でしょう or と思(おも)います if you need to hedge.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 手伝(てつだ)うよう → ✅ 手伝(てつだ)おう
    A う-verb ending in う becomes おう, not うよう. The よう ending belongs to る-verbs only.

  • ❌ 食(た)べろう → ✅ 食(た)べよう
    食(た)べる is a る-verb: drop る, add よう. ろう would be the う-verb pattern, which is why 帰(かえ)る - a う-verb despite its looks - really is 帰(かえ)ろう.

  • ❌ 来(く)る → きよう → ✅ 来(く)る → こよう
    The irregular shifts to the こ stem, the same one you already use for 来(こ)ない. Reading the written form 来(こ)よう as kiyou is a very common slip.

  • ❌ 明日(あした)は雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)ろう。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)るでしょう。
    Snow has no intentions. The volitional needs a subject that can choose.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 課長(かちょう)、コーヒーを飲(の)もう。 → ✅ 課長(かちょう)、コーヒーでもいかがですか。
    Grammatically fine, socially wrong. Do not propose things to a superior in the plain volitional.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 映画(えいが)を見(み)よう。 (b) 映画(えいが)を見(み)ようか。

(a) is a decision presented as already taken - you are telling the group what is happening, in a friendly way. It also works as a resolution said to yourself.

(b) hands the choice back: "shall we watch a film?" The か makes it a genuine question, so the other person can say no without pushing back against you. In a group where you are not the one who decides, (b) is the safer sentence.

～(よ)うと思う

Meaning · I think I'll ～; I'm thinking of ～ing - an intention, presented as a thought rather than a fact

～(よ)うと思(おも)う takes the volitional - the "let's / I'll" form - and wraps it in "I think". The result is the most natural way in Japanese to answer what are you going to do? It is softer than つもり because it frames the plan as something going on in your head right now, which leaves the door open. Note the shape: what you are thinking is 行(い)こう, "I'll go"; と quotes that thought.

～(よ)うと思う · Formation

The verb before と is the plain volitional, always. ましょう cannot be quoted here.

～(よ)うと思う · Nuance & register

The whole point of this pattern is softness. つもり states a settled plan; ～(よ)うと思(おも)います reports a thought, and a thought can change without anyone losing face. That makes it the default answer to a superior, a customer, or anyone whose reaction you have not heard yet.

The ています layer does most of the fine-tuning:

  • ～(よ)うと思(おも)います - the decision is being taken as you
    speak    . It fits a reply on the spot: 「じゃあ、そうしようと思(おも)います。」
  • ～(よ)うと思(おも)っています - you have been holding this intention
    for a while. It sounds more considered and, in practice, closer to a real plan. In a job interview or a meeting this is the form you want.

～(よ)うと思う · Nuance & register (cont.)

The same ています is what lets you talk about other people. Exactly as with 思(おも)う generally, a bare ～(よ)うと思(おも)う claims the thought is yours; someone else's intention needs 思(おも)っている, or evidence marking （思(おも)っているようです, 思(おも)っていると言(い)っていました）.

One structural point that catches people: the person doing the volitional verb must be the same person doing the thinking. You can say 私(わたし)が手伝(てつだ)おうと思(おも)います ("I'll help"), but you cannot put someone else in there - ❌彼(かれ)が来(こ)ようと思(おも)います is impossible. To talk about what you want others to do, you need a different pattern altogether.

～(よ)うと思う · Nuance & register (cont.)

Register is otherwise neutral. Plain 行(い)こうと思(おも)う is everyday casual speech; in stiffer written Japanese you will meet ～(よ)うと考(かんが)えております and ～たいと存(ぞん)じます, which do the same softening job in keigo. Adding か - ～(よ)うかと思(おも)っています - softens further, to roughly "I've half a mind to…", and is very useful when you genuinely have not decided.

～(よ)うと思う · ～(よ)うと思(おも)う vs ～つもりだ

Rule of thumb - ～(よ)うと思(おも)う is a decision being made or reported now; つもり is a decision already settled. Neither one is English "will".

来年(らいねん)、一人(ひとり)で住(す)もうと思(おも)っています。
I'm thinking of living on my own next year.

来年(らいねん)、一人(ひとり)で住(す)むつもりです。
I intend to live on my own next year.

The first invites a response - the listener could still say "are you sure?" and not be contradicting you. The second reports a fixed position, and pushing back against it means arguing. This is why ～(よ)うと思(おも)います is the safer thing to say when your plan touches someone else's, and why つもり is the right choice when you want to be believed.

～(よ)うと思う · ～(よ)うと思(おも)う vs ～つもりだ (cont.)

There is also a hard difference: つもり can be denied （行(い)くつもりはありません）and questioned about others （どうするつもりですか, which sounds like an interrogation). The 思(おも)う pattern has no equivalent edge to it.

～(よ)うと思う · ～(よ)うと思(おも)う vs ～と思(おも)う

Rule of thumb - volitional + と思(おも)う = my intention. Plain form + と思(おも)う = my opinion or prediction about how things will go.

私(わたし)も行(い)こうと思(おも)います。
I think I'll go too. (I'm deciding to go)

田中(たなか)さんも行(い)くと思(おも)います。
I think Tanaka will go too. (my guess about him)

Get this pair wrong and the sentence does not become ungrammatical - it becomes a different statement, which is worse. Saying 私(わたし)も行(い)くと思(おも)います about yourself sounds oddly detached, as though you were predicting your own behaviour from the outside. It is occasionally said, but it is not how you volunteer.

～(よ)うと思う · ～(よ)うと思(おも)う vs ～と思(おも)う (cont.)

Because the plain form is for prediction, it is also what you use for events nobody decides: 明日(あした)は晴(は)れると思(おも)います.

～(よ)うと思(おも)います vs ～(よ)うと思(おも)っています

Rule of thumb - 思(おも)います is the thought arriving now; 思(おも)っています is a thought you have been carrying.

それはいい案(あん)ですね。じゃあ、そうしようと思(おも)います。
That's a good idea. All right, I'll do that.

前(まえ)から会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めようと思(おも)っていました。
I'd been thinking about leaving the company for a while.

In the first, the decision is a response to what was just said, so 思(おも)います is right and 思(おも)っています would sound like you had planned it in advance. In the second, the ていました is doing real work: it says the intention predates this conversation.

～(よ)うと思う · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 行(い)きましょうと思(おも)います。 → ✅ 行(い)こうと思(おも)います。
    Only the plain volitional can be quoted with と. The politeness sits on 思(おも)います.

  • ❌ 田中(たなか)さんは会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めようと思(おも)います。 → ✅ …辞(や)めようと思(おも)っています。
    A bare 思(おも)う asserts your own mind. For a third person use 思(おも)っている, or hedge with 思(おも)っているようです.

  • ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ろうと思(おも)います。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ると思(おも)います。
    Weather has no volition, so it cannot take the volitional. Plain form + と思(おも)う is prediction.

  • ❌ 買(か)わないようと思(おも)います。 → ✅ 買(か)うのはやめようと思(おも)います。
    There is no everyday negative volitional. Negate the plan, not the verb.

～(よ)うと思う · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 彼(かれ)に手伝(てつだ)おうと思(おも)います。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)に手伝(てつだ)ってもらおうと思(おも)います。
    The volitional describes what you will do. If someone else does the acting, the sentence has to be rebuilt around that.

You cannot state a third person's inner decision with a bare 思(おも)う. 思(おも)っている presents it as a state you have observed. …と言(い)っていました would work too, and marks where the information came from.

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 明日(あした)、銀行(ぎんこう)へ行(い)こうと思(おも)います。 (b) 明日(あした)、銀行(ぎんこう)へ行(い)くと思(おも)います。

(a) is your own intention: the volitional says the going is your decision, and と思(おも)います presents it gently. This is what you say when someone asks about your plans.

(b) predicts. With the plain form, と思(おも)います gives an opinion about what will happen - most naturally about somebody else, and about yourself it sounds like you are guessing at your own future rather than choosing it. Learners produce (b) by translating "I think I'll go", which is exactly the wrong route.

～(よ)うとする

Meaning · to try to do something; to be on the point of doing it

～(よ)うとする takes the volitional - the will to do something - and puts it into a verb. It catches the moment before the action succeeds: the arm reaching for the handle, the sun touching the horizon. That is why the pattern so often ends in a "but": you tried, and something got in the way.

The meeting was on the point of starting, so I hurried to my seat.

～(よ)うとする · Nuance & register

Three uses live under one form, and they behave differently.

1. The interrupted attempt. In the past tense, ～(よ)うとした almost always implies that the action did not come off, or that something cut across it. If you tried and succeeded, Japanese just reports the success: 窓(まど)を開(あ)けました. This is a real difference from English, where "I tried to open the window" can be modest understatement for "I opened it". Here it means the window did not open.

2. On the verge. ～(よ)うとしている and ～(よ)うとしたとき mark the instant before something happens, and this is the one use that works with subjects that have no will at all: 台風(たいふう)が上陸(じょうりく)しようとしている, 一年(いちねん)が終(お)わろうとしている. It has a slightly heightened, narrative feel - common in news writing and novels, less so in casual chat, where もうすぐ or ところ does the job.

～(よ)うとする · Nuance & register (cont.)

3. Refusal. ～(よ)うとしない says someone is unwilling, not merely that they are not doing it. It carries a note of complaint, so it is comfortable about children, animals and people you are annoyed with, and risky about a superior: 部長(ぶちょう)は決(き)めようとしません is close to saying the boss is being obstructive.

A restriction worth internalising: for your own ongoing effort, the plain non-past ～(よ)うとする is unnatural. ❌毎日(まいにち)運動(うんどう)しようとしています should be 運動(うんどう)するようにしています. Reserve ～(よ)うとする for a single moment of trying, not a standing policy. The plain non-past does appear in general statements about people - 人(ひと)はだれでも楽(らく)をしようとする - where it is descriptive rather than personal.

Register is neutral. する carries the politeness, so 開(あ)けようとしました is as polite as its ending, and nothing about the pattern is formal or slangy in itself.

～(よ)うとする · ～(よ)うとする vs ～てみる

Rule of thumb - ～(よ)うとする is the attempt, which may never get off the ground. ～てみる is doing it to find out what happens - the action really takes place.

This pair is the classic confusion, because English uses "try" for both.

窓(まど)を開(あ)けようとしたが、固(かた)くて動(うご)かなかった。
I tried to open the window, but it was stiff and wouldn't budge.

窓(まど)を開(あ)けてみたら、涼(すず)しい風(かぜ)が入(はい)ってきた。
I opened the window to see, and a cool breeze came in.

～(よ)うとする · ～(よ)うとする vs ～てみる (cont.)

In the first, the window stayed shut. In the second it opened - the "trying" was about the result being unknown, not about whether the act was possible. So 納豆(なっとう)を食(た)べてみました means you ate some and now you know what it tastes like, while 納豆(なっとう)を食(た)べようとしました means you got as far as raising it to your mouth.

Note - てみる cannot express failure, and ～(よ)うとする cannot express curiosity. Neither can substitute for the other, and the two can be stacked when both ideas are present: 食(た)べてみようとしました - "I made an attempt at tasting it."

～(よ)うとする · ～(よ)うとしている vs ～ところだ

Rule of thumb - both mean "about to". ところだ is the everyday, neutral one and needs a subject with a plan. ～(よ)うとしている is more vivid, and it works for events nobody chose.

今(いま)、出(で)かけるところです。
I'm just about to head out.

電車(でんしゃ)が出発(しゅっぱつ)しようとしている。
The train is on the point of departing.

Swap them and both sentences get strange. 出(で)かけようとしています about yourself sounds like you are narrating your own life; a train has no ところ of its own to be at.

～(よ)うとする · ～(よ)うとしない vs the plain negative

Rule of thumb - ～ない reports that something is not happening. ～(よ)うとしない says the person is refusing to even try.

子(こ)どもが薬(くすり)を飲(の)まない。
My child isn't taking the medicine.

子(こ)どもが薬(くすり)を飲(の)もうとしない。
My child won't take the medicine. (clamps his mouth shut every time)

The second is a complaint. Because it attributes stubbornness, it is common in speech about kids and pets and best avoided about people you are supposed to defer to.

～(よ)うとする · Common mistakes

  • ❌ ドアを開(あ)けるとした → ✅ ドアを開(あ)けようとした
    とする attaches to the volitional, not the dictionary form. The dictionary form with とする is a different, much more advanced pattern meaning "supposing that".

  • ❌ 毎日(まいにち)漢字(かんじ)を覚(おぼ)えようとしています。 → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)漢字(かんじ)を覚(おぼ)えるようにしています。
    A standing effort of your own is ～ようにする. ～(よ)うとする is one moment of trying, not a habit.

  • ❌ ケーキを作(つく)ろうとしました。おいしかったです。 → ✅ ケーキを作(つく)ってみました。おいしかったです。
    If the cake got made and eaten, the attempt succeeded, so ～(よ)うとした is the wrong verb. Use てみる, or simply 作(つく)りました.

～(よ)うとする · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 先生(せんせい)はまだ来(こ)ようとしません。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)はまだ来(き)ていません。
    ～(よ)うとしない accuses the teacher of refusing to come. For a plain statement that someone has not arrived, use ている with a negative.

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 彼(かれ)は質問(しつもん)に答(こた)えなかった。 (b) 彼(かれ)は質問(しつもん)に答(こた)えようとしなかった。

～予定だ

Meaning · to be scheduled to; to be due to - the plan is on a calendar, not just in your head

予定(よてい) is an ordinary noun meaning schedule or arrangement, so 行(い)く予定(よてい)です says literally "the arrangement is that I go". That is why it feels so different from つもり, which reports what you have decided: 予定(よてい) points at something outside you - a date, a booking, a timetable other people can also see.

～予定だ · Nuance & register

予定(よてい) is impersonal by design, and that is its whole value. It reports an arrangement without saying whose will produced it, so it can describe a plane, a shop's opening hours, a company's project or your own Tuesday with the same words. つもり, by contrast, can only ever describe a person's intention.

That neutrality makes it the polite register for other people's plans. Asking a superior ご予定(よてい)はいかがでしょうか is safe; asking about their つもり sounds like an interrogation. In business Japanese the noun turns up constantly on its own - 予定(よてい)を立(た)てる (draw up a schedule), 予定(よてい)を変更(へんこう)する (reschedule), 予定(よてい)どおり (as planned) - and ～予定(よてい)となっております is the standard humble phrasing for telling a customer when something will happen.

Two limits to keep straight:

～予定だ · Nuance & register (cont.)

  • An arrangement is not a forecast. Something has to have been arranged by
    someone. ❌明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)る予定(よてい)です is wrong; weather takes 予報(よほう), でしょう or そうです. A train arriving at 10:04 is fine, because a timetable is an arrangement.
  • 予定(よてい)でした implies it did not go that way. Like
    つもりだった, the past tense sets up a "but". If the plan was kept, say 予定(よてい)どおり終(お)わりました.

Because 予定(よてい) keeps you out of the sentence, it can also be used to duck. Answering 参加(さんか)する予定(よてい)です instead of 参加(さんか)します quietly leaves room for the schedule to change - useful, and occasionally heard as non-committal.

～予定だ · ～予定(よてい)だ vs ～つもりだ

Rule of thumb - 予定(よてい) is what is booked; つもり is what you have decided. If nothing exists outside your own head, it is not a 予定(よてい).

来月(らいげつ)、京都(きょうと)で友(とも)だちに会(あ)う予定(よてい)です。
I'm meeting a friend in Kyoto next month. (arranged, date agreed)

来月(らいげつ)、京都(きょうと)で友(とも)だちに会(あ)うつもりです。
I mean to see a friend in Kyoto next month. (my plan; nothing fixed yet)

The corollary is that 予定(よてい) does not carry determination. Pairing it with a word like 絶対(ぜったい)に or 今年(ことし)こそ is odd, because those express resolve and a schedule has none. Resolve is つもり territory.

～予定だ · ～予定(よてい)だ vs ～(よ)うと思(おも)っている

Rule of thumb - three degrees of firmness: ～(よ)うと思(おも)っています (a thought) → つもりです (a decision) → 予定(よてい)です (an entry in the diary).

夏休(なつやす)みは北海道(ほっかいどう)へ行(い)こうと思(おも)っています。
I'm thinking of going to Hokkaido in the summer holidays.

夏休(なつやす)みは北海道(ほっかいどう)へ行(い)く予定(よてい)です。
I'm going to Hokkaido in the summer holidays. (tickets are booked)

Answering a colleague's casual question with the second when nothing is booked overstates your case; answering a travel agent with the first understates it. Match the form to how real the plan is.

～予定だ · ～予定(よてい)だ vs ～ことになっている

Rule of thumb - 予定(よてい) is a dated item: this happens, then. ことになっている is a standing arrangement or rule: this is how it is done.

来週(らいしゅう)の月曜日(げつようび)に試験(しけん)を受(う)ける予定(よてい)です。
I'm scheduled to sit the exam next Monday.

この部屋(へや)では靴(くつ)を脱(ぬ)ぐことになっています。
You're supposed to take your shoes off in this room.

Both hide the person who decided, which is why they feel related. 予定(よてい) attaches to a point in time and can be missed; ことになっている attaches to a practice and can be broken.

    予定(よてい) is a noun, so the clause in front of it is plain. The politeness goes once, on です.

  • ❌ 明日(あした)は雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)る予定(よてい)です。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)るそうです。
    Nobody schedules snow. 予定(よてい) needs an arrangement behind it; a forecast is 予報(よほう).

  • ❌ 来月(らいげつ)は引(ひ)っ越(こ)し予定(よてい)です。 → ✅ 来月(らいげつ)は引(ひ)っ越(こ)しの予定(よてい)です。
    Two nouns are joined with の. This is the same rule that gives 冗談(じょうだん)のつもり.

    今年(ことし)こそ is a declaration of will, and a schedule has no will. Determination belongs with つもり.

  • ❌ 先週(せんしゅう)の会議(かいぎ)に出(で)る予定(よてい)でした。（and you did go） → ✅ 先週(せんしゅう)の会議(かいぎ)には予定(よてい)どおり出(で)ました。

～ことにする

Meaning · to decide to do something - and the person deciding is the speaker

N5 gave you にする for choosing a noun: コーヒーにします, "I'll have the coffee." A verb cannot sit in that slot, so こと is added to turn the whole action into a thing that can be chosen. 歩(ある)くことにします is "I'll go with walking." The pattern always carries an agent, and unless the sentence says otherwise, that agent is you.

Rule of thumb - こと exists to nominalise. ❌行(い)くにする is impossible and ❌行(い)きますことにする is impossible; the verb goes in plain form and こと does the rest.

～ことにする · Nuance & register

The tense of する is doing more work than learners expect.

  • ことにしました - the decision is already made, and you are
    reporting it. This is by far the commonest form, and it is what English "I've decided to…" corresponds to.
  • ことにします - you are deciding at this moment, often out loud,
    in response to what has just been said: 「じゃあ、そうすることにします。」
  • ことにしている - not a single decision but a policy you keep to.
    肉(にく)は食(た)べないことにしています is "I don't eat meat" said as a rule you have adopted, which is subtly different from 食(た)べません (I don't) or 食(た)べられません (I can't).

～ことにする · Nuance & register (cont.)

～たことにする and ～なかったことにする mean treat it as though. They are enormously useful and slightly conspiratorial: 今(いま)のは聞(き)かなかったことにしてください ("please forget I said that"), この話(はなし)はなかったことにしましょう ("let's call the whole thing off"). Nothing about this is rude, but it does presuppose that both parties are willing to bend the record a little.

One sociolinguistic point worth knowing. In Japanese workplaces, people frequently report their own decisions with ことになりました - the became version - even when they made the choice themselves: 来月(らいげつ)、退職(たいしょく)することになりました. Saying ことにしました there is not wrong, but it puts your own will in the foreground, and the tendency is to avoid that when announcing something that affects other people. Treat this as a tendency, not a rule: among friends ことにした is completely normal.

The pattern itself is register-neutral, and する carries the politeness.

～ことにする · ～ことにする vs ～ことになる

Rule of thumb - who decided? ことにする: I did. ことになる: someone or something else did, or it simply worked out that way.

会社(かいしゃ)を辞(や)めることにしました。
I've decided to leave the company.

来月(らいげつ)から大阪(おおさか)で働(はたら)くことになりました。
It's been arranged that I'll be working in Osaka from next month.

The first is a resignation you chose. The second is a transfer that landed on you - or that you did engineer, but are presenting as an outcome. This is the single distinction that runs through both patterns, and it is the one exam questions test.

～ことにする · ～ことにする vs ～にする

Rule of thumb - にする chooses a noun; ことにする chooses an action. こと is the adapter between them.

昼(ひる)ごはんはそばにします。
I'll have soba for lunch.

昼(ひる)ごはんは外(そと)で食(た)べることにします。
I'll eat out for lunch.

Both are decisions and both use the same verb. What changes is only whether the thing being decided on is a noun or a whole verb phrase.

～ことにする · ～ことにしている vs ～ようにしている

Rule of thumb - ことにしている is a rule you follow; ようにしている is an effort you make. The first is kept or broken; the second is only ever attempted.

エレベーターは使(つか)わないことにしています。
I make it a rule not to use the lift.

なるべく階段(かいだん)を使(つか)うようにしています。
I try to use the stairs where I can.

The first sounds absolute - you have decided, and that is that. The second admits the days you take the lift anyway. Adverbs give it away: なるべく, できるだけ and 少(すこ)しずつ sit naturally with ようにしている and awkwardly with ことにしている.

～ことにする · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 毎日(まいにち)歩(ある)くにする。 → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)歩(ある)くことにする。
    A verb cannot attach to にする on its own. こと turns it into a noun first.

  • ❌ 行(い)きますことにしました。 → ✅ 行(い)くことにしました。
    こと is a noun being modified, so the clause in front of it is plain. The politeness is on しました.

  • ❌ 会議(かいぎ)は三時(さんじ)に始(はじ)まることにしました。 → ✅ 会議(かいぎ)は三時(さんじ)に始(はじ)めることにしました。
    始(はじ)まる is the intransitive verb - things starting by themselves - and it cannot be the object of a decision. If you decided, use the transitive 始(はじ)める; if the schedule decided, use 始(はじ)まることになりました.

  • ❌ 来週(らいしゅう)から東京(とうきょう)へ転勤(てんきん)することにしました。（the company moved you） → ✅ …転勤(てんきん)することになりました。
    If the choice was not yours, ことにする claims credit you do not have and sounds odd to a listener who knows how transfers work.

～ことにする · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 明日(あした)晴(は)れることにします。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は晴(は)れるでしょう。
    You can only decide things you control. Weather, arriving trains and other people's feelings are outside the pattern.

～ことになる

Meaning · it has been decided that ～; it works out that ～ - an outcome, with nobody named as having decided it

ことになる is the mirror image of ことにする. なる is the verb of things happening by themselves, so the sentence reports a decision or an arrangement as a result: something has been settled, and the sentence declines to say by whom. English almost always has to supply an agent ("they decided", "I've been transferred"); Japanese does not, and that gap is what makes this pattern feel alien at first.

～ことになる · Nuance & register

Three things live inside this one form.

1. A decision made elsewhere. The everyday use. 来月(らいげつ)、支店(してん)に移(うつ)ることになりました - the company decided, and you are passing on the result. Because no agent is named, the sentence works equally well when the decider is a committee, a rule, a circumstance, or nobody at all.

2. A rule in force. ことになっている is how Japanese states regulations, customs and standing arrangements: 会社(かいしゃ)では九時(くじ)までに来(く)ることになっています, ここでは写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)らないことになっています. The ている is essential - it says the decision is not news but the state of things. This is also the polite way to refuse: rather than "you can't", you say "it isn't done", and nobody has to be the one saying no.

～ことになる · Nuance & register (cont.)

3. Logical consequence. ということになる means that amounts to. It is how you sum up: では、来週(らいしゅう)は休(やす)みということになりますね. Here nothing has been decided at all; the sentence is doing arithmetic.

The social point is the interesting one. Japanese speakers very often report their own decisions with ことになりました: 来月(らいげつ)退職(たいしょく)することになりました, said by someone who resigned of their own accord. This is not deception. Putting the outcome forward instead of your will is a way of not imposing, and it is close to standard for announcements - marriages, job changes, moves - that affect the people listening. The tendency is strong in workplaces and weaker among close friends, where ことにした is perfectly normal. Read ことになりました as "here is what has been settled", and do not assume the speaker was passive.

～ことになる · Nuance & register (cont.)

Register is neutral; ことになっております is the humble business version and belongs in customer-facing speech.

～ことになる · ～ことになる vs ～ことにする

Rule of thumb - ことにする names the decider (you). ことになる reports the outcome and leaves the decider out. That is the whole distinction.

部屋(へや)を変(か)えてもらうことにしました。
I decided to ask them to change my room.

部屋(へや)を変(か)えてもらうことになりました。
It's been arranged that they'll change my room for me.

Same event, two framings. The first foregrounds your initiative; the second foregrounds the arrangement. Neither is more truthful - Japanese simply offers a choice about how much of your own will to put on the table, and takes the modest option more often than English does.

～ことになる · ～ことになった vs ～ことになっている

Rule of thumb - ことになった is the moment something was settled; ことになっている is the state of its being settled. News versus rule.

来月(らいげつ)から制服(せいふく)を着(き)ることになりました。
From next month we'll be wearing uniforms. (this has just been decided)

この学校(がっこう)では制服(せいふく)を着(き)ることになっています。
At this school you wear a uniform. (that's how it is)

Use the past form to announce a change, and the ている form to explain the way things are. Saying ことになりました about a rule that has been in place for decades makes it sound like this morning's memo.

～ことになる · ～ことになる vs ～ようになる

Rule of thumb - ことになる is a decision landing; ようになる is a change arriving - a new ability, or a habit that grew.

来年(らいねん)、海外(かいがい)で働(はたら)くことになりました。
It's been settled that I'll work abroad next year.

一年(いちねん)習(なら)って、英語(えいご)で話(はな)せるようになりました。
After a year of lessons, I've got to the point where I can hold a conversation in English.

The first happened at a stroke, because someone decided. The second happened gradually, because you changed. If you can point to a meeting where it was agreed, it is ことになる.

～ことになる · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 行(い)きますことになりました。 → ✅ 行(い)くことになりました。
    こと is a noun being modified, so the verb before it is plain.

  • ❌ この会社(かいしゃ)では制服(せいふく)を着(き)ることになりました。（describing a long-standing rule） → ✅ …着(き)ることになっています。
    A rule already in force needs the ている form. The past tense announces a change that has just been made.

  • ❌ 今晩(こんばん)はカレーを作(つく)ることになります。（your own choice） → ✅ 今晩(こんばん)はカレーを作(つく)ることにします。
    If you are the one deciding and there is no arrangement to report, ことにする is the honest form. ことになる here sounds as though the curry were imposed on you.

  • ❌ 来週(らいしゅう)から忙(いそが)しいことになります。 → ✅ 来週(らいしゅう)から忙(いそが)しくなります。

～ようにする

Meaning · to make an effort to do something; to see to it that something is the case

The よう here is the noun meaning manner or way, so 歩(ある)くようにする is literally "act in such a way that walking happens." That gives the pattern its two faces: an ongoing effort you make about your own behaviour, and an arrangement you put in place so that some state holds. Both are about steering, not about a single act - which is exactly what separates it from ことにする.

～ようにする · Nuance & register

The commonest form by far is ようにしている. It says: this is something I aim at, and I mostly manage it. Crucially, it does not claim success every time - which is why it is the honest way to describe a resolution you keep about eighty percent of. It sits naturally with hedging adverbs: なるべく, できるだけ, 少(すこ)しずつ, 毎日(まいにち). Compare the flat present tense 毎日(まいにち)歩(ある)きます, which simply asserts that you do.

ようにします - non-past, no ている - is a promise to start. 今度(こんど)から気(き)をつけるようにします is the standard thing to say after being corrected: it accepts the point and commits to changing without claiming you have already changed.

～ようにする · Nuance & register (cont.)

ようにしてください is worth its own paragraph. Beside plain てください it is less immediate and more supervisory. てください asks for an action now; ようにしてください asks you to arrange your behaviour so that something is generally true. That makes it the natural form for rules, instructions and reminders - and it can carry a faint note of "I shouldn't have to say this", so among friends it is heavier than it looks. A teacher, a doctor or a manual will use it constantly.

The second flavour of the pattern - arranging conditions - usually shows up with potential or intransitive verbs: 子(こ)どもが触(さわ)れないようにする ("make it so children can't touch it"), 後(うし)ろからも聞(き)こえるようにする ("make it audible from the back"). Here nobody is making an effort; something is being set up.

Register is neutral, and する carries the politeness as usual. One small pitfall: よう used this way is not the よう of 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るようだ ("it seems"). Same syllable, unrelated job.

～ようにする · ～ようにする vs ～ことにする

Rule of thumb - ことにする is a decision, taken once. ようにする is an effort, made repeatedly. A decision is announced; an effort is only ever aimed at.

来月(らいげつ)からジムに通(かよ)うことにしました。
I've decided to start going to the gym next month.

週(しゅう)に二回(にかい)はジムに行(い)くようにしています。
I try to get to the gym twice a week.

The first is a single event: you decided. The second describes how you conduct yourself over time, and it quietly admits the weeks you miss. This is why a one-off plan cannot take ようにする: ❌来週(らいしゅう)、京都(きょうと)へ行(い)くようにしました is wrong, because a single trip is not a policy.

～ようにする · ～ようにする vs ～ようになる

Rule of thumb - する is you steering; なる is the change arriving. Effort versus result.

漢字(かんじ)を毎日(まいにち)十個(じゅっこ)ずつ覚(おぼ)えるようにしました。
I made a point of learning ten kanji a day.

新聞(しんぶん)が少(すこ)し読(よ)めるようになりました。
I've got to the point where I can read a newspaper a little.

The first is the work; the second is what the work produced. A very common error is to use する where the sentence is really about a change you underwent: ❌日本語(にほんご)が話(はな)せるようにしました claims you engineered your own fluency, and native ears hear it as strange. You became able - なりました.

～ようにする · ～ようにしてください vs ～てください

Rule of thumb - てください asks for one action. ようにしてください asks you to make it generally so, and often carries an ongoing obligation.

まどを閉(し)めてください。
Please close the window.

出(で)かけるときは、まどを閉(し)めるようにしてください。
Please make sure you close the windows when you go out.

The first is about this window, now. The second is a standing instruction, and it implies that on some past occasion the windows were left open. That implication is why the form can sting: to a colleague, 返事(へんじ)を早(はや)く出(だ)すようにしてください lands closer to a reprimand than a request.

  • ❌ 歩(ある)きますようにしています。 → ✅ 歩(ある)くようにしています。
    よう is a noun, so the verb in front of it is plain.

  • ❌ 甘(あま)いものを食(た)べるようにしていません。 → ✅ 甘(あま)いものを食(た)べないようにしています。
    The negative belongs on the main verb. Negating する denies that you are making any effort at all.

  • ❌ 来週(らいしゅう)、映画(えいが)を見(み)に行(い)くようにしました。 → ✅ 来週(らいしゅう)、映画(えいが)を見(み)に行(い)くことにしました。
    A single planned outing is a decision, not a policy. ようにする needs something repeated or maintained.

  • ❌ 一年(いちねん)勉強(べんきょう)して、日本語(にほんご)が話(はな)せるようにしました。 → ✅ …話(はな)せるようになりました。
    You did not arrange your own fluency; you acquired it. Change that happens to you takes なる.

～ようにする · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 毎日(まいにち)野菜(やさい)を食(た)べるようになっています。（meaning "I try to"） → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)野菜(やさい)を食(た)べるようにしています。
    ようになっている would report a state that came about by itself. If the effort is yours, it is して.

～ようにする · Practice 1

1. 健康(けんこう)のために、エレベーターを使(つか)わない____しています。

使(つか)わないようにしています。

An ongoing effort about your own behaviour is ようにする, and the negative sits on 使(つか)う. The ている says the effort is still running.

～ようにする · Practice 2

2. 来月(らいげつ)、引(ひ)っ越(こ)す（ようにしました・ことにしました）。

引(ひ)っ越(こ)すことにしました。

Moving house happens once, so it is a decision, not a policy. ようにする needs something you do repeatedly or keep true over time.

～ようにする · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "Please make sure you don't forget your umbrella."

かさを忘(わす)れないようにしてください。

ようにしてください asks the listener to arrange things so that forgetting does not happen, which is what "make sure" means. Plain 忘(わす)れないでください is also possible and slightly more direct - it addresses this one umbrella rather than the habit.

～ようにする · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 三年(さんねん)練習(れんしゅう)して、ピアノが上手(じょうず)に弾(ひ)けるようにしました。

✅ 三年(さんねん)練習(れんしゅう)して、ピアノが上手(じょうず)に弾(ひ)けるようになりました。

The sentence is about a change the speaker underwent as a result of practice, so it needs なる. ようにしました would claim you arranged your own ability, which is not something a person can do to themselves.

～ようにする · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 毎朝(まいあさ)六時(ろくじ)に起(お)きます。 (b) 毎朝(まいあさ)六時(ろくじ)に起(お)きるようにしています。

(a) is a plain statement of fact: this is what happens every morning. It makes no reference to effort and implies no exceptions.

(b) says you aim at six and generally get there. The ようにしている frames it as a policy you maintain, which both credits you with the discipline and quietly leaves room for the mornings it does not work out. If someone asks how you manage such an early start, (b) is the modest, natural answer.

～ようになる

Meaning · to reach the point where ～; to come to do ～ - a change that has taken place over time

N5 introduced なる with the note that verbs need ように in front of it. This is that pattern in full. ようになる marks a threshold crossed: there was a time when this was not so, and now it is. That is why it is the natural home of the potential form - 話(はな)せるようになりました, "I've got to where I can speak" - and why it cannot describe a single event.

～ようになる · Formation

Attaches to Form Example
Verb dictionary form + ようになる 泳(およ)ぐ → 泳(およ)ぐようになる
Verb (potential) potential form + ようになる 泳(およ)げる → 泳(およ)げるようになる
Verb (negative) ない-form → なくなる 泳(およ)がない → 泳(およ)がなくなる
Change completed ようになった／なりました 泳(およ)げるようになりました
State that has come about ようになっている 自動(じどう)で閉(し)まるようになっている
い-adjective (no よう) drop い + くなる 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)くなる
な-adjective / Noun (no よう) + に + なる 静(しず)か → 静(しず)かになる

～ようになる · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - ように is the adapter that lets a verb plug into なる. Adjectives and nouns already have their own connectors (く and に) and must not take it: ❌寒(さむ)いようになる.

Note - the negative is the exception you have to memorise. In principle 飲(の)まないようになる is formable, but ordinary Japanese contracts it to 飲(の)まなくなる, treating ない as the い-adjective it grammatically is. Use なくなる; you will meet ないようになる occasionally, usually where the speaker wants to stress that the change was deliberate.

～ようになる · Examples

やっと自転車(じてんしゃ)に乗(の)れるようになりました。
I can finally ride a bike.

最近(さいきん)、朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べるようになった。
Lately I've started eating breakfast.

練習(れんしゅう)して、平仮名(ひらがな)が全部(ぜんぶ)読(よ)めるようになりました。
With practice I've got to the point where I can read all the hiragana.

春(はる)になって、公園(こうえん)で遊(あそ)ぶ子(こ)どもをよく見(み)かけるようになった。
Now that it's spring, I see children playing in the park all the time.

この店(みせ)のドアは、人(ひと)が近(ちか)づくと自動(じどう)で開(あ)くようになっています。
The door of this shop is set up to open automatically when someone approaches.

～ようになる · Examples (cont.)

祖母(そぼ)はスマホでメールを送(おく)れるようになって、とても喜(よろこ)んでいます。
My grandmother has learnt to send messages on her phone and is delighted about it.

子(こ)どもが生(う)まれてから、夜(よる)は早(はや)く家(いえ)に帰(かえ)るようになりました。
Since our child was born I've taken to coming home early in the evenings.

日本(にほん)に来(き)たばかりのころは何(なに)も分(わ)かりませんでしたが、半年(はんとし)で買(か)い物(もの)ぐらいはできるようになりました。
When I'd just arrived in Japan I understood nothing, but within six months I could at least manage the shopping.

～ようになる · Nuance & register

Everything about this pattern depends on there being a before. The sentence implicitly says "it wasn't like this previously", so it is odd wherever no change is involved. This is why a one-off future event cannot use it: ❌明日(あした)、会議(かいぎ)に出(で)るようになります - attending one meeting is not a new state of affairs. That sentence wants ことになります.

The two everyday uses are:

  • New ability. Almost always with the potential form:
    運転(うんてん)できるようになった, 漢字(かんじ)が書(か)けるようになった. Learners under-use this and reach for the bare potential instead, which reports the ability without the achievement in it.
  • New habit. With a plain verb:
    最近(さいきん)、本(ほん)を読(よ)むようになりました. Nothing about the speaker's ability changed; their behaviour did.

～ようになる · Nuance & register (cont.)

ようになっている is a third, quieter use: a state that has come about, or a mechanism set up so that something happens. この機械(きかい)は三十分(さんじゅっぷん)で止(と)まるようになっています is how you describe the way a device behaves, and manuals are full of it. The close relative ようにできている puts the stress on the design rather than on the state.

Because the change is presented as something that happened to the subject, the pattern is modest by nature. Saying 話(はな)せるようになりました about your own Japanese sounds more graceful than 話(はな)せます, and it is the standard answer when someone compliments your progress. It is register-neutral otherwise; なる carries the politeness.

Note - 分(わ)かる, できる and ある already contain the sense of a state, and they take ようになる directly: 分(わ)かるようになりました. There is no ❌分(わ)かれるようになる.

～ようになる · ～ようになる vs ～なくなる

Rule of thumb - these are the positive and negative halves of one idea. Started doing it: ようになった. Stopped doing it: なくなった.

引(ひ)っ越(こ)してから、バスを使(つか)うようになりました。
Since moving I've started using the bus.

引(ひ)っ越(こ)してから、電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)らなくなりました。
Since moving I've stopped taking the train.

The asymmetry catches people: the positive needs ように, the negative does not, because ない behaves like an い-adjective and takes the く connector. The same split gives you 読(よ)めるようになった against 読(よ)めなくなった.

～ようになる · ～ようになる vs ～ようにする

Rule of thumb - なる is the change; する is the effort that produces it. Do not use する for something that happened to you.

毎日(まいにち)少(すこ)しずつ漢字(かんじ)を覚(おぼ)えるようにしました。
I made a point of learning a few kanji every day.

新聞(しんぶん)の見出(みだ)しが読(よ)めるようになりました。
I got to the point where I could read newspaper headlines.

The first sentence is the practice; the second is the result, and the two are often said one after the other. Where English says "I managed to learn to read", Japanese splits the effort from the outcome and marks which one it means.

～ようになる · ～ようになる vs ～くなる／～になる

Rule of thumb - ように is only for verbs. い-adjectives take く, and な-adjectives and nouns take に.

日(ひ)が長(なが)くなりました。
The days have got longer.

日本語(にほんご)で冗談(じょうだん)が言(い)えるようになりました。
I've reached the point where I can crack jokes in Japanese.

Watch the potential form here. 言(い)える is a verb, not an adjective, so it takes ように even though its る ending makes it look like a candidate for くなる. ❌言(い)えくなる is a fairly common slip.

～ようになる · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 泳(およ)ぐになりました → ✅ 泳(およ)ぐようになりました
    A verb needs ように to attach to なる. Only nouns join なる with a bare に.

  • ❌ 話(はな)せくなりました → ✅ 話(はな)せるようになりました
    The potential form is a verb. くなる is the い-adjective connector and does not apply.

  • ❌ たばこを吸(す)わないようになりました → ✅ たばこを吸(す)わなくなりました
    For a change in the negative direction, ない contracts with なる directly. The ようになる version is not impossible but sounds laboured.

  • ❌ 部屋(へや)が静(しず)かなようになりました。 → ✅ 部屋(へや)が静(しず)かになりました。
    な-adjectives take に. ように here would be the "seems like" よう, which gives a completely different sentence.

～ようになる · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 来月(らいげつ)から大阪(おおさか)に住(す)むようになります。 → ✅ 来月(らいげつ)から大阪(おおさか)に住(す)むことになりました。
    A single arranged move is a decision landing, not a gradual change. Use ことになる.

～ようになる · Practice 1

1. 毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)したので、ギターが少(すこ)し弾(ひ)ける____なりました。

弾(ひ)けるようになりました。

弾(ひ)ける is a potential verb, and verbs attach to なる through ように. The sentence reports a threshold crossed - you could not before, and now you can.

～ようになる · Practice 2

2. 大人(おとな)になってから、朝(あさ)のコーヒーを（飲(の)まないようになりました・飲(の)まなくなりました）。

飲(の)まなくなりました。

For a change away from doing something, ない joins なる directly as なくなる, because ない inflects like an い-adjective. The ないようになる version is grammatical but stilted, and no one would choose it here.

～ようになる · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "My daughter has started reading books on her own."

娘(むすめ)が一人(ひとり)で本(ほん)を読(よ)むようになりました。

This is a new habit rather than a new ability, so the plain dictionary form goes in front of ようになる. Using the potential 読(よ)めるようになりました would change the meaning to "she has learnt to read".

～ようになる · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 運動(うんどう)を始(はじ)めてから、体(からだ)の調子(ちょうし)がいいようになりました。

✅ 運動(うんどう)を始(はじ)めてから、体(からだ)の調子(ちょうし)がよくなりました。

いい is an い-adjective, so it changes with くなる - and irregularly, through the よ stem: いい → よくなる. ように only ever attaches to verbs.

～ようになる · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 日本語(にほんご)のニュースが分(わ)かります。 (b) 日本語(にほんご)のニュースが分(わ)かるようになりました。

(a) states a present ability flatly: you understand Japanese news. It says nothing about where you started.

(b) puts a history behind it - there was a time when you did not, and now you do. That makes it both more informative and more modest: the change is presented as something that happened, rather than as a skill you are asserting. When a Japanese speaker praises your progress, (b) is the graceful reply.
