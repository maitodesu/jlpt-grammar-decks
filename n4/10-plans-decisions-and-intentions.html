Volitional form - ～(よ)う

Meaning · let's do it; I'll do it - the form a verb wears when it carries a will

The volitional is the plain-form counterpart of ましょう. It does two jobs that English keeps apart: proposing something to the people you are with ("let's go"), and announcing a decision to yourself ("right - I'll go"). It is also the base that a whole family of N4 patterns is built on, so the table below is worth more than the page around it: ～(よ)うと思おもう, ～(よ)うとする and ～(よ)うか all need this form and nothing else will do.

Note - verbs come in three classes: う-verbs (五段ごだん / godan / Group 1), る-verbs (一段いちだん / ichidan / Group 2), and the two irregulars する and 来くる. This page says う-verb and る-verb throughout.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · Formation

う-verbs swap the final -u vowel for -o and add う; る-verbs drop る and add よう. That is the whole rule, and unlike the て-form there are no sound changes to memorise.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · Nuance & register

The plain volitional is casual. Used as a proposal it belongs with friends, family, classmates and people below you at work; said to a customer or a 部長ぶちょう it is startling. Upward, use ましょう - and even ましょう can presume too much, so ～ましょうか ("shall I / shall we?") and ～ませんか ("won't you?") are the genuinely safe openers.

Intonation splits the two main uses more reliably than grammar does. Flat or falling, 行いこう is a decision you are handing to the group. With か on the end, 行いこうか hands the decision back to them. Adding よ - 行いこうよ - pushes, and can sound like coaxing or whining depending on who is speaking.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two colloquial tails worth recognising:

  • ～(よ)うっと is a private mutter, not a proposal. Nobody is being invited:
    「あー、もう寝ねようっと。」 It sounds relaxed and slightly childlike, and it is common on social media.
  • ～(よ)うか迷まよっている ("I can't decide whether to…") is the standard
    way to say you are torn: 買かおうか迷まよっています。

In writing, the volitional turns up as the essayist's "let us": 次つぎに、この問題もんだいについて考かんがえよう。 That is a register away from the friendly spoken use, but it is the same form. The stiff literary ～であろう ("it may well be that…") is something to recognise in print and never to say out loud.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · Nuance & register (cont.)

ましょう has one job the plain form cannot do: it is polite. It cannot, however, be embedded - ～ましょうと思おもう is not Japanese. Whenever the volitional is followed by something, it goes in plain.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · ～(よ)う vs ～ましょう

Rule of thumb - same meaning, different politeness. ましょう is the polite sentence-final form; ～(よ)う is the plain one, and it is the only one that can have something attached after it.

みんなで**歌うたおう**。
Let's all sing.

みんなで**歌うたいましょう**。
Let's all sing. (polite / addressed to a group you are leading)

The moment another clause follows, politeness moves to the end of the sentence and the volitional reverts to plain:

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · ～(よ)う vs ～ましょう (cont.)

来週らいしゅうから**走はしろう**と思おもっています。
I'm thinking of taking up running from next week.

❌走はしりましょうと思おもっています is impossible. Politeness is marked once, at the end.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · ～(よ)う vs ～ませんか

Rule of thumb - ～(よ)う and ましょう assume the answer is yes. ～ませんか genuinely asks, so it is the polite way to invite someone who is free to refuse.

昼ひるごはん、いっしょに**食たべよう**。
Let's have lunch together. (we're already doing this)

昼ひるごはん、いっしょに**食たべませんか**。
Would you like to have lunch together? (a real invitation)

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · ～(よ)う vs ～ませんか (cont.)

Inviting someone for the first time, ませんか is almost always the better choice; ましょう after they have agreed is what settles the details. A useful ordering from most to least presumptuous: 行いこう → 行いきましょう → 行いきましょうか → 行いきませんか. Treat that as a tendency, not a rulebook - tone of voice moves any of them.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · ～(よ)う vs the plain non-past

Rule of thumb - English "will" is usually not the volitional. A plain future statement is just the dictionary form or ます; the volitional adds the feeling of a will being exercised at this moment.

明日あした、病院びょういんへ**行いきます**。
I'm going to the hospital tomorrow. (a fact about my schedule)

やっぱり明日あした、病院びょういんへ**行いこう**。
You know what - I'll go to the hospital tomorrow. (deciding as I speak)

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · ～(よ)う vs the plain non-past (cont.)

This is why the volitional cannot report the weather, someone else's plans, or anything that happens without a decision. Those all take the plain form, with でしょう or と思おもいます if you need to hedge.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 手伝てつだうよう → ✅ 手伝てつだおう
    A う-verb ending in う becomes おう, not うよう. The よう ending belongs to る-verbs only.

  • ❌ 食たべろう → ✅ 食たべよう
    食たべる is a る-verb: drop る, add よう. ろう would be the う-verb pattern, which is why 帰かえる - a う-verb despite its looks - really is 帰かえろう.

  • ❌ 来くる → きよう → ✅ 来くる → こよう
    The irregular shifts to the こ stem, the same one you already use for 来こない. Reading the written form 来こよう as kiyou is a very common slip.

  • ❌ 明日あしたは雪ゆきが降ふろう。 → ✅ 明日あしたは雪ゆきが降ふるでしょう。
    Snow has no intentions. The volitional needs a subject that can choose.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 課長かちょう、コーヒーを飲のもう。 → ✅ 課長かちょう、コーヒーでもいかがですか。
    Grammatically fine, socially wrong. Do not propose things to a superior in the plain volitional.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · Practice 1

1. 荷物にもつが重おもいね。タクシーで帰かえ____。

タクシーで帰かえろう。

帰かえる is a う-verb, so the final る becomes ろ and takes う. The よう ending would only be right if this were a る-verb.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · Practice 2

2. 部長ぶちょうに向むかって：「そろそろ（出でかけよう・出でかけましょう）。」

そろそろ**出でかけましょう**。

The plain volitional is for friends and juniors. Speaking to a 部長ぶちょう you need ましょう, and if you want to leave him room to say no, 出でかけましょうか is softer still.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese, casually: "It's cold - shall we go inside?"

寒さむいね。中なかに**入はいろうか**。

入はいる is a う-verb → 入はいろう, and the か turns a flat proposal into a check with the other person. Without か it would be "come on, inside" rather than "shall we?".

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 明日あした映画えいがを見みましょうと思おもっています。

✅ 明日あした映画えいがを**見みよう**と思おもっています。

ましょう cannot be embedded in a larger sentence. When something follows the volitional it must be plain, and the politeness is carried by 思おもっています at the end.

Volitional form - ～(よ)う · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 映画えいがを見みよう。 (b) 映画えいがを見みようか。

(a) is a decision presented as already taken - you are telling the group what is happening, in a friendly way. It also works as a resolution said to yourself.

(b) hands the choice back: "shall we watch a film?" The か makes it a genuine question, so the other person can say no without pushing back against you. In a group where you are not the one who decides, (b) is the safer sentence.

～(よ)うと思う

Meaning · I think I'll ～; I'm thinking of ～ing - an intention, presented as a thought rather than a fact

～(よ)うと思おもう takes the volitional - the "let's / I'll" form - and wraps it in "I think". The result is the most natural way in Japanese to answer what are you going to do? It is softer than つもり because it frames the plan as something going on in your head right now, which leaves the door open. Note the shape: what you are thinking is 行いこう, "I'll go"; と quotes that thought.

～(よ)うと思う · Formation

The verb before と is the plain volitional, always. ましょう cannot be quoted here.

～(よ)うと思う · Nuance & register

The whole point of this pattern is softness. つもり states a settled plan; ～(よ)うと思おもいます reports a thought, and a thought can change without anyone losing face. That makes it the default answer to a superior, a customer, or anyone whose reaction you have not heard yet.

The ています layer does most of the fine-tuning:

～(よ)うと思う · Nuance & register (cont.)

  • ～(よ)うと思おもいます - the decision is being taken as you
    speak    . It fits a reply on the spot: 「じゃあ、そうしようと思おもいます。」
  • ～(よ)うと思おもっています - you have been holding this intention
    for a while. It sounds more considered and, in practice, closer to a real plan. In a job interview or a meeting this is the form you want.

The same ています is what lets you talk about other people. Exactly as with 思おもう generally, a bare ～(よ)うと思おもう claims the thought is yours; someone else's intention needs 思おもっている, or evidence marking （思おもっているようです, 思おもっていると言いっていました）.

～(よ)うと思う · Nuance & register (cont.)

One structural point that catches people: the person doing the volitional verb must be the same person doing the thinking. You can say 私わたしが手伝てつだおうと思おもいます ("I'll help"), but you cannot put someone else in there - ❌彼かれが来こようと思おもいます is impossible. To talk about what you want others to do, you need a different pattern altogether.

Register is otherwise neutral. Plain 行いこうと思おもう is everyday casual speech; in stiffer written Japanese you will meet ～(よ)うと考かんがえております and ～たいと存ぞんじます, which do the same softening job in keigo. Adding か - ～(よ)うかと思おもっています - softens further, to roughly "I've half a mind to…", and is very useful when you genuinely have not decided.

～(よ)うと思う · ～(よ)うと思う vs ～つもりだ

Rule of thumb - ～(よ)うと思おもう is a decision being made or reported now; つもり is a decision already settled. Neither one is English "will".

来年らいねん、一人ひとりで住すもうと思おもっています。
I'm thinking of living on my own next year.

来年らいねん、一人ひとりで**住すむつもりです**。
I intend to live on my own next year.

～(よ)うと思う · ～(よ)うと思う vs ～つもりだ (cont.)

The first invites a response - the listener could still say "are you sure?" and not be contradicting you. The second reports a fixed position, and pushing back against it means arguing. This is why ～(よ)うと思おもいます is the safer thing to say when your plan touches someone else's, and why つもり is the right choice when you want to be believed.

There is also a hard difference: つもり can be denied （行いくつもりはありません）and questioned about others （どうするつもりですか, which sounds like an interrogation). The 思おもう pattern has no equivalent edge to it.

～(よ)うと思う · ～(よ)うと思う vs ～と思う

Rule of thumb - volitional + と思おもう = my intention. Plain form + と思おもう = my opinion or prediction about how things will go.

私わたしも**行いこうと思おもいます**。
I think I'll go too. (I'm deciding to go)

田中たなかさんも**行いくと思おもいます**。
I think Tanaka will go too. (my guess about him)

～(よ)うと思う · ～(よ)うと思う vs ～と思う (cont.)

Get this pair wrong and the sentence does not become ungrammatical - it becomes a different statement, which is worse. Saying 私わたしも行いくと思おもいます about yourself sounds oddly detached, as though you were predicting your own behaviour from the outside. It is occasionally said, but it is not how you volunteer.

Because the plain form is for prediction, it is also what you use for events nobody decides: 明日あしたは晴はれると思おもいます.

～(よ)うと思う · ～(よ)うと思います vs ～(よ)うと思っています

Rule of thumb - 思おもいます is the thought arriving now; 思おもっています is a thought you have been carrying.

それはいい案あんですね。じゃあ、そうしようと思おもいます。
That's a good idea. All right, I'll do that.

前まえから会社かいしゃを**辞やめようと思おもっていました**。
I'd been thinking about leaving the company for a while.

～(よ)うと思う · ～(よ)うと思います vs ～(よ)うと思っています (cont.)

In the first, the decision is a response to what was just said, so 思おもいます is right and 思おもっています would sound like you had planned it in advance. In the second, the ていました is doing real work: it says the intention predates this conversation.

～(よ)うと思う · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 行いきましょうと思おもいます。 → ✅ 行いこうと思おもいます。
    Only the plain volitional can be quoted with と. The politeness sits on 思おもいます.

  • ❌ 田中たなかさんは会社かいしゃを辞やめようと思おもいます。 → ✅ …辞やめようと思おもっています。
    A bare 思おもう asserts your own mind. For a third person use 思おもっている, or hedge with 思おもっているようです.

  • ❌ 明日あしたは雨あめが降ふろうと思おもいます。 → ✅ 明日あしたは雨あめが降ふると思おもいます。
    Weather has no volition, so it cannot take the volitional. Plain form + と思おもう is prediction.

  • ❌ 買かわないようと思おもいます。 → ✅ 買かうのはやめようと思おもいます。
    There is no everyday negative volitional. Negate the plan, not the verb.

～(よ)うと思う · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 彼かれに手伝てつだおうと思おもいます。 → ✅ 彼かれに手伝てつだってもらおうと思おもいます。
    The volitional describes what you will do. If someone else does the acting, the sentence has to be rebuilt around that.

～(よ)うと思う · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 弟おとうとは大学だいがくで医学いがくを勉強べんきょうしようと思おもいます。

～(よ)うと思う · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 明日あした、銀行ぎんこうへ行いこうと思おもいます。 (b) 明日あした、銀行ぎんこうへ行いくと思おもいます。

(a) is your own intention: the volitional says the going is your decision, and と思おもいます presents it gently. This is what you say when someone asks about your plans.

(b) predicts. With the plain form, と思おもいます gives an opinion about what will happen - most naturally about somebody else, and about yourself it sounds like you are guessing at your own future rather than choosing it. Learners produce (b) by translating "I think I'll go", which is exactly the wrong route.

～(よ)うとする

Meaning · to try to do something; to be on the point of doing it

～(よ)うとする takes the volitional - the will to do something - and puts it into a verb. It catches the moment before the action succeeds: the arm reaching for the handle, the sun touching the horizon. That is why the pattern so often ends in a "but": you tried, and something got in the way.

Rule of thumb - する is the ordinary verb here, so it conjugates normally. Everything before と is frozen in the plain volitional: 開あけようとしました, never ❌開あけましょうとした.

赤あかちゃんが**立たとうとしている**。
The baby is trying to stand up.

弟おとうとは野菜やさいを**食たべようとしない**。
My little brother won't even try to eat vegetables.

ドアを**開あけようとした**ら、かぎがかかっていました。
I went to open the door and found it locked.

日ひが**沈しずもうとしている**。
The sun is about to set.

～(よ)うとする · Examples (cont.)

電車でんしゃに**乗のろうとした**とき、後うしろから名前なまえを呼よばれた。
Just as I was getting on the train, someone called my name from behind.

何度なんど説明せつめいしても、彼かれは話はなしを**聞きこうとしなかった**。
No matter how many times I explained, he refused to listen.

荷物にもつを棚たなに**上あげようとしました**が、重おもすぎて一人ひとりでは無理むりでした。
I tried to lift my bag onto the rack, but it was too heavy to manage on my own.

会議かいぎが**始はじまろうとしていた**ので、急いそいで席せきに着つきました。
The meeting was on the point of starting, so I hurried to my seat.

～(よ)うとする · Nuance & register

Three uses live under one form, and they behave differently.

1. The interrupted attempt. In the past tense, ～(よ)うとした almost always implies that the action did not come off, or that something cut across it. If you tried and succeeded, Japanese just reports the success: 窓まどを開あけました. This is a real difference from English, where "I tried to open the window" can be modest understatement for "I opened it". Here it means the window did not open.

～(よ)うとする · Nuance & register (cont.)

2. On the verge. ～(よ)うとしている and ～(よ)うとしたとき mark the instant before something happens, and this is the one use that works with subjects that have no will at all: 台風たいふうが上陸じょうりくしようとしている, 一年いちねんが終おわろうとしている. It has a slightly heightened, narrative feel - common in news writing and novels, less so in casual chat, where もうすぐ or ところ does the job.

3. Refusal. ～(よ)うとしない says someone is unwilling, not merely that they are not doing it. It carries a note of complaint, so it is comfortable about children, animals and people you are annoyed with, and risky about a superior: 部長ぶちょうは決きめようとしません is close to saying the boss is being obstructive.

～(よ)うとする · Nuance & register (cont.)

A restriction worth internalising: for your own ongoing effort, the plain non-past ～(よ)うとする is unnatural. ❌毎日まいにち運動うんどうしようとしています should be 運動うんどうするようにしています. Reserve ～(よ)うとする for a single moment of trying, not a standing policy. The plain non-past does appear in general statements about people - 人ひとはだれでも楽らくをしようとする - where it is descriptive rather than personal.

Register is neutral. する carries the politeness, so 開あけようとしました is as polite as its ending, and nothing about the pattern is formal or slangy in itself.

～(よ)うとする · ～(よ)うとする vs ～てみる

Rule of thumb - ～(よ)うとする is the attempt, which may never get off the ground. ～てみる is doing it to find out what happens - the action really takes place.

This pair is the classic confusion, because English uses "try" for both.

窓まどを**開あけようとした**が、固かたくて動うごかなかった。
I tried to open the window, but it was stiff and wouldn't budge.

窓まどを**開あけてみた**ら、涼すずしい風かぜが入はいってきた。
I opened the window to see, and a cool breeze came in.

～(よ)うとする · ～(よ)うとする vs ～てみる (cont.)

In the first, the window stayed shut. In the second it opened - the "trying" was about the result being unknown, not about whether the act was possible. So 納豆なっとうを食たべてみました means you ate some and now you know what it tastes like, while 納豆なっとうを食たべようとしました means you got as far as raising it to your mouth.

Note - てみる cannot express failure, and ～(よ)うとする cannot express curiosity. Neither can substitute for the other, and the two can be stacked when both ideas are present: 食たべてみようとしました - "I made an attempt at tasting it."

～(よ)うとする · ～(よ)うとしている vs ～ところだ

Rule of thumb - both mean "about to". ところだ is the everyday, neutral one and needs a subject with a plan. ～(よ)うとしている is more vivid, and it works for events nobody chose.

今いま、出でかけるところです。
I'm just about to head out.

電車でんしゃが**出発しゅっぱつしようとしている**。
The train is on the point of departing.

Swap them and both sentences get strange. 出でかけようとしています about yourself sounds like you are narrating your own life; a train has no ところ of its own to be at.

～(よ)うとする · ～(よ)うとしない vs the plain negative

Rule of thumb - ～ない reports that something is not happening. ～(よ)うとしない says the person is refusing to even try.

子こどもが薬くすりを**飲のまない**。
My child isn't taking the medicine.

子こどもが薬くすりを**飲のもうとしない**。
My child won't take the medicine. (clamps his mouth shut every time)

The second is a complaint. Because it attributes stubbornness, it is common in speech about kids and pets and best avoided about people you are supposed to defer to.

～(よ)うとする · Common mistakes

  • ❌ ドアを開あけるとした → ✅ ドアを開あけようとした
    とする attaches to the volitional, not the dictionary form. The dictionary form with とする is a different, much more advanced pattern meaning "supposing that".

  • ❌ 毎日まいにち漢字かんじを覚おぼえようとしています。 → ✅ 毎日まいにち漢字かんじを覚おぼえるようにしています。
    A standing effort of your own is ～ようにする. ～(よ)うとする is one moment of trying, not a habit.

  • ❌ ケーキを作つくろうとしました。おいしかったです。 → ✅ ケーキを作つくってみました。おいしかったです。
    If the cake got made and eaten, the attempt succeeded, so ～(よ)うとした is the wrong verb. Use てみる, or simply 作つくりました.

～(よ)うとする · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 先生せんせいはまだ来こようとしません。 → ✅ 先生せんせいはまだ来きていません。
    ～(よ)うとしない accuses the teacher of refusing to come. For a plain statement that someone has not arrived, use ている with a negative.

～(よ)うとする · Practice 1

1. バスに乗の____とした時とき、さいふがないことに気きがついた。

バスに乗のろうとした時とき…

乗のる is a う-verb, so the volitional is 乗のろう. ～(よ)うとしたとき pins the sentence to the instant before boarding - which is exactly when you would notice the wallet was gone.

～(よ)うとする · Practice 2

2. 新あたらしいカレーの店みせへ行いって、（食たべようとしました・食たべてみました）。とてもおいしかったです。

食たべてみました。

The food was eaten and judged delicious, so the action succeeded. てみる is "did it to find out what it was like". 食たべようとしました would say you never got it down.

～(よ)うとする · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "I tried to phone her, but the line was busy."

彼女かのじょに**電話でんわしようとした**が、話はなし中ちゅうでした。

The call never connected, so this is an attempt cut short - ～(よ)うとした, not てみた. Note that する verbs give しよう: 電話でんわする → 電話でんわしよう.

～(よ)うとする · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 健康けんこうのために、毎朝まいあさ野菜やさいジュースを飲のもうとしています。

✅ 健康けんこうのために、毎朝まいあさ野菜やさいジュースを**飲のむようにしています**。

毎朝まいあさ makes this a habit you are maintaining, and habits of effort take ～ようにしている. ～(よ)うとしている would describe a single moment - you, right now, with the glass halfway to your lips.

～(よ)うとする · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 彼かれは質問しつもんに答こたえなかった。 (b) 彼かれは質問しつもんに答こたえようとしなかった。

(a) simply reports that no answer came. He might have been asleep, absent, or unable to hear the question; the sentence takes no position on why.

(b) puts his will in the frame: he was asked, he understood, and he would not make the attempt. It reads as criticism, and a listener will hear the speaker's irritation in it. That extra layer is the whole point of ～(よ)うとしない.

～予定だ

Meaning · to be scheduled to; to be due to - the plan is on a calendar, not just in your head

予定よてい is an ordinary noun meaning schedule or arrangement, so 行いく予定よていです says literally "the arrangement is that I go". That is why it feels so different from つもり, which reports what you have decided: 予定よてい points at something outside you - a date, a booking, a timetable other people can also see.

～予定だ · Formation

The verb in front stays plain, exactly as it does before つもり.

Rule of thumb - ❌行いきます予定よていです is the same error as ❌行いきますつもりです. A noun is being modified, and a noun-modifying clause is always plain.

～予定だ · Examples

会議かいぎは三時さんじに**終おわる予定よていです**。
The meeting is scheduled to finish at three.

来週らいしゅう、大阪おおさかへ**出張しゅっちょうする予定よていです**。
I'm due to travel to Osaka on business next week.

明日あしたの午後ごごは健康けんこう診断しんだんの予定よていです。
Tomorrow afternoon is down for a health check.

今月こんげつは忙いそがしいので、旅行りょこうに**行いく予定よていはありません**。
I'm busy this month, so I have no trips scheduled.

～予定だ · Examples (cont.)

飛行機ひこうきは十時じゅうじに**着つく予定よていでした**が、一時間いちじかん遅おくれました。
The plane was due in at ten, but it was an hour late.

部長ぶちょう、来週らいしゅうの**ご予定よてい**を教おしえていただけますか。
Could you let me know your schedule for next week, sir?

駅前えきまえの工事こうじは来年らいねんの春はるに**終おわる予定よていだった**が、雨あめが多おおくて遅おくれている。
The work in front of the station was supposed to finish next spring, but there's been a lot of rain and it's behind.

申もうし訳わけありません、新あたらしい商品しょうひんは来月らいげつに**入はいる予定よていになっております**。
I'm sorry - the new stock is scheduled to arrive next month.

～予定だ · Nuance & register

予定よてい is impersonal by design, and that is its whole value. It reports an arrangement without saying whose will produced it, so it can describe a plane, a shop's opening hours, a company's project or your own Tuesday with the same words. つもり, by contrast, can only ever describe a person's intention.

～予定だ · Nuance & register (cont.)

That neutrality makes it the polite register for other people's plans. Asking a superior ご予定よていはいかがでしょうか is safe; asking about their つもり sounds like an interrogation. In business Japanese the noun turns up constantly on its own - 予定よていを立たてる (draw up a schedule), 予定よていを変更へんこうする (reschedule), 予定よていどおり (as planned) - and ～予定よていとなっております is the standard humble phrasing for telling a customer when something will happen.

Two limits to keep straight:

～予定だ · Nuance & register (cont.)

  • An arrangement is not a forecast. Something has to have been arranged by
    someone. ❌明日あしたは雨あめが降ふる予定よていです is wrong; weather takes 予報よほう, でしょう or そうです. A train arriving at 10:04 is fine, because a timetable is an arrangement.
  • 予定よていでした implies it did not go that way. Like
    つもりだった, the past tense sets up a "but". If the plan was kept, say 予定よていどおり終おわりました.

Because 予定よてい keeps you out of the sentence, it can also be used to duck. Answering 参加さんかする予定よていです instead of 参加さんかします quietly leaves room for the schedule to change - useful, and occasionally heard as non-committal.

～予定だ · ～予定だ vs ～つもりだ

Rule of thumb - 予定よてい is what is booked; つもり is what you have decided. If nothing exists outside your own head, it is not a 予定よてい.

来月らいげつ、京都きょうとで友ともだちに**会あう予定よていです**。
I'm meeting a friend in Kyoto next month. (arranged, date agreed)

来月らいげつ、京都きょうとで友ともだちに**会あうつもりです**。
I mean to see a friend in Kyoto next month. (my plan; nothing fixed yet)

The corollary is that 予定よてい does not carry determination. Pairing it with a word like 絶対ぜったいに or 今年ことしこそ is odd, because those express resolve and a schedule has none. Resolve is つもり territory.

～予定だ · ～予定だ vs ～(よ)うと思っている

Rule of thumb - three degrees of firmness: ～(よ)うと思おもっています (a thought) → つもりです (a decision) → 予定よていです (an entry in the diary).

夏休なつやすみは北海道ほっかいどうへ**行いこうと思おもっています**。
I'm thinking of going to Hokkaido in the summer holidays.

夏休なつやすみは北海道ほっかいどうへ**行いく予定よていです**。
I'm going to Hokkaido in the summer holidays. (tickets are booked)

Answering a colleague's casual question with the second when nothing is booked overstates your case; answering a travel agent with the first understates it. Match the form to how real the plan is.

～予定だ · ～予定だ vs ～ことになっている

Rule of thumb - 予定よてい is a dated item: this happens, then. ことになっている is a standing arrangement or rule: this is how it is done.

来週らいしゅうの月曜日げつようびに試験しけんを**受うける予定よていです**。
I'm scheduled to sit the exam next Monday.

この部屋へやでは靴くつを**脱ぬぐことになっています**。
You're supposed to take your shoes off in this room.

Both hide the person who decided, which is why they feel related. 予定よてい attaches to a point in time and can be missed; ことになっている attaches to a practice and can be broken.

～予定だ · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 来週らいしゅう、帰かえります予定よていです。 → ✅ 来週らいしゅう、帰かえる予定よていです。
    予定よてい is a noun, so the clause in front of it is plain. The politeness goes once, on です.

  • ❌ 明日あしたは雪ゆきが降ふる予定よていです。 → ✅ 明日あしたは雪ゆきが降ふるそうです。
    Nobody schedules snow. 予定よてい needs an arrangement behind it; a forecast is 予報よほう.

  • ❌ 来月らいげつは引ひっ越こし予定よていです。 → ✅ 来月らいげつは引ひっ越こしの予定よていです。
    Two nouns are joined with の. This is the same rule that gives 冗談じょうだんのつもり.

  • ❌ 今年ことしこそ、たばこをやめる予定よていです。 → ✅ 今年ことしこそ、たばこをやめるつもりです。
    今年ことしこそ is a declaration of will, and a schedule has no will. Determination belongs with つもり.

～予定だ · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 先週せんしゅうの会議かいぎに出でる予定よていでした。（and you did go） → ✅ 先週せんしゅうの会議かいぎには予定よていどおり出でました。
    The past 予定よていでした almost always sets up a "but" - it says the plan was not kept. If it was, 予定よていどおり is the phrase you want.

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 来年らいねん、留学りゅうがくする予定よていです。 (b) 来年らいねん、留学りゅうがくするつもりです。

～ことにする

Meaning · to decide to do something - and the person deciding is the speaker

N5 gave you にする for choosing a noun: コーヒーにします, "I'll have the coffee." A verb cannot sit in that slot, so こと is added to turn the whole action into a thing that can be chosen. 歩あるくことにします is "I'll go with walking." The pattern always carries an agent, and unless the sentence says otherwise, that agent is you.

Attaches to Form Example

Rule of thumb - こと exists to nominalise. ❌行いくにする is impossible and ❌行いきますことにする is impossible; the verb goes in plain form and こと does the rest.

～ことにする · Examples

今年ことしの夏なつは山やまに**登のぼることにしました**。
We've decided to go climbing this summer.

しばらくゲームをしないことにした。
I've decided to lay off games for a while.

土曜日どようびはゆっくり**休やすむことにします**。
I'm going to take Saturday off and rest properly.

寝ねる前まえに日記にっきを**書かくことにしています**。
I make a point of writing in my diary before bed.

～ことにする · Examples (cont.)

今日きょうのことは**見みなかったことにします**。
I'll pretend I didn't see anything today.

よく考かんがえて、大学院だいがくいんには**行いかないことにしました**。
After thinking it over carefully, I've decided against graduate school.

駅えきまで歩あるくと三十分さんじゅっぷんかかるので、自転車じてんしゃで**行いくことにした**。
It takes thirty minutes to walk to the station, so I decided to cycle.

家族かぞくと話はなし合あって、来年らいねんの春はるに**引ひっ越こすことにしました**。
We talked it over as a family and decided to move next spring.

～ことにする · Nuance & register

The tense of する is doing more work than learners expect.

  • ことにしました - the decision is already made, and you are
    reporting it. This is by far the commonest form, and it is what English "I've decided to…" corresponds to.
  • ことにします - you are deciding at this moment, often out loud,
    in response to what has just been said: 「じゃあ、そうすることにします。」
  • ことにしている - not a single decision but a policy you keep to.
    肉にくは食たべないことにしています is "I don't eat meat" said as a rule you have adopted, which is subtly different from 食たべません (I don't) or 食たべられません (I can't).

～ことにする · Nuance & register (cont.)

～たことにする and ～なかったことにする mean treat it as though. They are enormously useful and slightly conspiratorial: 今いまのは聞きかなかったことにしてください ("please forget I said that"), この話はなしはなかったことにしましょう ("let's call the whole thing off"). Nothing about this is rude, but it does presuppose that both parties are willing to bend the record a little.

～ことにする · Nuance & register (cont.)

One sociolinguistic point worth knowing. In Japanese workplaces, people frequently report their own decisions with ことになりました - the became version - even when they made the choice themselves: 来月らいげつ、退職たいしょくすることになりました. Saying ことにしました there is not wrong, but it puts your own will in the foreground, and the tendency is to avoid that when announcing something that affects other people. Treat this as a tendency, not a rule: among friends ことにした is completely normal.

The pattern itself is register-neutral, and する carries the politeness.

～ことにする · ～ことにする vs ～ことになる

Rule of thumb - who decided? ことにする: I did. ことになる: someone or something else did, or it simply worked out that way.

会社かいしゃを**辞やめることにしました**。
I've decided to leave the company.

来月らいげつから大阪おおさかで**働はたらくことになりました**。
It's been arranged that I'll be working in Osaka from next month.

The first is a resignation you chose. The second is a transfer that landed on you - or that you did engineer, but are presenting as an outcome. This is the single distinction that runs through both patterns, and it is the one exam questions test.

～ことにする · ～ことにする vs ～にする

Rule of thumb - にする chooses a noun; ことにする chooses an action. こと is the adapter between them.

昼ひるごはんはそばにします。
I'll have soba for lunch.

昼ひるごはんは外そとで**食たべることにします**。
I'll eat out for lunch.

Both are decisions and both use the same verb. What changes is only whether the thing being decided on is a noun or a whole verb phrase.

～ことにする · ～ことにしている vs ～ようにしている

Rule of thumb - ことにしている is a rule you follow; ようにしている is an effort you make. The first is kept or broken; the second is only ever attempted.

エレベーターは**使つかわないことにしています**。
I make it a rule not to use the lift.

なるべく階段かいだんを**使つかうようにしています**。
I try to use the stairs where I can.

～ことにする · ～ことにしている vs ～ようにしている (cont.)

The first sounds absolute - you have decided, and that is that. The second admits the days you take the lift anyway. Adverbs give it away: なるべく, できるだけ and 少すこしずつ sit naturally with ようにしている and awkwardly with ことにしている.

～ことにする · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 毎日まいにち歩あるくにする。 → ✅ 毎日まいにち歩あるくことにする。
    A verb cannot attach to にする on its own. こと turns it into a noun first.

  • ❌ 行いきますことにしました。 → ✅ 行いくことにしました。
    こと is a noun being modified, so the clause in front of it is plain. The politeness is on しました.

  • ❌ 会議かいぎは三時さんじに始はじまることにしました。 → ✅ 会議かいぎは三時さんじに始はじめることにしました。
    始はじまる is the intransitive verb - things starting by themselves - and it cannot be the object of a decision. If you decided, use the transitive 始はじめる; if the schedule decided, use 始はじまることになりました.

～ことにする · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 来週らいしゅうから東京とうきょうへ転勤てんきんすることにしました。（the company moved you） → ✅ …転勤てんきんすることになりました。
    If the choice was not yours, ことにする claims credit you do not have and sounds odd to a listener who knows how transfers work.

  • ❌ 明日あした晴はれることにします。 → ✅ 明日あしたは晴はれるでしょう。
    You can only decide things you control. Weather, arriving trains and other people's feelings are outside the pattern.

The choice is the speaker's own, so ことにする is the right pattern, and the past しました reports a decision already taken. Note that the negative goes on the main verb, not on する.

The speaker is an employee reporting a decision the president made. Since the decision was not theirs, ことになる is the honest choice. ことにしました would claim the decision as their own.

～ことにする · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 朝あさごはんを食たべることにしました。 (b) 朝あさごはんを食たべることにしています。

(a) is a decision taken at one point in the past: at some moment you resolved to start eating breakfast. It says nothing about whether you have kept it up.

(b) describes an ongoing practice governed by that decision - "I make a point of eating breakfast." The ている turns the single act of deciding into a policy that is still in force. If someone asks about your daily routine, (b) is the answer; (a) is what you say the day you make the change.

～ことになる

Meaning · it has been decided that ～; it works out that ～ - an outcome, with nobody named as having decided it

ことになる is the mirror image of ことにする. なる is the verb of things happening by themselves, so the sentence reports a decision or an arrangement as a result: something has been settled, and the sentence declines to say by whom. English almost always has to supply an agent ("they decided", "I've been transferred"); Japanese does not, and that gap is what makes this pattern feel alien at first.

Rule of thumb - ことになる takes a verb clause. A change of state in an adjective or noun uses plain なる: 寒さむくなる, 先生せんせいになる - never ❌寒さむいことになる.

～ことになる · Examples

～ことになる · Examples (cont.)

Come March next year, I'll have been at this company ten years.

つまり、最初さいしょから全部ぜんぶやり直なおすということになりますね。
So in other words, we start the whole thing over from scratch.

息子むすこの学校がっこうが遠とおいので、毎朝まいあさ駅えきまで**送おくっていくことになりました**。
My son's school is a long way off, so it's ended up with me driving him to the station every morning.

ご注文ちゅうもんの品しなは、来週らいしゅうの水曜日すいようびにお届とどけすることになっております。
Your order is scheduled for delivery next Wednesday.

～ことになる · Nuance & register

Three things live inside this one form.

1. A decision made elsewhere. The everyday use. 来月らいげつ、支店してんに移うつることになりました - the company decided, and you are passing on the result. Because no agent is named, the sentence works equally well when the decider is a committee, a rule, a circumstance, or nobody at all.

～ことになる · Nuance & register (cont.)

2. A rule in force. ことになっている is how Japanese states regulations, customs and standing arrangements: 会社かいしゃでは九時くじまでに来くることになっています, ここでは写真しゃしんを撮とらないことになっています. The ている is essential - it says the decision is not news but the state of things. This is also the polite way to refuse: rather than "you can't", you say "it isn't done", and nobody has to be the one saying no.

3. Logical consequence. ということになる means that amounts to. It is how you sum up: では、来週らいしゅうは休やすみということになりますね. Here nothing has been decided at all; the sentence is doing arithmetic.

～ことになる · Nuance & register (cont.)

The social point is the interesting one. Japanese speakers very often report their own decisions with ことになりました: 来月らいげつ退職たいしょくすることになりました, said by someone who resigned of their own accord. This is not deception. Putting the outcome forward instead of your will is a way of not imposing, and it is close to standard for announcements - marriages, job changes, moves - that affect the people listening. The tendency is strong in workplaces and weaker among close friends, where ことにした is perfectly normal. Read ことになりました as "here is what has been settled", and do not assume the speaker was passive.

Register is neutral; ことになっております is the humble business version and belongs in customer-facing speech.

～ことになる · ～ことになる vs ～ことにする

Rule of thumb - ことにする names the decider (you). ことになる reports the outcome and leaves the decider out. That is the whole distinction.

部屋へやを変かえてもらうことにしました。
I decided to ask them to change my room.

部屋へやを変かえてもらうことになりました。
It's been arranged that they'll change my room for me.

Same event, two framings. The first foregrounds your initiative; the second foregrounds the arrangement. Neither is more truthful - Japanese simply offers a choice about how much of your own will to put on the table, and takes the modest option more often than English does.

～ことになる · ～ことになった vs ～ことになっている

Rule of thumb - ことになった is the moment something was settled; ことになっている is the state of its being settled. News versus rule.

来月らいげつから制服せいふくを**着きることになりました**。
From next month we'll be wearing uniforms. (this has just been decided)

この学校がっこうでは制服せいふくを**着きることになっています**。
At this school you wear a uniform. (that's how it is)

Use the past form to announce a change, and the ている form to explain the way things are. Saying ことになりました about a rule that has been in place for decades makes it sound like this morning's memo.

～ことになる · ～ことになる vs ～ようになる

Rule of thumb - ことになる is a decision landing; ようになる is a change arriving - a new ability, or a habit that grew.

来年らいねん、海外かいがいで**働はたらくことになりました**。
It's been settled that I'll work abroad next year.

一年いちねん習ならって、英語えいごで**話はなせるようになりました**。
After a year of lessons, I've got to the point where I can hold a conversation in English.

The first happened at a stroke, because someone decided. The second happened gradually, because you changed. If you can point to a meeting where it was agreed, it is ことになる.

～ことになる · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 行いきますことになりました。 → ✅ 行いくことになりました。
    こと is a noun being modified, so the verb before it is plain.

  • ❌ この会社かいしゃでは制服せいふくを着きることになりました。（describing a long-standing rule） → ✅ …着きることになっています。
    A rule already in force needs the ている form. The past tense announces a change that has just been made.

  • ❌ 今晩こんばんはカレーを作つくることになります。（your own choice） → ✅ 今晩こんばんはカレーを作つくることにします。
    If you are the one deciding and there is no arrangement to report, ことにする is the honest form. ことになる here sounds as though the curry were imposed on you.

～ことになる · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 来週らいしゅうから忙いそがしいことになります。 → ✅ 来週らいしゅうから忙いそがしくなります。
    ことになる attaches to verbs. Adjectives change with plain なる.

  • ❌ 試験しけんは中止ちゅうしことになりました。 → ✅ 試験しけんは中止ちゅうしということになりました。
    A noun needs という before こと. Without it there is nothing joining the two nouns.

The company's circumstances made the decision, so the outcome is reported with ことになる and the agent is left unnamed. ことにしました would claim the move was your idea.

～ことになる · Practice 4

休やすみ is a noun, and a noun cannot modify こと directly. という is the connector. Alternatively, rebuild it around a verb: 休やすむことになりました.

～ことになる · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 来月らいげつ、引ひっ越こすことにしました。 (b) 来月らいげつ、引ひっ越こすことになりました。

(a) says the move is your decision, taken by you, and it puts your will in the foreground. It is what you say to a friend who asked what you had decided.

(b) reports the move as something that has been settled. It might mean your company transferred you - or it might mean exactly the same situation as (a), announced modestly. Japanese speakers frequently choose (b) for news that affects other people, because it does not push the speaker's own choices at the listener. Without further context, (b) does not actually tell you who decided; that is the point of it.

～ようにする

Meaning · to make an effort to do something; to see to it that something is the case

The よう here is the noun meaning manner or way, so 歩あるくようにする is literally "act in such a way that walking happens." That gives the pattern its two faces: an ongoing effort you make about your own behaviour, and an arrangement you put in place so that some state holds. Both are about steering, not about a single act - which is exactly what separates it from ことにする.

～ようにする · Formation

Attaches to Form Example
Verb dictionary form + ようにする 歩あるく → 歩あるくようにする
Verb (negative) ない-form + ようにする 遅おくれない → 遅おくれないようにする
Verb (potential) potential + ようにする 読よめる → 読よめるようにする
Effort you keep up ようにしている 歩あるくようにしています

Request / instruction ようにしてください 遅おくれないようにしてください
Arrangement made ようにした 見みえるようにした

～ようにする · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - the negative goes on the main verb, never on する. 食たべないようにする = "try not to eat". ❌食たべるようにしない would mean something like "not make the effort", which is not what anyone wants to say.

～ようにする · Examples

毎晩まいばん十二時じゅうにじまでに**寝ねるようにしています**。
I try to be in bed by midnight every night.

授業じゅぎょうに**遅おくれないようにします**。
I'll make sure not to be late for class.

塩しおを**使つかいすぎないようにしています**。
I'm trying not to use too much salt.

パスワードは人ひとに**見みられないようにしてください**。
Please make sure nobody can see your password.

～ようにする · Examples (cont.)

明日あしたは八時はちじまでに**来くるようにしてください**。
Please see to it that you're here by eight tomorrow.

留学生りゅうがくせいと話はなすときは、やさしい言葉ことばを**使つかうようにしています**。
When I talk with international students I make a point of using simple words.

夜よる遅おそくコーヒーを**飲のまないようにした**ら、よく眠ねむれるようになりました。
Once I made a point of not drinking coffee late at night, I started sleeping well.

父ちちは医者いしゃに注意ちゅういされてから、毎朝まいあさ三十分さんじゅっぷん歩あるくようにしているそうです。
Apparently my father has been making a point of walking for thirty minutes every morning since the doctor had a word with him.

～ようにする · Nuance & register

The commonest form by far is ようにしている. It says: this is something I aim at, and I mostly manage it. Crucially, it does not claim success every time - which is why it is the honest way to describe a resolution you keep about eighty percent of. It sits naturally with hedging adverbs: なるべく, できるだけ, 少すこしずつ, 毎日まいにち. Compare the flat present tense 毎日まいにち歩あるきます, which simply asserts that you do.

ようにします - non-past, no ている - is a promise to start. 今度こんどから気きをつけるようにします is the standard thing to say after being corrected: it accepts the point and commits to changing without claiming you have already changed.

～ようにする · Nuance & register (cont.)

ようにしてください is worth its own paragraph. Beside plain てください it is less immediate and more supervisory. てください asks for an action now; ようにしてください asks you to arrange your behaviour so that something is generally true. That makes it the natural form for rules, instructions and reminders - and it can carry a faint note of "I shouldn't have to say this", so among friends it is heavier than it looks. A teacher, a doctor or a manual will use it constantly.

The second flavour of the pattern - arranging conditions - usually shows up with potential or intransitive verbs: 子こどもが触さわれないようにする ("make it so children can't touch it"), 後うしろからも聞きこえるようにする ("make it audible from the back"). Here nobody is making an effort; something is being set up.

～ようにする · Nuance & register (cont.)

Register is neutral, and する carries the politeness as usual. One small pitfall: よう used this way is not the よう of 雨あめが降ふるようだ ("it seems"). Same syllable, unrelated job.

～ようにする · ～ようにする vs ～ことにする

Rule of thumb - ことにする is a decision, taken once. ようにする is an effort, made repeatedly. A decision is announced; an effort is only ever aimed at.

来月らいげつからジムに**通かようことにしました**。
I've decided to start going to the gym next month.

週しゅうに二回にかいはジムに**行いくようにしています**。
I try to get to the gym twice a week.

～ようにする · ～ようにする vs ～ことにする (cont.)

The first is a single event: you decided. The second describes how you conduct yourself over time, and it quietly admits the weeks you miss. This is why a one-off plan cannot take ようにする: ❌来週らいしゅう、京都きょうとへ行いくようにしました is wrong, because a single trip is not a policy.

～ようにする · ～ようにする vs ～ようになる

Rule of thumb - する is you steering; なる is the change arriving. Effort versus result.

漢字かんじを毎日まいにち十個じゅっこずつ**覚おぼえるようにしました**。
I made a point of learning ten kanji a day.

新聞しんぶんが少すこし**読よめるようになりました**。
I've got to the point where I can read a newspaper a little.

～ようにする · ～ようにする vs ～ようになる (cont.)

The first is the work; the second is what the work produced. A very common error is to use する where the sentence is really about a change you underwent: ❌日本語にほんごが話はなせるようにしました claims you engineered your own fluency, and native ears hear it as strange. You became able - なりました.

～ようにする · ～ようにしてください vs ～てください

Rule of thumb - てください asks for one action. ようにしてください asks you to make it generally so, and often carries an ongoing obligation.

まどを**閉しめてください**。
Please close the window.

出でかけるときは、まどを**閉しめるようにしてください**。
Please make sure you close the windows when you go out.

～ようにする · ～ようにしてください vs ～てください (cont.)

The first is about this window, now. The second is a standing instruction, and it implies that on some past occasion the windows were left open. That implication is why the form can sting: to a colleague, 返事へんじを早はやく出だすようにしてください lands closer to a reprimand than a request.

～ようにする · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 歩あるきますようにしています。 → ✅ 歩あるくようにしています。
    よう is a noun, so the verb in front of it is plain.

  • ❌ 甘あまいものを食たべるようにしていません。 → ✅ 甘あまいものを食たべないようにしています。
    The negative belongs on the main verb. Negating する denies that you are making any effort at all.

  • ❌ 来週らいしゅう、映画えいがを見みに行いくようにしました。 → ✅ 来週らいしゅう、映画えいがを見みに行いくことにしました。
    A single planned outing is a decision, not a policy. ようにする needs something repeated or maintained.

  • ❌ 一年いちねん勉強べんきょうして、日本語にほんごが話はなせるようにしました。 → ✅ …話はなせるようになりました。
    You did not arrange your own fluency; you acquired it. Change that happens to you takes なる.

～ようにする · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 毎日まいにち野菜やさいを食たべるようになっています。（meaning "I try to"） → ✅ 毎日まいにち野菜やさいを食たべるようにしています。
    ようになっている would report a state that came about by itself. If the effort is yours, it is して.

～ようにする · Practice 4

The sentence is about a change the speaker underwent as a result of practice, so it needs なる. ようにしました would claim you arranged your own ability, which is not something a person can do to themselves.

～ようにする · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 毎朝まいあさ六時ろくじに起おきます。 (b) 毎朝まいあさ六時ろくじに起おきるようにしています。

～ようになる

Meaning · to reach the point where ～; to come to do ～ - a change that has taken place over time

N5 introduced なる with the note that verbs need ように in front of it. This is that pattern in full. ようになる marks a threshold crossed: there was a time when this was not so, and now it is. That is why it is the natural home of the potential form - 話はなせるようになりました, "I've got to where I can speak" - and why it cannot describe a single event.

～ようになる · Formation

Attaches to Form Example

Use なくなる; you will meet ないようになる occasionally, usually where the speaker wants to stress that the change was deliberate.

この店みせのドアは、人ひとが近ちかづくと自動じどうで**開あくようになっています**。
The door of this shop is set up to open automatically when someone approaches.

祖母そぼはスマホでメールを**送おくれるようになって**、とても喜よろこんでいます。
My grandmother has learnt to send messages on her phone and is delighted about it.

子こどもが生うまれてから、夜よるは早はやく家いえに**帰かえるようになりました**。
Since our child was born I've taken to coming home early in the evenings.

日本にほんに来きたばかりのころは何なにも分わかりませんでしたが、半年はんとしで買かい物ものぐらいはできるようになりました。
When I'd just arrived in Japan I understood nothing, but within six months I could at least manage the shopping.

～ようになる · Nuance & register

Everything about this pattern depends on there being a before. The sentence implicitly says "it wasn't like this previously", so it is odd wherever no change is involved. This is why a one-off future event cannot use it: ❌明日あした、会議かいぎに出でるようになります - attending one meeting is not a new state of affairs. That sentence wants ことになります.

The two everyday uses are:

～ようになる · Nuance & register (cont.)

  • New ability. Almost always with the potential form:
    運転うんてんできるようになった, 漢字かんじが書かけるようになった. Learners under-use this and reach for the bare potential instead, which reports the ability without the achievement in it.
  • New habit. With a plain verb:
    最近さいきん、本ほんを読よむようになりました. Nothing about the speaker's ability changed; their behaviour did.

～ようになる · Nuance & register (cont.)

ようになっている is a third, quieter use: a state that has come about, or a mechanism set up so that something happens. この機械きかいは三十分さんじゅっぷんで止とまるようになっています is how you describe the way a device behaves, and manuals are full of it. The close relative ようにできている puts the stress on the design rather than on the state.

Because the change is presented as something that happened to the subject, the pattern is modest by nature. Saying 話はなせるようになりました about your own Japanese sounds more graceful than 話はなせます, and it is the standard answer when someone compliments your progress. It is register-neutral otherwise; なる carries the politeness.

～ようになる · Nuance & register (cont.)

Note - 分わかる, できる and ある already contain the sense of a state, and they take ようになる directly: 分わかるようになりました. There is no ❌分わかれるようになる.

～ようになる · ～ようになる vs ～なくなる

Rule of thumb - these are the positive and negative halves of one idea. Started doing it: ようになった. Stopped doing it: なくなった.

引ひっ越こしてから、バスを**使つかうようになりました**。
Since moving I've started using the bus.

引ひっ越こしてから、電車でんしゃに**乗のらなくなりました**。
Since moving I've stopped taking the train.

～ようになる · ～ようになる vs ～なくなる (cont.)

The asymmetry catches people: the positive needs ように, the negative does not, because ない behaves like an い-adjective and takes the く connector. The same split gives you 読よめるようになった against 読よめなくなった.

～ようになる · ～ようになる vs ～ようにする

Rule of thumb - なる is the change; する is the effort that produces it. Do not use する for something that happened to you.

毎日まいにち少すこしずつ漢字かんじを**覚おぼえるようにしました**。
I made a point of learning a few kanji every day.

新聞しんぶんの見出みだしが**読よめるようになりました**。
I got to the point where I could read newspaper headlines.

The first sentence is the practice; the second is the result, and the two are often said one after the other. Where English says "I managed to learn to read", Japanese splits the effort from the outcome and marks which one it means.

～ようになる · ～ようになる vs ～くなる／～になる

Rule of thumb - ように is only for verbs. い-adjectives take く, and な-adjectives and nouns take に.

日ひが長ながくなりました。
The days have got longer.

日本語にほんごで冗談じょうだんが言いえるようになりました。
I've reached the point where I can crack jokes in Japanese.

Watch the potential form here. 言いえる is a verb, not an adjective, so it takes ように even though its る ending makes it look like a candidate for くなる. ❌言いえくなる is a fairly common slip.

～ようになる · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 泳およぐになりました → ✅ 泳およぐようになりました
    A verb needs ように to attach to なる. Only nouns join なる with a bare に.

  • ❌ 話はなせくなりました → ✅ 話はなせるようになりました
    The potential form is a verb. くなる is the い-adjective connector and does not apply.

  • ❌ たばこを吸すわないようになりました → ✅ たばこを吸すわなくなりました
    For a change in the negative direction, ない contracts with なる directly. The ようになる version is not impossible but sounds laboured.

～ようになる · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 部屋へやが静しずかなようになりました。 → ✅ 部屋へやが静しずかになりました。
    な-adjectives take に. ように here would be the "seems like" よう, which gives a completely different sentence.

  • ❌ 来月らいげつから大阪おおさかに住すむようになります。 → ✅ 来月らいげつから大阪おおさかに住すむことになりました。
    A single arranged move is a decision landing, not a gradual change. Use ことになる.

～ようになる · Practice 1

1. 毎日まいにち練習れんしゅうしたので、ギターが少すこし弾ひける____なりました。

弾ひけるようになりました。

弾ひける is a potential verb, and verbs attach to なる through ように. The sentence reports a threshold crossed - you could not before, and now you can.

～ようになる · Practice 2

2. 大人おとなになってから、朝あさのコーヒーを（飲のまないようになりました・飲のまなくなりました）。

飲のまなくなりました。

For a change away from doing something, ない joins なる directly as なくなる, because ない inflects like an い-adjective. The ないようになる version is grammatical but stilted, and no one would choose it here.

～ようになる · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "My daughter has started reading books on her own."

娘むすめが一人ひとりで本ほんを**読よむようになりました**。

This is a new habit rather than a new ability, so the plain dictionary form goes in front of ようになる. Using the potential 読よめるようになりました would change the meaning to "she has learnt to read".

～ようになる · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 運動うんどうを始はじめてから、体からだの調子ちょうしがいいようになりました。

✅ 運動うんどうを始はじめてから、体からだの調子ちょうしがよくなりました。

いい is an い-adjective, so it changes with くなる - and irregularly, through the よ stem: いい → よくなる. ように only ever attaches to verbs.

～ようになる · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 日本語にほんごのニュースが分わかります。 (b) 日本語にほんごのニュースが分わかるようになりました。

(a) states a present ability flatly: you understand Japanese news. It says nothing about where you started.

(b) puts a history behind it - there was a time when you did not, and now you do. That makes it both more informative and more modest: the change is presented as something that happened, rather than as a skill you are asserting. When a Japanese speaker praises your progress, (b) is the graceful reply.
