～ことができる

Meaning · can; be able to - the long, formal way of saying that something is possible

～ことができる turns a verb into a noun-like chunk with こと and then says that that chunk "exists as a possibility". It means exactly what the short potential form means, but it is longer, flatter and more official - the shape you meet on signs, in manuals, in contracts and in written explanations.

～ことができる · Formation

Attach ことができる to the dictionary form. Nothing about the verb changes, which is what makes this pattern the safe fallback while the potential form is still settling in.

～ことができる · Formation (cont.)

Note - できる is an ordinary る-verb, so everything after こと conjugates normally: ことができて, ことができれば, ことができるように. The こと itself never changes and is never replaced by の.

～ことができる · Examples

私(わたし)はピアノを弾(ひ)くことができます。
I can play the piano.

ここで写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)ることができません。
You can't take photographs here.

田中(たなか)さんは中国語(ちゅうごくご)を話(はな)すことができる。
Tanaka can speak Chinese.

このカードで電車(でんしゃ)にも乗(の)ることができます。
You can use this card on the trains as well.

子(こ)どものころは自転車(じてんしゃ)に乗(の)ることができなかった。
As a child I couldn't ride a bicycle.

～ことができる · Examples (cont.)

予約(よやく)は三日(みっか)前(まえ)まで変更(へんこう)することができます。
Reservations may be changed up to three days in advance.

インターネットさえあれば、家(いえ)からでも会議(かいぎ)に参加(さんか)することができます。

～ことができる · Nuance & register

～ことができる is not casual. It is grammatically fine at any politeness level, but its flavour is written and institutional. A shop puts ご利用(りよう)いただくことができます on a sign; a friend asking whether you can come tonight says 来(こ)られる?, never ❌来(く)ることができる?. Using it in chatty speech is the single clearest way to sound like a textbook.

Three places where it is genuinely the better choice:

  • Rules and notices. 館内(かんない)では飲食(いんしょく)することができません.
    The length reads as officialdom, which is the point.
  • When the potential form would be ambiguous. For る-verbs,
    食(た)べられる could be "can eat" or "gets eaten"; 食(た)べることができる can only be "can eat".
  • In careful writing, where the ら抜(ぬ)き potential
    (食(た)べれる) is unacceptable and the full 食(た)べられる feels heavy.

～ことができる · Nuance & register (cont.)

With する-verbs the pattern is often padded out for no reason. 運転(うんてん)することができます and 運転(うんてん)できます mean the same thing, and the short one is what people say. Reserve the long version for the register that wants it.

Finally, note that できる here is a stative verb, so it does not take ～ている for a present ability: ❌泳(およ)ぐことができています. Plain 泳(およ)ぐことができます already covers the present.

～ことができる · ～ことができる vs the potential form

Rule of thumb - same meaning, different register. The potential form is what you say; ～ことができる is what the notice on the wall prints.

私(わたし)は漢字(かんじ)を書(か)くことができます。
I can write kanji. (measured, explanatory)

私(わたし)は漢字(かんじ)が書(か)けます。
I can write kanji. (ordinary spoken Japanese)

Watch the particle. ～ことができる keeps the original を, because the object still belongs to the inner verb. The potential form pulls the object into が. Both 漢字(かんじ)を書(か)ける and 漢字(かんじ)が書(か)ける exist, but ❌漢字(かんじ)が書(か)くことができる does not - the が has nothing to attach to.

～ことができる · ～ことができる vs Noun + ができる

Rule of thumb - a verb needs こと in front of が. A noun takes が directly and must not have こと.

弟(おとうと)はスキーができます。
My little brother can ski.

弟(おとうと)はスキーをすることができます。
My little brother is able to ski. (stiffer, but grammatical)

Nouns of activity - スキー, テニス, 料理(りょうり), 運転(うんてん), 通訳(つうやく) - go straight into が + できる. Adding こと to a noun (❌スキーのことができる) is meaningless.

～ことができる · ～ることができる vs ～たことがある

Rule of thumb - the tense before こと decides everything. Dictionary form + ことができる = can. た-form + ことがある = have ever.

These two look almost identical on the page and mean completely different things.

富士山(ふじさん)に登(のぼ)ることができます。
You can climb Mt Fuji. (it is possible)

富士山(ふじさん)に登(のぼ)ったことがあります。
I have climbed Mt Fuji. (experience)

The verb at the end differs too - できる for ability, ある for experience. Mixing them (❌登(のぼ)ったことができます) produces a sentence that means nothing.

～ことができる · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 泳(およ)ぐのができます。 → ✅ 泳(およ)ぐことができます。
    の nominalises fine before 好(す)き and 上手(じょうず), but this pattern is fixed with こと.

  • ❌ 食(た)べられることができる → ✅ 食(た)べることができる／食(た)べられる
    Pick one. Stacking the potential form inside ことができる says "can" twice.

  • ❌ 日本語(にほんご)をことができます。 → ✅ 日本語(にほんご)ができます。
    A bare noun takes が + できる with no こと and no を.

  • ❌ 来(く)ることができないでした。 → ✅ 来(く)ることができませんでした。
    できる is a verb, not an adjective; the past negative is できませんでした.

  • ❌ 漢字(かんじ)が読(よ)むことができます。 → ✅ 漢字(かんじ)を読(よ)むことができます。
    Inside the こと chunk the verb is still ordinary and transitive, so its object keeps を.

～ことができる · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) この部屋(へや)を使(つか)うことができます。 (b) この部屋(へや)が使(つか)えます。

Both say the room is available. (a) is the announcement version - a receptionist explaining the rules, a sign on the door, a written policy. Its length and the retained を make it sound like an official statement of what is permitted.

(b) is what a colleague says to you in the corridor. The potential form 使(つか)える with が is compact and unremarkable, and in ordinary conversation (a) would sound oddly ceremonious for the same piece of information.

～られる - potential, "can do"

Meaning · be able to do it - the ability, or the circumstances, are there

The potential form says that an action is available to the subject. It covers both halves of English can: the skill you have (泳(およ)げる, "I can swim") and the opportunity the world gives you (今日(きょう)は休(やす)める, "I can take today off"). Japanese does not separate the two.

～られる - potential, "can do" · Nuance & register

The potential form is register-neutral and is the default way to say can in speech. It has no built-in politeness; ～ます on the end handles that.

The particle. が is the textbook answer and the safest one: 日本語(にほんご)が話(はな)せます. を is also alive and common, and it is preferred when the object is long, when the clause already contains a が, or when the を was never a direct object to begin with - 道(みち)を歩(ある)ける, 家(いえ)を出(で)られる.

～られる - potential, "can do" · Nuance & register (cont.)

ら抜(ぬ)き言葉(ことば). In everyday speech many speakers drop the ら from the る-verb and 来(く)る potentials: 食(た)べれる, 見(み)れる, 起(お)きれる, 来(こ)れる. This is extremely widespread - in some regions it is simply the normal form - and it has a real advantage: it separates potential from passive, which the full form cannot do. It is nevertheless still classed as nonstandard. Do not write it in an exam, an essay or a business email, and note that it never touches う-verbs: 書(か)ける is not ら抜(ぬ)き, it is just the potential.

れ足(た)す言葉(ことば) is the opposite error and is unambiguously wrong: ❌行(い)けれる, ❌読(よ)めれる. An extra れ is inserted where nothing belongs.

～られる - potential, "can do" · Nuance & register (cont.)

Verbs with no potential form. Some verbs already contain the idea of ability or spontaneous perception and refuse the potential: わかる, できる, 見(み)える, 聞(き)こえる. ある has none either. And verbs describing something involuntary - 疲(つか)れる, 降(ふ)る - take one only in odd, marked contexts.

Politeness by potential. Turning a request into a potential question softens it a great deal: 窓(まど)を開(あ)けてもらえますか is gentler than 開(あ)けてください, and 開(あ)けていただけませんか gentler still. This is one of the main jobs the form does in adult conversation.

～られる - potential, "can do" · ～られる - potential vs passive

Rule of thumb - for う-verbs the two forms are different words (読(よ)める vs 読(よ)まれる). For る-verbs and 来(く)る they are identical, and only the particles and the context tell you which one you are looking at.

食(た)べられる is genuinely two words wearing one coat:

私(わたし)は納豆(なっとう)が食(た)べられる。
I can eat nattō. (potential)

ケーキを弟(おとうと)に食(た)べられた。
My little brother ate my cake (on me). (passive)

～られる - potential, "can do" · ～られる - potential vs passive (cont.)

Three signals separate them. The potential marks its object with が and has no agent; the passive marks its agent with に. The potential is about the subject's own capacity; the passive is about something done to the subject. And the passive very often turns up in the past, reporting a nuisance.

When the sentence would be genuinely ambiguous, Japanese avoids the form rather than tolerating it: speakers reach for 食(た)べれる in casual speech or 食(た)べることができる in writing, both of which can only mean "can eat".

～られる - potential, "can do" · the potential form vs ～ことができる

Rule of thumb - identical meaning; the potential form is spoken Japanese, ～ことができる is written and institutional Japanese.

ここから富士山(ふじさん)が見(み)られます。
You can see Mt Fuji from here.

ここから富士山(ふじさん)を見(み)ることができます。
Mt Fuji can be viewed from here. (a sign, a guidebook)

Note the particle again: the potential form pulls the object to が, while ～ことができる leaves the inner verb intact with its を.

～られる - potential, "can do" · 見(み)られる vs 見(み)える

Rule of thumb - 見(み)られる is able to see - you have the chance, the permission, the ticket. 見(み)える is is visible - the image simply arrives at your eyes.

窓(まど)から海(うみ)が見(み)える。
You can see the sea from the window. (it is simply there)

チケットがあれば、リハーサルも見(み)られる。
If you have a ticket, you can watch the rehearsal too. (access)

The same split runs through 聞(き)こえる (a sound reaches you) and 聞(き)ける (you get the opportunity to listen). Because 見(み)える and 聞(き)こえる already carry the meaning, they have no potential form of their own: ❌見(み)えられる.

～られる - potential, "can do" · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 話(はな)せられる → ✅ 話(はな)せる
    られる is only for る-verbs and 来(く)る. An う-verb changes its own ending and stops there.

  • ❌ 行(い)けれる／読(よ)めれる → ✅ 行(い)ける／読(よ)める
    This is れ足(た)す言葉(ことば) - an inserted れ. Unlike ら抜(ぬ)き it is not accepted in any register.

  • ❌ 明日(あした)来(き)られますか。 → ✅ 明日(あした)来(こ)られますか。
    The potential stem of 来(く)る is こ. き is the ます-stem (来(き)ます), and it does not belong here.

  • ❌ 日本語(にほんご)がわかれます。 → ✅ 日本語(にほんご)がわかります。
    わかる already means "is understandable to me". It has no potential form.

  • ❌ レポートを食(た)べれます（in an essay） → ✅ …食(た)べられます
    ら抜(ぬ)き is fine among friends and wrong in writing. In anything marked or submitted, keep the ら.

～られる - passive, "it was done to me"

Meaning · something is done to the subject - often, in Japanese, something the subject would rather have avoided

The passive rebuilds a sentence around the person or thing on the receiving end. English does this to move an object into the subject slot. Japanese does that too, but it also does something English cannot: it can build a passive on an intransitive verb, to say that an event happened and you were stuck with the consequences.

～られる - passive, "it was done to me" · Nuance & register

The passive itself is register-neutral; politeness comes from ～ます as usual. What is not neutral is how often you reach for it. English uses the passive freely to avoid naming an agent; Japanese usually solves that problem by simply leaving the subject out of an active sentence. Translating every English passive into a Japanese one produces stiff, faintly self-pitying prose.

Marking the agent. に is the default. Two alternatives matter:

  • によって for creation, discovery, invention and other one-off acts, especially
    with an inanimate subject: この絵(え)はピカソによって描(か)かれた. Saying ピカソに描(か)かれた makes the painting sound put-upon.
  • から with verbs of giving, telling and teaching, where the thing flows out of
    the agent: 先生(せんせい)から注意(ちゅうい)された.

～られる - passive, "it was done to me" · Nuance & register (cont.)

The affected subject. A Japanese passive normally wants a person as its subject, because the form is fundamentally about who is affected. Inanimate subjects are fine when the sentence is a neutral report - 会議(かいぎ)が行(おこな)われる, 新(あたら)しい店(みせ)が開(ひら)かれる, この本(ほん)はよく読(よ)まれている - and that use is at home in newspapers and formal writing.

られる as honorific. The same form doubles as a light honorific: 社長(しゃちょう)はもう帰(かえ)られました means "the president has already gone home", not that anyone went home on him. It is weaker than お帰(かえ)りになる and very common in offices precisely because it is easy. Context disambiguates: an honorific られる has no に-marked agent and its subject is someone you are looking up to.

～られる - passive vs potential

Rule of thumb - う-verbs keep them apart (読(よ)まれる vs 読(よ)める). る-verbs and 来(く)る do not - 食(た)べられる, 見(み)られる and 来(こ)られる are one shape doing two jobs.

夜中(よなか)に友(とも)だちに来(こ)られて、寝(ね)られなかった。
A friend turned up in the middle of the night and I couldn't sleep.

That sentence contains both: 来(こ)られて is passive (a friend did it to me, and it was a nuisance), 寝(ね)られなかった is potential (I lacked the ability). Nothing in the endings tells you apart - the particles do. A passive has an agent in に; a potential has an object in が and no agent.

Because the collision is real, speakers avoid it. In casual speech the ら抜(ぬ)き potential (食(た)べれる, 見(み)れる, 来(こ)れる) is unambiguous, which is one honest reason it keeps spreading despite being nonstandard. In writing, ～ことができる does the same job.

the plain passive vs the suffering passive

Rule of thumb - if the active sentence had an object, the passive is the ordinary kind. If it had no object at all, you are looking at the suffering passive, and its whole job is to add a victim who was not in the original sentence.

Start from an intransitive verb with nobody affected:

雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った。
It rained.

雨(あめ)に降(ふ)られた。
I got rained on. (and it ruined things for me)

the plain passive vs the suffering passive (cont.)

The second sentence has a silent 私(わたし)は in front. Nothing was done to the rain; the passive exists purely to say that the rain's falling landed on me. The same machinery gives 子(こ)どもに泣(な)かれる, 前(まえ)の人(ひと)に立(た)たれる, 客(きゃく)に来(こ)られる, and - for one of the heaviest sentences in the language - 父(ちち)に死(し)なれる, "my father died on me".

A closely related form takes a transitive verb but puts the owner, not the object, in the subject slot:

誰(だれ)かに自転車(じてんしゃ)を盗(ぬす)まれた。
Someone stole my bicycle.

Note that を survives: 自転車(じてんしゃ) is still the object of the stealing. The subject is me, because I am the one out a bicycle. Saying 自転車(じてんしゃ)が盗(ぬす)まれた is grammatical but sounds like a police report about a bicycle, not a complaint about my morning.

～られる - passive, "it was done to me" · ～られる vs ～てもらう

Rule of thumb - same event, opposite feelings. ～てもらう says you are grateful; the passive typically says you are not.

友(とも)だちに宿題(しゅくだい)を手伝(てつだ)ってもらった。
A friend helped me with my homework. (thank you)

友(とも)だちに日記(にっき)を読(よ)まれた。
A friend read my diary. (thanks for nothing)

Both take the other person in に and both make me the subject, so the only thing carrying the attitude is the choice of ending. Using the passive for something kind - ❌先生(せんせい)に教(おし)えられた for "my teacher taught me" - reads as a complaint; 教(おし)えていただきました is what you want.

～られる - passive, "it was done to me" · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 買(か)あれる → ✅ 買(か)われる
    Verbs ending in ～う go to わ, never あ. Same rule as the ない-form: 買(か)わない.

  • ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)られた。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)に降(ふ)られた。
    In a suffering passive the cause is the agent, and agents take に. The が slot belongs to the person who suffered.

  • ❌ 私(わたし)は先生(せんせい)を褒(ほ)められた。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は先生(せんせい)に褒(ほ)められた。
    The praiser is the agent, marked に. を here would try to make the teacher the thing being praised.

  • ❌ この小説(しょうせつ)は有名(ゆうめい)な作家(さっか)に書(か)かれた。 → ✅ …作家(さっか)によって書(か)かれた。
    With acts of creation and an inanimate subject, によって is the natural agent marker; plain に makes the novel sound like a victim.

～られる - passive, "it was done to me" · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 夜中(よなか)に友(とも)だちに来(き)られた。 → ✅ …友(とも)だちに来(こ)られた。
    The passive stem of 来(く)る is こ. き is the ます-stem and has no business in a passive.

Both are the same form, but the に-marked agent and the を-marked object make this a passive, not a potential - and a passive about a completed nuisance goes in the past. A potential reading would need が and no agent: ケーキが食(た)べられる, "I'm able to eat cake".

～られる - passive, "it was done to me" · Practice 5

～させる - causative

Meaning · make someone do something, or let someone do something

One form, two meanings that feel like opposites in English. ～させる says that the subject caused the action - and Japanese leaves it to the particles and the situation to say whether that causing was an order or a permission. Learning to read that difference is most of the work on this page.

Not coming home for three days without a word - there's a limit to how much worry you can put your family through.

～させる - causative · Nuance & register

The form is register-neutral, but the social geometry is not. A causative sentence says the subject has enough authority over the causee to bring the action about. That means it is fundamentally a downward or lateral form: a parent over a child, a teacher over a student, a manager over staff. Using it with a superior as the causee is not merely impolite, it is close to nonsense - ❌社長(しゃちょう)に資料(しりょう)を作(つく)らせました claims you gave the president orders. What you want is 作(つく)っていただきました.

The one place a causative points upward is when you turn it on yourself. ～させていただく frames your own action as something the listener graciously permits, and it is the backbone of business humility: 説明(せつめい)させていただきます, お先(さき)に失礼(しつれい)させていただきます.

～させる - causative · Nuance & register (cont.)

The short causative. う-verbs have a contracted causative in ～す: 行(い)かす, 飲(の)ます, 待(ま)たす, やらす. It is casual, common in speech and especially associated with Kansai. It conjugates as an う-verb (行(い)かした, 行(い)かして). Keep ～せる in writing and in polite speech.

Emotion causatives are so common they are worth learning as vocabulary: 喜(よろこ)ばせる, 困(こま)らせる, 驚(おどろ)かせる, 安心(あんしん)させる, がっかりさせる. They take the person in を, and they are the normal way to say "make someone feel X" - there is no separate construction.

～させる - causative · させる - "make" vs "let"

Rule of thumb - nothing in the verb distinguishes them. Look at three things: the particle on the causee, whether the causee wanted it, and whether a ～てあげる／～てくれる is attached.

With an intransitive verb the particle is a live choice, and it carries weight:

息子(むすこ)を塾(じゅく)に通(かよ)わせている。
I have my son attending cram school. (my decision)

息子(むすこ)に好(す)きなだけ遊(あそ)ばせている。
I let my son play as much as he likes. (his wish, I allow it)

～させる - causative · させる - "make" vs "let" (cont.)

を presents the causee as something the subject moves around; に presents them as a person with their own will who is being given room. With a transitive verb the object has already claimed を, so the causee is stuck with に and the particle stops helping - 弟(おとうと)に皿(さら)を洗(あら)わせた is "made" or "let" depending entirely on whether 弟(おとうと) wanted to.

The clearest signal in real Japanese is the benefactive tail. A causative with ～てくれる, ～てあげる or ～てやる is always let, because those verbs mean the action was a favour:

課長(かちょう)は会議(かいぎ)に新人(しんじん)を出(で)させてくれた。
The section chief let the new hire sit in on the meeting.

Strip the くれた and 新人(しんじん)を出(で)させた tilts straight back towards "made him attend".

～させる - causative · ～させる vs ～てもらう

Rule of thumb - who is holding the power? ～させる puts it with the subject; ～てもらう puts it with the other person, whose cooperation you receive.

部長(ぶちょう)は山田(やまだ)さんに資料(しりょう)を集(あつ)めさせた。
The manager had Yamada gather the materials.

山田(やまだ)さんに資料(しりょう)を集(あつ)めてもらった。
I got Yamada to gather the materials (and I'm grateful).

Both English translations can be "had someone do it", which is why the pair is confusing. The Japanese is not ambiguous at all: させる says you had the standing to require it; てもらう says they did you a favour. Since you almost never have standing over a customer or a senior, ～ていただく is the only safe choice upwards.

～させる - causative · ～させる vs a lexical transitive verb

Rule of thumb - if the language already owns a transitive verb for the idea, use it. A causative built on the intransitive twin sounds laboured, and sometimes means something different.

Japanese has hundreds of intransitive/transitive pairs, and the transitive member already contains "cause":

毎朝(まいあさ)七(しち)時(じ)に子(こ)どもを起(お)こす。
I wake the children at seven every morning.

起(お)きさせる exists, but it means something narrower and stranger - forcing someone to get themselves up. The same holds for 見(み)せる versus 見(み)させる: 写真(しゃしん)を見(み)せる is "show someone a photo", while 写真(しゃしん)を見(み)させる is "make them look at it". Other pairs to reach for first: 着(き)せる (dress someone), 止(と)める (stop something), 入(い)れる (put in), 決(き)める (decide).

～させる - causative · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 使(つか)あせる → ✅ 使(つか)わせる
    Verbs in ～う take わ, matching 使(つか)わない and 使(つか)われる.

  • ❌ 食(た)べらせる → ✅ 食(た)べさせる
    る-verbs add the whole of させる. Only う-verbs get the bare せる.

  • ❌ 子(こ)どもに泣(な)かせた。 → ✅ 子(こ)どもを泣(な)かせた。
    Nobody permits a child to cry. Verbs of emotion take the causee in を, because the "letting" reading is unavailable.

  • ❌ 学生(がくせい)を作文(さくぶん)を書(か)かせる → ✅ 学生(がくせい)に作文(さくぶん)を書(か)かせる
    One を per clause. The object has it, so the causee falls to に.

  • ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)に説明(せつめい)させました。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)に説明(せつめい)していただきました。
    A causative claims authority over the causee. Pointed at your boss it is worse than rude - it is a status error.

～させられる - causative-passive

Meaning · to be made to do something you did not want to do

This is the causative with the passive stacked on top of it, and the meaning is exactly that sum: somebody made me do it, and I am the one it happened to. Almost every sentence built with ～させられる carries an audible and I hated it.

る-verbs, する and 来(く)る never contract either: 食(た)べさせられる, させられる, 来(こ)させられる are the only forms.

The sentence shape is the passive's, with the person who gave the order in に:

私(わたし)は 母(はは)に 野菜(やさい)を 食(た)べさせられた。

～させられる - causative-passive · Nuance & register

The default colour of ～させられる is grievance. Even without any explicit complaint attached, the form implies the speaker had no say. That makes it the natural way to describe unpaid overtime, childhood piano lessons, drinking-party rituals and school rules, and an odd way to describe anything you enjoyed. If the action was welcome, use ～させてもらう or ～させていただく instead: 好(す)きな仕事(しごと)をやらせてもらっている.

Contracted or full? For う-verbs, the contracted ～される is what people actually say: 待(ま)たされる, 飲(の)まされる, 買(か)わされる. The full ～せられる is not wrong, but it sounds heavy and slightly literary, and outside the ～す verbs you will rarely hear it in conversation.

～させられる - causative-passive · Nuance & register (cont.)

The one warm use. A handful of verbs of thought and feeling take the causative-passive to mean "it moved me", with no unwillingness at all - 考(かんが)えさせられる, 驚(おどろ)かされる, 感動(かんどう)させられる. Here the cause is usually a thing rather than a person, and the に marks that thing: その言葉(ことば)に考(かんが)えさせられた. This is common in reviews and essays.

Register. The form is neutral and works at any politeness level - 残業(ざんぎょう)させられました is perfectly polite. What it is not is neutral in content: describing your boss's instructions as させられました in front of that boss is a small act of protest, and will be heard as one.

～させられる - causative-passive · ～させられる vs ～させる

Rule of thumb - same event seen from opposite ends. ～させる puts the person with the power in the subject slot; ～させられる puts the person who obeyed there.

先生(せんせい)は私(わたし)に教室(きょうしつ)を片付(かたづ)けさせた。
The teacher made me tidy the classroom.

私(わたし)は先生(せんせい)に教室(きょうしつ)を片付(かたづ)けさせられた。
I was made to tidy the classroom by the teacher.

The facts are identical; the sympathy is not. The second sentence is the one you say to a friend afterwards. Note the particle swap: the teacher moves from subject to に-marked agent, exactly as in an ordinary passive.

～させられる vs the plain passive ～られる

Rule of thumb - in the plain passive somebody did the action to you; in the causative-passive you did the action, under orders.

先生(せんせい)に作文(さくぶん)を読(よ)まれた。
The teacher read my essay. (the teacher's eyes, my writing)

先生(せんせい)に作文(さくぶん)を読(よ)まされた。
The teacher made me read my essay out. (my mouth, under duress)

The two differ by a single さ, and they attribute the action to different people. Check who physically performs the verb: if it is you, the sentence needs the extra causative layer.

～させられる - causative-passive · ～させられる vs ～なければならない

Rule of thumb - ～させられる needs a person applying pressure. ～なければならない is obligation with no named source - rules, circumstance, common sense.

明日(あした)までにこの書類(しょるい)を出(だ)さなければならない。
I have to submit this document by tomorrow.

課長(かちょう)に明日(あした)までにこの書類(しょるい)を出(だ)させられる。
The section chief is making me submit this document by tomorrow.

The first is a statement about a deadline; the second is a complaint about a man. Because ～させられる points at a person, it is far more loaded - using it where plain obligation was meant sounds like you are picking a fight.

～させられる - causative-passive · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 話(はな)さされる → ✅ 話(はな)させられる
    Verbs ending in ～す do not contract. The same goes for 出(だ)させられる and 直(なお)させられる.

  • ❌ 読(よ)まさせられる → ✅ 読(よ)ませられる／読(よ)まされる
    This is さ入(い)れ言葉(ことば) - an extra さ in an う-verb. Either the full form or the contraction, never a hybrid of the two.

  • ❌ 食(た)べさされる → ✅ 食(た)べさせられる
    る-verbs never contract. Only う-verbs can lose the せら.

  • ❌ 母(はは)を野菜(やさい)を食(た)べさせられた。 → ✅ 母(はは)に野菜(やさい)を食(た)べさせられた。
    The person giving the order is an agent, and agents in a passive take に.

  • ❌ 休(やす)みの日(ひ)に会社(かいしゃ)に来(き)させられた。 → ✅ …来(こ)させられた。
    The causative stem of 来(く)る is こ. き belongs only to 来(き)ます.

～させてください

Meaning · please let me do it - asking permission by asking the other person to cause it

Literally this says "please cause me to do X". You are not asking whether you may; you are asking the listener to grant it, which puts the decision squarely on their side of the table. That framing is why ～させてください is so much more deferential than a flat "is it okay if I…".

～させてください · Nuance & register

～させてください is polite, but it is not humble in itself; it is a direct request with ください on the end. In business the humble branch of the ladder does most of the work:

  • ～させていただきます announces what you are about to do while crediting the
    other side with permitting it: 担当(たんとう)の田中(たなか)が 説明(せつめい)させていただきます. It is everywhere in Japanese business and broadcasting, and it is also the form that gets criticised for overuse - used where nobody is granting anything (開店(かいてん)させていただきます on a shop notice), it sounds hollow.
  • ～させていただけませんか is a genuine question and the safest way to ask a
    superior for something inconvenient.
  • ～させていただきたいのですが trails off deliberately, leaving the listener to
    offer. Very common, very soft.

～させてください · Nuance & register (cont.)

Several fixed phrases live here and are worth memorising whole: お先(さき)に失礼(しつれい)させていただきます (leaving before your colleagues), 遠慮(えんりょ)させていただきます (a polite refusal - literally "let me hold back"), 検討(けんとう)させていただきます (we'll consider it, which in practice often means no).

In casual speech the bare て-form is the everyday version: ちょっと見(み)させて, 明日(あした)使(つか)わせて. Among friends ～させてよ and ～させてくれ also occur, the latter distinctly masculine and blunt.

One structural point: because the speaker is the one performing the action, ～させてください almost always has an unstated 私(わたし)に. Adding it explicitly (私(わたし)にやらせてください) is a way of pushing yourself forward - volunteering, or claiming the job over someone else.

～させてください · ～させてください vs ～てください

Rule of thumb - ～てください asks you to do it. ～させてください asks you to let me do it. The させ flips who acts.

This is the highest-frequency error at N4, because English "please help" hides the distinction entirely.

手伝(てつだ)ってください。
Please help me. (you do the helping)

手伝(てつだ)わせてください。
Please let me help. (I do the helping)

Both are polite, and they are nearly opposite offers. Before you say either one, check whose hands are going to move.

～させてください · ～させてください vs ～てもいいですか

Rule of thumb - ～てもいいですか asks whether a rule allows it. ～させてください asks a person to allow it. The second is warmer and puts you lower.

この席(せき)に座(すわ)ってもいいですか。
Is it okay if I sit here? (checking, neutral)

その仕事(しごと)を私(わたし)に担当(たんとう)させてください。
Please put me on that job. (asking to be granted it)

Use ～てもいいですか for small, impersonal permissions - a seat, a window, a photograph. Use ～させてください when the other person genuinely holds the authority, and when you want to sound like you are asking a favour rather than checking the rules. Asking a manager ❌明日(あした)休(やす)んでもいいですか is not rude exactly, but 休(やす)ませていただけませんか is what a careful employee says.

～させてください · ～させてください vs ～させていただきます

Rule of thumb - ～させてください asks. ～させていただきます announces, having already assumed the permission.

会議(かいぎ)の前(まえ)に、資料(しりょう)を配(くば)らせてください。
Please let me hand out the materials before the meeting.

それでは、資料(しりょう)を配(くば)らせていただきます。
Right then - allow me to hand out the materials.

The first is still open; the second is what you say as you start moving. Because いただきます is a statement, using it for something the listener has not agreed to can come across as presumptuous - that is the real risk with this very useful phrase.

～させてください · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 手伝(てつだ)ってください（meaning "let me help"） → ✅ 手伝(てつだ)わせてください
    Without させ you are asking the other person to do the work.

  • ❌ 使(つか)わさせてください → ✅ 使(つか)わせてください
    This is さ入(い)れ言葉(ことば). う-verbs take a bare せる, so the causative て-form is 使(つか)わせて - no extra さ.

  • ❌ 帰(かえ)らさせていただきます → ✅ 帰(かえ)らせていただきます
    Same error, in the phrase where it is heard most often. 帰(かえ)る is an う-verb.

  • ❌ 考(かんが)えらせてください → ✅ 考(かんが)えさせてください
    考(かんが)える is a る-verb, so it takes the full させる, not せる.

  • ❌ 来(き)させてください → ✅ 来(こ)させてください
    The causative stem of 来(く)る is こ.

命令形 - the plain imperative · Nuance & register

Treat the 命令形(めいれいけい) as a form you must recognise rather than one you use. Pointed at a real person it is harsh - it is what you hear in an argument, from an angry parent, or from a drill instructor. Most Japanese adults go weeks without saying one to another adult.

Where it does live:

命令形 - the plain imperative · Nuance & register (cont.)

  • Emergencies. Politeness costs syllables, and syllables cost lives:
    逃(に)げろ, 伏(ふ)せろ, 離(はな)れろ. Nobody hears these as rude.
  • Signs and instructions. 止(と)まれ, 押(お)せ, 引(ひ)け. Impersonal
    and addressed to no one in particular. Exam papers use the よ variant: 答(こた)えよ, 選(えら)べ, 述(の)べよ.
  • Cheering. がんばれ, 勝(か)て, 行(い)け - a stadium is exempt.
  • Sport, the military, fiction. Coaches to players, seniors to juniors in a
    club, and a very large share of what characters shout in manga and anime.
  • Quoting. Reporting an order almost always keeps the imperative inside the
    quote: 「待(ま)て」と言(い)われた.

命令形 - the plain imperative · Nuance & register (cont.)

Register colouring. The plain imperative is strongly male-associated in conversation. Women use it in emergencies, in anger and in cheering, but a woman telling a friend 早(はや)く食(た)べろ would be marked. The gentler downward orders - ～なさい and the bare て-form - are unmarked for either speaker.

ろ versus よ: ろ is spoken, よ is written and formal. 見(み)ろ in speech, 見(み)よ in a slogan or on a test. For する the split is しろ／せよ, and せよ has a distinctly stiff, official ring.

Adding a particle does not rescue it. 早(はや)くしろよ is nagging; 来(こ)いよ is a blunt invitation between close male friends. If you want to be gentle, change the form, not the ending.

命令形 - the plain imperative · 命令形(めいれいけい) vs ～なさい

Rule of thumb - both are orders given downward, but ～なさい comes with authority and care, while the 命令形(めいれいけい) comes with force. A parent uses ～なさい; the same parent uses the 命令形(めいれいけい) when they have run out of patience.

早(はや)く寝(ね)なさい。
Go to bed now. (parent, firm but calm)

早(はや)く寝(ね)ろ。
Get to bed. (sharp, angry, or male-casual)

～なさい is also the only one of the two that appears in neutral written instructions to a person - school worksheets, cookery books for children. The 命令形(めいれいけい) in writing is reserved for signage and for the terse よ form on exams.

命令形(めいれいけい) vs ～てください and the bare ～て

Rule of thumb - ～てください is a request and can go upward or sideways. The bare て-form is a casual request between equals. The 命令形(めいれいけい) is neither - it does not request anything.

窓(まど)を閉(し)めてください。
Please close the window.

窓(まど)、閉(し)めて。
Shut the window, would you.

窓(まど)を閉(し)めろ。
Shut that window.

The bare て-form is the one to reach for in casual speech. It is friendly, it is used by everyone, and it is what learners usually mean when they produce a 命令形(めいれいけい) by accident.

命令形(めいれいけい) vs ～な - the negative order

Rule of thumb - the positive order changes the verb's ending; the negative order does not. Dictionary form + な is "don't", and it is just as blunt.

そこに入(はい)れ。
Get in there.

そこに入(はい)るな。
Don't go in there.

These two are a matched pair in tone - signs use both (止(と)まれ, 入(はい)るな) and so do angry people. Beware the collision with the softened command written the same way: ます-stem + な is a gentle order contracted from ～なさい. 食(た)べるな means "don't eat"; 食(た)べな means "eat up". The difference is a single る.

命令形 - the plain imperative · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 来(く)ろ／来(き)ろ → ✅ 来(こ)い
    来(く)る has no ろ imperative at all; the whole word is replaced.

  • ❌ するろ／しれ → ✅ しろ（written: せよ）
    する is irregular here too. Its imperative is not built from the dictionary form.

  • ❌ くれろ → ✅ くれ
    くれる looks like an ordinary る-verb and is not. Compare 見(み)せる → 見(み)せろ, which is regular.

  • ❌ 食(た)べれ → ✅ 食(た)べろ
    Applying the う-verb rule to a る-verb produces 食(た)べれ, which is not a word. る-verbs take ろ.

  • ❌ 先生(せんせい)、これを読(よ)め。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)、これを読(よ)んでください。
    The conjugation is fine; the sentence is not. Never aim a 命令形(めいれいけい) at anyone you owe respect to.

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 静(しず)かにしろ。 (b) 静(しず)かにしなさい。

(a) is raw force. It is what someone says when they have lost their temper, or a line in a film; from a stranger it would start a fight, and in conversation it is strongly male-coded.

～なさい

Meaning · a firm instruction given downward - "do it", said with authority rather than anger

～なさい is the imperative of なさる, the honorific verb for "do". The politeness in its ancestry has drained away, and what is left is a command that is softer in tone than the bare 命令形(めいれいけい) but strictly one-directional: parent to child, teacher to student, exam paper to candidate. You cannot aim it upwards, and pointing it at an adult equal is condescending.

～なさい · Nuance & register

The politeness of ～なさい is a historical fact, not a usable one. Because it descends from the honorific なさる, it sounds educated and controlled rather than crude - but its social direction is fixed downward. A teacher, a parent, an older sibling or a coach can use it. To a colleague it is patronising; to a customer or a superior it is unthinkable.

Three settings where it is completely unmarked:

～なさい · Nuance & register (cont.)

  • Adults to children. This is its home. 早(はや)く起(お)きなさい,
    ちゃんと挨拶(あいさつ)しなさい.
  • Written instructions to a reader. Textbook exercises and exam papers use it
    constantly: 正(ただ)しいものを選(えら)びなさい, 理由(りゆう)を書(か)きなさい. Here it addresses nobody in particular and carries no attitude.
  • Set expressions. お休(やす)みなさい and お帰(かえ)りなさい are frozen
    ～なさい forms that stopped being commands centuries ago. Nobody hears them as orders.

The contracted ～な. Dropping さい gives a much warmer, more casual order: 食(た)べな, 行(い)きな, 見(み)な, もう帰(かえ)りな. It is friendly - closer to English "go on, eat up" - and is common from older to younger and among close friends. It leans masculine in some regions but is by no means exclusively so. Never use it in writing.

～なさい · Nuance & register (cont.)

There is no negative. ❌食(た)べないなさい does not exist. To forbid something at the same level of authority you switch construction: 食(た)べてはいけません, 食(た)べないようにしなさい, or - much blunter - the prohibitive 食(た)べるな.

お～なさい softens it a little further: お入(はい)りなさい, お食(た)べなさい. This sounds distinctly old-fashioned and grandmotherly in modern Japanese, and you will meet it more in fiction than in life.

～なさい · ～なさい vs 命令形(めいれいけい)

Rule of thumb - same direction, different heat. ～なさい is the order of someone with legitimate authority; the 命令形(めいれいけい) is the order of someone applying force.

自分(じぶん)の部屋(へや)を片付(かたづ)けなさい。
Tidy your room.

自分(じぶん)の部屋(へや)を片付(かたづ)けろ。
Tidy your damn room.

A parent says the first every week and the second when they have snapped. There is also a difference in who says them: the 命令形(めいれいけい) is strongly male-coded in conversation, while ～なさい is used freely by everyone.

～なさい · ～なさい vs 命令形(めいれいけい) (cont.)

Note too that only ～なさい is at home in print. A worksheet says 計算(けいさん)しなさい; a road sign says 止(と)まれ. Neither would trade places.

～なさい · ～なさい vs ～てください

Rule of thumb - ～てください asks, and can point in any direction. ～なさい tells, and only points down.

すみません、こちらに座(すわ)ってください。
Please have a seat over here.

そこに座(すわ)りなさい。
Sit down there. (teacher to pupil)

Learners often assume that because ～なさい looks longer and comes from an honorific verb, it must be politer than ～てください. It is not. ～てください is the neutral all-purpose form; ～なさい is what you use precisely when you are not asking. Said to a guest, 座(すわ)りなさい would be startling - お座(すわ)りください is the polite version.

～なさい · 食(た)べな vs 食(た)べるな

Rule of thumb - the same な after two different stems means opposite things. ます-stem + な = do it. Dictionary form + な = don't do it.

冷(さ)めないうちに食(た)べな。
Go on, eat it while it's hot.

まだ食(た)べるな。
Don't eat it yet.

The only visible difference is the る. The first is ～なさい with its tail bitten off and is friendly; the second is the prohibitive and is as blunt as the 命令形(めいれいけい). Intonation helps in speech - the prohibitive な is clipped and stressed - but the stem is the reliable signal.

～なさい · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 来(こ)なさい → ✅ 来(き)なさい
    なさい attaches to the ます-stem, and the ます-stem of 来(く)る is 来(き). The こ stem belongs to 来(こ)い, 来(こ)られる and 来(こ)させる.

  • ❌ 食(た)べるなさい → ✅ 食(た)べなさい
    Never attach なさい to the dictionary form. 食(た)べます → 食(た)べ + なさい.

  • ❌ するなさい → ✅ しなさい
    The ます-stem of する is し.

  • ❌ 廊下(ろうか)を走(はし)らないなさい。 → ✅ 廊下(ろうか)を走(はし)ってはいけません／走(はし)らないようにしなさい。
    ～なさい has no negative form. You have to change construction.

  • ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、ここに名前(なまえ)を書(か)きなさい。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、ここにお名前(なまえ)をお書(か)きください。
    Correctly formed and socially impossible. ～なさい only ever points downward.

～なさい · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) もう帰(かえ)りなさい。 (b) もう帰(かえ)りな。

(a) is an instruction from someone in charge - a parent sending a child home, a teacher clearing the classroom. It is calm, but it is not negotiable, and the full なさい keeps a certain formality.

(b) is the same order with the ending clipped, and clipping it makes it kind. It sounds like an older friend saying "go on, head home" - casual, a bit warm, and completely unusable in writing. Note that neither can be said upwards: even the friendly (b) assumes you are the senior one.

～ように言う

Meaning · tell someone to do something - reporting an instruction without quoting it

～ように言(い)う passes on the content of an order or request. The original words are gone: you cannot tell from the report whether the speaker barked 帰(かえ)れ or murmured お帰(かえ)りいただけますか. That flattening is the whole point of the pattern, and it is why learners meet it most often in the passive - 言(い)われた, "I was told to".

～ように言う · Formation

ように takes the plain form of the verb, positive or negative. Nothing changes about the verb itself, which is why the four verb classes all behave the same way here.

～ように言う · Nuance & register

The pattern is register-neutral. It appears in family conversation and in business email alike, and any politeness sits on the final verb - 言(い)いました, 伝(つた)えていただけますか, お願(ねが)いしました.

The lost politeness. Because ように reports content rather than words, the report is silent about the tone of the original. If that matters, you say so outside the pattern: 強(つよ)い口調(くちょう)で言(い)われた, 丁寧(ていねい)に頼(たの)まれた. Alternatively, quote directly with と.

ようにと. Adding と - 来(く)るようにと言(い)われた - is very common and slightly more formal or written. It changes nothing semantically; think of it as the quotative と reasserting itself.

～ように言う · Nuance & register (cont.)

The passive is the everyday form. In real speech you will hear ～ように言(い)われた far more than the active. Reporting what you were told to do is simply a more frequent thing to say than reporting what you told someone else.

Business softening. Upward, 言(い)う is too blunt. Use ようにお願(ねが)いします or ようにお伝(つた)えください - 山田(やまだ)さんに折(お)り返(かえ)し電話(でんわ)をくださるように お伝(つた)えください is the sort of sentence this pattern was made for.

Dangling ように. A written instruction sometimes stops after ように, with the verb left off entirely: 受付(うけつけ)にお越(こ)しくださいますように. This is formal and a bit old-fashioned, but you will see it on notices.

～ように言う · ～ように言(い)う vs ～と言(い)う

Rule of thumb - と quotes the words; ように reports the instruction. If you can put quotation marks round it, use と.

父(ちち)は「早(はや)く寝(ね)ろ」と言(い)った。
My father said, "Get to bed."

父(ちち)は早(はや)く寝(ね)るように言(い)った。
My father told me to go to bed early.

The first preserves the imperative and with it the temper. The second could have been said in any tone at all. Note what happens to the verb: inside a と quote you keep whatever form was actually uttered (寝(ね)ろ, 寝(ね)なさい, 寝(ね)てください); with ように the verb goes into the plain dictionary form no matter what.

～ように言う · ～ように言(い)う vs ～と言(い)う (cont.)

と can also report a request in indirect speech - 早(はや)く寝(ね)るように言(い)った and 「早(はや)く寝(ね)なさい」と言(い)った are both natural - but ❌「早(はや)く寝(ね)なさい」ように言(い)った mixes the two and is simply ungrammatical.

～ように言う · ～ように頼(たの)む vs ～てくださいと頼(たの)む

Rule of thumb - same message, different packaging. ～てくださいと reproduces the polite words that were used; ～ように summarises what was asked for.

友(とも)だちに空港(くうこう)まで送(おく)ってくれるように頼(たの)んだ。
I asked my friend to drive me to the airport.

友(とも)だちに「空港(くうこう)まで送(おく)ってください」と頼(たの)んだ。
I asked my friend, "Please take me to the airport."

When the request is a favour rather than an order, the ように version usually keeps a benefactive verb - ～てくれるように, ～てもらえるように - because without it the sentence sounds like an instruction rather than a request. Compare bare 送(おく)るように頼(たの)んだ, which reads oddly high-handed for something a friend is doing for you.

～ように言う · ～ように言(い)う vs ～ようにする・～ようになる

Rule of thumb - same ように, three unrelated jobs. 言(い)う reports an instruction, する describes an effort you make, なる describes a change that happened.

医者(いしゃ)に毎日(まいにち)歩(ある)くように言(い)われた。
The doctor told me to walk every day.

毎日(まいにち)歩(ある)くようにしている。
I make a point of walking every day.

毎日(まいにち)歩(ある)くようになった。
I've got into the habit of walking every day.

Learners meet all three within a few weeks of each other and fold them together. Keep the final verb in view: it, not ように, is carrying the meaning.

～ように言う · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 母(はは)は「早(はや)く帰(かえ)りなさい」ように言(い)った。 → ✅ 母(はは)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)るように言(い)った。
    ように never follows a quotation. Either quote with と or summarise with ように - not both.

  • ❌ 来(き)てように言(い)う → ✅ 来(く)るように言(い)う
    ように takes the plain form, not the て-form.

  • ❌ 遅(おく)れないでように言(い)う → ✅ 遅(おく)れないように言(い)う
    The negative slot wants the plain ない-form. ～ないで belongs with ～ないでください, a different pattern.

  • ❌ 先生(せんせい)は私(わたし)を発表(はっぴょう)するように言(い)った。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)は私(わたし)に…
    The person told is the addressee of a speech act, so に.

  • ❌ 静(しず)かように言(い)った → ✅ 静(しず)かにするように言(い)った
    ように needs a verb in front of it. A な-adjective has to be turned into one with ～にする.

～ように言う · Practice 1

1. 受付(うけつけ)の人(ひと)に、ここで待(ま)____ように言(い)われた。

ここで待(ま)つように言(い)われた。

ように takes the plain dictionary form, so 待(ま)つ goes in unchanged. The receptionist is the source of the instruction and takes に in the passive.

～ように言う · Practice 2

2. 田中(たなか)さんは「明日(あした)は早(はや)く来(き)てください」（と・ように）言(い)いました。

「明日(あした)は早(はや)く来(き)てください」と言(い)いました。

Once the words are inside quotation marks, only the quotative と can follow. The ように version would drop the quotes and the politeness: 明日(あした)は早(はや)く来(く)るように言(い)いました.

～ように言う · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "The doctor told me not to eat anything before the examination."

医者(いしゃ)に、検査(けんさ)の前(まえ)は何(なに)も食(た)べないように言(い)われた。

A negative instruction goes in as the plain ない-form, 食(た)べない, directly before ように. The passive 言(い)われた is the natural way to report an instruction you received.

～ように言う · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 部長(ぶちょう)に、この書類(しょるい)をコピーしてように頼(たの)まれました。

✅ 部長(ぶちょう)に、この書類(しょるい)をコピーするように頼(たの)まれました。

The て-form cannot sit in front of ように; the slot takes the plain form, here コピーする. If you wanted to keep the て-form you would have to quote: 「コピーしてください」と頼(たの)まれました.

～ように言う · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 先生(せんせい)は毎日(まいにち)日記(にっき)を書(か)くように言(い)った。 (b) 先生(せんせい)は「毎日(まいにち)日記(にっき)を書(か)きなさい」と言(い)った。

(a) reports only the substance of the instruction. It tells you what the students now have to do and says nothing about how the teacher put it - she may have asked politely or issued an order.

(b) reproduces the teacher's actual words, and those words carry information: the なさい marks her as speaking downward with authority, calmly rather than angrily. Direct quotation is how Japanese keeps that texture, since ように deliberately throws it away.
