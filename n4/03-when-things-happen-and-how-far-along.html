～とき

Meaning · when; at the time that

とき is the ordinary noun for "time", pressed into service as a joint between two clauses: at the time when X, Y. What lifts it above vocabulary is the tense inside the clause. Japanese sets it by looking at the main verb, not at the present moment - so 行(い)くとき and 行(い)ったとき can both sit in a sentence about last year and mean quite different things.

～とき · Formation

とき is a noun, so anything that can modify a noun can modify it - and it takes that modifier in the plain form.

～とき · Formation (cont.)

Attaches to Form Example

Verb (ない-form) ない-form + とき わからない → わからないとき
Verb (ている) ている + とき 運転(うんてん)している → 運転(うんてん)しているとき
い-adjective plain い + とき 忙(いそが)しい → 忙(いそが)しいとき
な-adjective な + とき 暇(ひま)だ → 暇(ひま)なとき

～とき · Formation (cont.)

Attaches to Form Example
In kanji とき = 時(とき) 子(こ)どもの時(とき)
Politeness on the final verb only 来(く)るとき買(か)います

Rule of thumb - the verb in front of とき is plain. ❌来(き)ますとき is not how ordinary Japanese sounds; politeness lives on the last verb of the sentence and nowhere else.

Note - the に is optional. Adding it pins the clause to a definite point (大学(だいがく)に入(はい)ったときに買(か)いました); leaving it off lets the clause read as a general circumstance (寒(さむ)いとき、温(あたた)かいものが食(た)べたくなる). With は on the end - ときは - the clause becomes the topic, which is how you state a rule or a piece of advice.

～とき · Examples

暇(ひま)なとき、何(なに)をしますか。
What do you do when you have free time?

子(こ)どものとき、ピアノを習(なら)っていました。
I used to take piano lessons when I was little.

眠(ねむ)いときは、冷(つめ)たい水(みず)で顔(かお)を洗(あら)います。
When I'm sleepy I wash my face with cold water.

道(みち)がわからないときは、駅員(えきいん)さんに聞(き)いてください。
When you can't work out the way, ask a member of station staff.

家(いえ)を出(で)るとき、かぎをかけるのを忘(わす)れてしまいました。
I forgot to lock up as I was leaving the house.

～とき · Examples (cont.)

大阪(おおさか)に着(つ)いたとき、もう夜(よる)の十時(じゅうじ)を過(す)ぎていました。
By the time I got to Osaka it was already gone ten at night.

運転(うんてん)しているときは、携帯(けいたい)を見(み)ないでください。危(あぶ)ないですから。
Please don't look at your phone while you're driving - it's dangerous.

会議(かいぎ)が長(なが)くなりそうなときは、先(さき)に昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べておいたほうがいいですよ。
When it looks like the meeting is going to run long, you're better off eating lunch first.

～とき · Nuance & register

とき is register-neutral and appears in every kind of Japanese, from a text message to a contract. The politeness of the sentence is decided entirely by the final verb, so 子(こ)どものとき、よく泣(な)いた and 子(こ)どものとき、よく泣(な)きました differ only at the end.

One thing that is worth copying from careful writing: when とき is doing this grammatical job, Japanese usually writes it in kana. The kanji 時(とき) is kept for cases where it means time as a real thing - 時(とき)が経(た)つ, 時(とき)には. Both spellings are read the same and neither is wrong; kana simply looks more natural on the page.

～とき · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two step-ups you will meet as the register rises. 際(さい)に is the formal twin of とき and is everywhere in announcements and manuals: ご利用(りよう)の際(さい)は、係員(かかりいん)にお申(もう)し出(で)ください. And 場合(ばあい) ("in the case that") takes over when the sentence is about a contingency rather than a time: 地震(じしん)の場合(ばあい)は、エレベーターを使(つか)わないでください. Using とき there is not wrong, but 場合 is what the sign says.

Going the other way, casual speech shortens nothing about とき itself - it just drops the に and often the comma, and adds さ or ね: 高校(こうこう)のときさ、よくここで遊(あそ)んだよね.

～とき · 行(い)くとき vs 行(い)ったとき

Rule of thumb - the tense before とき is measured against the main clause, not against now. Dictionary form: at the moment of the main action, the とき-event has not finished. た-form: it has.

This is the single most tested thing about とき, and English gives you no help, because English says "when" for both.

日本(にほん)へ行(い)くとき、スーツケースを買(か)いました。
I bought a suitcase for my trip to Japan. (bought before I got there)

日本(にほん)へ行(い)ったとき、スーツケースを買(か)いました。
I bought a suitcase when I was in Japan. (bought after I arrived)

～とき · 行(い)くとき vs 行(い)ったとき (cont.)

Both sentences are about a finished trip, and both end in ました. Only the form in front of とき tells you which side of the arrival the shopping happened on.

The same logic makes some pairs obviously right and obviously wrong:

お風呂(ふろ)に入(はい)るとき、服(ふく)を脱(ぬ)ぎます。
You take your clothes off when you get into the bath.

You are not yet in the bath when you undress, so it has to be 入(はい)るとき. ❌入(はい)ったとき服(ふく)を脱(ぬ)ぐ describes someone undressing in the water.

～とき · ～とき vs ～たら

Rule of thumb - とき names a time; たら sets a condition. If you could replace the English with "at the time when", use とき. If it is really "once" or "if", use たら.

駅(えき)に着(つ)いたら、電話(でんわ)してください。
Give me a ring once you get to the station.

駅(えき)に着(つ)いたとき、あの店(みせ)はもう閉(し)まっていました。
When I got to the station, that shop had already closed.

たら looks forward and triggers something; とき looks at a point on the timeline and reports what was true there. That is why an instruction about the future almost always takes たら, and why a narrated memory almost always takes とき.

～とき · ～とき vs ～たら (cont.)

とき also reaches places たら cannot go without extra machinery: it attaches straight to a noun with の - 学生(がくせい)のとき - where たら needs 学生(がくせい)だったら, and that already sounds hypothetical ("if you were a student").

～とき · ～ているとき vs ～ながら

Rule of thumb - ながら needs one person doing two things at once, and the main action is the one at the end. ているとき has no such restriction: the two clauses can have different subjects entirely.

テレビを見(み)ながらごはんを食(た)べます。
I eat while watching TV.

私(わたし)がごはんを食(た)べているときに、姉(あね)が帰(かえ)ってきました。
My older sister got home while I was eating.

Swap them and the second sentence breaks: ❌食(た)べながら姉(あね)が帰(かえ)ってきた would claim your sister was eating and coming home simultaneously. When the two events belong to different people, ているとき is the only one of the two that works.

～とき · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 子(こ)どもとき、よく熱(ねつ)を出(だ)しました。 → ✅ 子(こ)どものとき、よく熱(ねつ)を出(だ)しました。
    とき is a noun, so a noun in front of it needs の, exactly as in 日本語(にほんご)の先生(せんせい).

  • ❌ 静(しず)かとき → ✅ 静(しず)かなとき
    な-adjectives keep their な before any noun, and とき is a noun.

  • ❌ 国(くに)へ帰(かえ)りますとき、連絡(れんらく)します。 → ✅ 国(くに)へ帰(かえ)るとき、連絡(れんらく)します。
    The clause before とき is plain. Politeness goes on the final verb only.

  • ❌ ガイドブックは京都(きょうと)へ行(い)ったとき、東京(とうきょう)の本屋(ほんや)で買(か)いました。 → ✅ 京都(きょうと)へ行(い)くとき、東京(とうきょう)の本屋(ほんや)で買(か)いました。
    You bought it before you set off, so the trip had not happened yet: dictionary form. The た-form would put the Tokyo bookshop after your arrival in Kyoto.

～とき · Practice 1

1. 大学生(だいがくせい)____とき、北海道(ほっかいどう)を旅行(りょこう)しました。

大学生(だいがくせい)のとき、北海道(ほっかいどう)を旅行(りょこう)しました。

とき is a noun, and a noun modifying another noun needs の between them. Only verbs and adjectives attach to とき directly.

～とき · Practice 2

2. ドアを（開(あ)ける・開(あ)けた）とき、このかぎを使(つか)ってください。

ドアを開(あ)けるとき、このかぎを使(つか)ってください。

The key is what makes the opening possible, so at the moment you use it the door is still shut - the とき-event has not finished. That is the dictionary form. 開(あ)けたとき would have you fiddling with the key after the door was already open.

～とき · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "When I was a high school student, I worked part-time at a convenience store."

高校生(こうこうせい)のとき、コンビニでアルバイトをしていました。

A noun takes の before とき. The main verb carries both the tense and the politeness, and ていました is the natural choice because the job lasted a stretch of time rather than happening once.

～とき · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 忙(いそが)しいですとき、電話(でんわ)しないでください。

✅ 忙(いそが)しいとき、電話(でんわ)しないでください。

An い-adjective goes in front of とき in its plain form. です is a sentence ending and cannot sit in the middle of a clause that modifies a noun.

～とき · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 家(いえ)を出(で)るとき、電気(でんき)を消(け)しました。 (b) 家(いえ)を出(で)たとき、となりの人(ひと)に会(あ)いました。

(a) uses the dictionary form because the leaving was not finished yet: you were still inside, on your way out, when you hit the light switch. (b) uses the た-form because the leaving was finished: you were already through the door when you ran into the neighbour.

Swapping them produces nonsense in both directions - turning off the lights from the street, and bumping into the neighbour in your own hallway.

～後で

Meaning · after something; afterwards, later on

N5 taught this word in kana, as ～たあとで. Here it is in kanji, plus the two jobs the kana page left alone: 後で standing on its own as the adverb later, and the four different readings the character takes once it appears in writing.

～後で · Formation

The clause in front of 後で is in the た-form, whatever the tense of the sentence as a whole. A noun joins with の.

～後で · Formation (cont.)

Attaches to Form Example
Verb た-form + 後(あと)で 食(た)べる → 食(た)べた後(あと)で
Noun Noun + の + 後(あと)で 授業(じゅぎょう) → 授業(じゅぎょう)の後(あと)で
On its own (adverb) 後(あと)で + clause 後(あと)で連絡(れんらく)します
Demonstrative その後(あと)で／この後(あと) その後(あと)で決(き)めましょう
Without で た-form + 後(あと) 見(み)た後(あと)、考(かんが)えます
With に instead た-form + 後(あと)に 私(わたし)の後(あと)に発表(はっぴょう)します
Amount of time Quantity + 後(ご)（に） 三日(みっか)後(ご)に、一時間(いちじかん)後(ご)に

～後で · Formation (cont.)

Attaches to Form Example
Politeness on the final verb only 着(つ)いた後(あと)で電話(でんわ)します
❌ ❌ dictionary form + 後(あと)で ❌食(た)べる後(あと)で

Rule of thumb - one character, four readings, and they are not interchangeable. 後(あと) is after in time, 後(うし)ろ is behind in space, 後(ご) is the suffix on Sino-Japanese words and on amounts of time, and 後(のち) is the formal, written one. Reading 三日(みっか)後(ご) as あと marks you out instantly.

～後で · Formation (cont.)

Note - で is optional and drops freely, and the whole phrase can take は or も: 食(た)べた後(あと)は眠(ねむ)くなる. What you cannot do is put a verb in the dictionary form in front of it - that slot belongs to 前(まえ)に.

～後で · Examples

後(あと)でメールしますね。
I'll email you later.

薬(くすり)は食事(しょくじ)の後(あと)で飲(の)んでください。
Please take the medicine after meals.

仕事(しごと)の後(あと)で、ジムで泳(およ)ぐつもりです。
I'm planning to swim at the gym after work.

宿題(しゅくだい)を終(お)わらせた後(あと)でなら、ゲームをしてもいいよ。
You can play games once you've finished your homework.

試合(しあい)の後(あと)で、チームのみんなでラーメンを食(た)べに行(い)った。
After the match the whole team went out for ramen.

～後で · Examples (cont.)

引(ひ)っ越(こ)した後(あと)で、住所(じゅうしょ)が変(か)わったことを会社(かいしゃ)に伝(つた)えました。
After I moved, I let the company know my address had changed.

説明(せつめい)を聞(き)いた後(あと)で質問(しつもん)がある方(かた)は、手(て)を挙(あ)げてください。
If you have a question after hearing the explanation, please raise your hand.

三十分(さんじゅっぷん)**後(ご)**に出発(しゅっぱつ)しますので、それまでに準備(じゅんび)をお願(ねが)いします。
We leave in thirty minutes, so please be ready by then.

～後で · Nuance & register

As a clause connector 後で is plain and neutral. As a standalone adverb it carries something extra, and that is worth knowing: 後で is deliberately vague. It means "at some point after now, probably today", and it names no time at all.

That vagueness is friendly among equals - じゃあ、また後(あと)でね is how you end a conversation you expect to resume - and unhelpful upward. Told 後(あと)でやります, a manager has learned nothing; 三時(さんじ)までに出(だ)します is what they wanted. If you need the vagueness and the politeness, the business word is 後(のち)ほど: 後(のち)ほど担当(たんとう)よりご連絡(れんらく)いたします. It is one register step above 後で and never sounds casual.

～後で · Nuance & register (cont.)

Spelling is a genuine choice here. In the grammatical use, careful writing often prefers kana - 食(た)べたあとで - because the kanji pulls the reader towards the "behind" meaning. Inside a compound the kanji is compulsory and the reading switches: 卒業(そつぎょう)後(ご), 開店(かいてん)後(ご), 二年(にねん)後(ご).

One last spoken habit: 後(あと)にする means "leave it for later" - その話(はなし)は後(あと)にしよう. It is not the same construction as 後で, but it lives in the same corner of the language.

～後で · ～た後(あと)で vs ～てから

Rule of thumb - てから says the second thing does not happen until the first is done. 後で only says it happens later. When the order is a requirement, use てから; when it is just the order things came in, 後で is enough.

お金(かね)を入(い)れてから、ボタンを押(お)してください。
Put the money in first, then press the button.

Here てから is doing real work: pressing the button before paying achieves nothing. Swap in 入(い)れた後(あと)で and the instruction goes soft - it now merely reports a sequence.

映画(えいが)の後(あと)で、喫茶店(きっさてん)で少(すこ)し話(はな)した。
After the film we chatted for a bit in a coffee shop.

～後で · ～た後(あと)で vs ～てから (cont.)

Nothing about the film enabled the chat. This is plain sequence, so 後で fits and てから would sound oddly purposeful.

てから has one job 後で cannot take on at all: measuring elapsed time from a starting point. 日本(にほん)に来(き)てから三年(さんねん)になります - "it's been three years since I came to Japan." ❌来(き)た後(あと)で三年(さんねん)になります is not Japanese. Going the other way, 後で attaches straight to a noun with の, which てから cannot do: 会議(かいぎ)の後(あと)で has no てから equivalent short of adding a verb.

～後で · 後(あと)で vs 後(あと)に vs ～後(ご)に

Rule of thumb - で is the everyday "afterwards". に locates a point: use it when you are placing something in an order, and you must use the 後(ご) reading with a measured quantity of time.

田中(たなか)さんの後(あと)に発表(はっぴょう)します。
I'm presenting after Tanaka.

五分(ごふん)**後(ご)**にまたかけ直(なお)します。
I'll call back in five minutes.

A quantity of time never takes 後で: ❌五分(ごふん)後(あと)で is simply wrong, both in the particle and in the reading. In the ordinary "X happened, then Y" use, 後で and 後に overlap heavily and 後で is the safer spoken default; 後に leans written, and it is what you want when the sentence is about a slot in a sequence rather than about a time.

～後で · 後(あと)で vs 後(あと)に vs ～後(ご)に (cont.)

There is also 後(のち)に, read のち, which means "later in life" or "subsequently" and belongs to biography and history: 彼(かれ)は後(のち)に市長(しちょう)になった. 後で cannot carry that sense.

～後で · 後(あと)で vs 今度(こんど)・そのうち

Rule of thumb - 後で is later today. 今度(こんど) is next time, on some other occasion. そのうち is eventually, with no occasion in mind at all.

English collapses all three into "later", and picking the wrong one changes what you have promised.

その書類(しょるい)は後(あと)で見(み)ておきます。
I'll look over that document later on. (today, before I go home)

**今度(こんど)**いっしょに飲(の)みに行(い)きましょう。
Let's go for a drink sometime.

～後で · 後(あと)で vs 今度(こんど)・そのうち (cont.)

That second sentence is an invitation, not an appointment, and everyone hears it that way. If you say 今度(こんど)電話(でんわ)します to someone waiting for a call in the next hour, they will assume you mean next week. そのうち is vaguer still - そのうち片付(かたづ)けます is close to admitting it may never happen.

～後で · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 食(た)べる後(あと)で歯(は)をみがきます。 → ✅ 食(た)べた後(あと)で歯(は)をみがきます。
    後で takes the た-form. The dictionary form in that slot belongs to 前(まえ)に.

  • ❌ 授業(じゅぎょう)後(あと)で質問(しつもん)しました。 → ✅ 授業(じゅぎょう)の後(あと)で質問(しつもん)しました。
    後 is a noun, so a noun in front of it needs の. (The compound 授業(じゅぎょう)後(ご) exists and needs no の - but then it is read ご and belongs to written style.)

  • ❌ 二時間(にじかん)後(あと)で戻(もど)ります。 → ✅ 二時間(にじかん)後(ご)に戻(もど)ります。
    With a measured amount of time the reading is ご and the particle is に.

  • ❌ すぐ終(お)わるので、**今度(こんど)**電話(でんわ)します。 → ✅ すぐ終(お)わるので、後(あと)で電話(でんわ)します。
    今度(こんど) pushes the call to another occasion. For "later today", it has to be 後で.

～後で · Practice 1

1. シャワーを浴(あ)び____後(あと)で、ビールを飲(の)みます。

シャワーを浴(あ)びた後(あと)で、ビールを飲(の)みます。

The verb in front of 後で is always in the た-form, even though the sentence describes a habit and ends in the present ます. The た marks the shower as complete relative to the beer, not relative to now.

～後で · Practice 2

2. 十分(じゅっぷん)（後(あと)で・後(ご)に）会議(かいぎ)が始(はじ)まります。

十分(じゅっぷん)後(ご)に会議(かいぎ)が始(はじ)まります。

A measured quantity of time takes the ご reading and the particle に. ❌十分(じゅっぷん)後(あと)で is wrong on both counts, and native listeners notice the reading before they notice the particle.

～後で · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "I'll explain the details later - let's eat first."

詳(くわ)しいことは後(あと)で説明(せつめい)します。まず食(た)べましょう。

後で on its own is the everyday adverb "later", and it is exactly right for pushing something to the end of the same afternoon. 今度(こんど) would mean the explanation comes on a different day.

～後で · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べる後(あと)で、公園(こうえん)を散歩(さんぽ)しませんか。

✅ 昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べた後(あと)で、公園(こうえん)を散歩(さんぽ)しませんか。

Only the た-form goes in front of 後で. The invitation is still about the future, but the た is measuring the lunch against the walk, not against the present moment.

～後で · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 後(あと)でご連絡(れんらく)します。 (b) 後(のち)ほどご連絡(れんらく)いたします。

Both promise a message with no time attached. (a) is ordinary polite Japanese and fits a colleague, a friend, or a shop you deal with often. (b) is the same promise in business register: 後ほど is the formal word for "later", and いたします lowers the speaker. You would hear (b) from a call centre or a receptionist, and it would sound stiff between friends.

～までに

Meaning · by - at any point up to that limit, but not after it

N5 gave you まで, "until", which measures a stretch of time. Adding に turns the stretch into an edge. までに names the last moment at which something may happen, and says nothing about when inside the window it actually does. It is the particle of deadlines.

～までに · Formation

までに follows a time expression, or a clause in the dictionary form that names the moment the window closes.

Emphasis までに + は 八時(はちじ)までには着(つ)きます
Very often with までに + ～ておく 買(か)っておく、片付(かたづ)けておく
❌ ❌ た-form + までに ❌着(つ)いたまでに
❌ ❌ ない-form + までに ❌来(こ)ないまでに

～までに · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - the main verb of a までに sentence has to be something that finishes in one go: 出(だ)す, 着(つ)く, 決(き)める, 終(お)わらせる, 帰(かえ)る. If the verb describes something that goes on - 待(ま)つ, 寝(ね)る, 働(はたら)く - you want plain まで, not までに.

Note - までには adds "at the latest, and I mean it": 七時(しちじ)までには戻(もど)ります promises seven as the outer bound, whereas 七時(しちじ)までに戻(もど)ります merely states the deadline. The は is what turns a fact into a commitment.

～までに · Examples

五時(ごじ)までに帰(かえ)ってきてね。
Be back by five, all right?

レポートは金曜日(きんようび)までに出(だ)してください。
Please hand the report in by Friday.

電車(でんしゃ)が来(く)るまでに、きっぷを買(か)っておきます。
I'll buy the ticket before the train gets here.

夏(なつ)が終(お)わるまでに、一度(いちど)は海(うみ)に行(い)きたいなあ。
I'd like to get to the sea at least once before the summer's over.

来週(らいしゅう)の月曜日(げつようび)までにお返事(へんじ)をいただけますか。
Could I have your reply by Monday next week?

～までに · Examples (cont.)

お客(きゃく)さんが来(く)るまでに、会議室(かいぎしつ)を片付(かたづ)けておきましょう。
Let's get the meeting room tidied up before the client arrives.

三十歳(さんじゅっさい)になるまでに、自分(じぶん)の店(みせ)を持(も)ちたいと思(おも)っています。
I'm hoping to have my own shop by the time I turn thirty.

申(もう)し込(こ)みは今月(こんげつ)の終(お)わりまで受(う)け付(つ)けていますが、できれば今週(こんしゅう)までに決(き)めてほしいそうです。
Applications are open to the end of the month, but apparently they'd like a decision by this week if possible.

～までに · Nuance & register

までに is register-neutral and lives in the parts of life that have deadlines: homework, forms, trains, work. Nothing about it is polite or blunt in itself - the verb at the end does that. 金曜(きんよう)までに出(だ)せ is an order, 金曜(きんよう)までにご提出(ていしゅつ)ください is a notice, and the までに is identical in both.

What it does carry is pressure, because it draws a line. Softening usually happens around it rather than to it. できれば ("if possible"), 念(ねん)のため ("just in case") and turning the request into a question - 明日(あした)までにいただけますか - all take the edge off without moving the deadline. Dropping に does not soften anything; it changes the meaning.

～までに · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two things you will hear rather than read. First, in speech the whole phrase is often reduced to the bare time: じゃ、六時(ろくじ)ね. Second, the normal way to ask about a deadline keeps the に in place and adds a verb - いつまでに出(だ)せばいいですか, いつまでにお返事(へんじ)すればよろしいでしょうか. Asking いつまでですか without the に is a different question: it asks how long something lasts, not when it is due.

～までに · までに vs まで

Rule of thumb - まで fills the whole span; までに picks one moment inside it. Ask whether the verb can go on for hours. If it can, まで. If it happens and is over, までに.

This is the whole of the point, and the two are not interchangeable in either direction.

三時(さんじ)までここで待(ま)っています。
I'll be waiting here until three.

Waiting occupies every minute up to three o'clock, so まで. There is no single instant at which the waiting happens.

三時(さんじ)までにここへ来(き)てください。
Please get here by three.

～までに · までに vs まで (cont.)

Arriving takes an instant, and any instant before three will do. Say ❌三時(さんじ)まで来(き)てください and you have asked someone to keep arriving continuously until three.

The mirror error is just as common. ❌三時(さんじ)までに待(ま)っていてください asks the listener to complete the act of waiting before three - which is not a thing waiting can do.

～までに · までに vs ～前(まえ)に

Rule of thumb - 前(まえ)に reports order; までに imposes a limit. Both often fit the same sentence, but only までに implies that being late is a failure.

会議(かいぎ)の前(まえ)に資料(しりょう)を読(よ)みました。
I read the material before the meeting.

会議(かいぎ)までに資料(しりょう)を読(よ)んでおいてください。
Please have read the material before the meeting.

The first simply tells you which came first. The second sets a deadline and implies a consequence for missing it, which is why notices, homework instructions and workplace requests reach for までに.

～までに · までに vs ～前(まえ)に (cont.)

Two mechanical differences. 前(まえ)に attaches to a noun with の - 食事(しょくじ)の前(まえ)に - while までに attaches to the bare noun: 食事(しょくじ)までに. And 前(まえ)に can follow a duration to mean "ago" - 三日(みっか)前(まえ)に - which までに can never do.

～までに · までに vs ～中(じゅう)に

Rule of thumb - までに draws a line and lets you act any time before it. ～中(じゅう)に names a period and asks you to act inside it.

今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に返事(へんじ)をします。
I'll get back to you before the day is out.

明日(あした)までに返事(へんじ)をします。
I'll get back to you by tomorrow.

今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に is said on the day in question and treats today as a container. 明日(あした)までに can be said days in advance and treats tomorrow as a wall. When the two overlap - 今日(きょう)までに versus 今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に - the first is what a form says to you in advance, the second is what a colleague says to you at nine in the morning.

～までに · までに vs ～中(じゅう)に (cont.)

Note - watch the reading of 中. After a span you can live through it is じゅう: 今日(きょう)中(じゅう)に, 今週(こんしゅう)中(じゅう)に, 一日(いちにち)中(じゅう). After a word naming an activity or a portion of the day it is ちゅう: 午前(ごぜん)中(ちゅう)に, 会議(かいぎ)中(ちゅう), 仕事(しごと)中(ちゅう). Same character, and no rule will save you at the moment you have to say it aloud.

～までに · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 五時(ごじ)まで宿題(しゅくだい)を出(だ)してください。 → ✅ 五時(ごじ)までに宿題(しゅくだい)を出(だ)してください。
    Handing something in is momentary, so it needs the deadline particle. Without に you have asked for continuous handing-in until five.

  • ❌ 雨(あめ)がやむまでに、ここで休(やす)んでいましょう。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)がやむまで、ここで休(やす)んでいましょう。
    Resting goes on throughout, so the span particle is the right one.

  • ❌ 家(いえ)に着(つ)いたまでに → ✅ 家(いえ)に着(つ)くまでに
    A clause before までに is in the dictionary form. It names the moment the window shuts, and that moment has not arrived yet.

  • ❌ 夏休(なつやす)みまでに毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)します。 → ✅ 夏休(なつやす)みまで毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)します。
    毎日(まいにち) spreads the action over the whole period, which is exactly what までに forbids.

1. 九時(くじ)____空港(くうこう)に着(つ)かなければなりません。

Arriving is a one-off event, and nine o'clock is the limit it must fall inside. 九時(くじ)まで着(つ)く would ask you to go on arriving all morning.

～までに · Practice 2

2. 雨(あめ)がやむ（まで・までに）、この喫茶店(きっさてん)にいましょう。

Staying somewhere occupies the whole stretch of time, so it takes the span particle. までに would need a verb that finishes in a moment - for instance 雨(あめ)がやむまでに注文(ちゅうもん)しましょう.

～までに · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "Could you finish this by Wednesday?"

これを水曜日(すいようび)までに終(お)わらせてもらえますか。

までに marks Wednesday as the deadline, and any time before it counts as on time. Turning the request into a question with もらえますか is what keeps it polite; the deadline itself is not softened.

～までに · Practice 4

The clause naming the deadline goes in the dictionary form. From the point of view of the shopping, getting home has not happened yet, so た is impossible here.

～までに · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) レポートは金曜日(きんようび)までに出(だ)します。 (b) レポートは金曜日(きんようび)まで書(か)きます。

(a) is about a deadline: the report leaves your hands at some single moment no later than Friday, and it might well be Wednesday. (b) is about a span: you will be writing continuously up to Friday, and Friday is when you stop - with no promise that it is finished then.

The particle and the verb move together. Momentary 出(だ)す pulls までに; ongoing 書(か)く pulls まで.

～ごろ

Meaning · around, about - attached to a point in time and nothing else

ごろ blurs a moment on the clock or the calendar: 三時(さんじ)ごろ, 来週(らいしゅう)ごろ. It is not a general-purpose "approximately". The moment you are approximating an amount - three hours, five people, a thousand yen - ごろ is the wrong word and ぐらい is the right one.

～ごろ · Formation

ごろ is a suffix. It goes straight onto the time word, with nothing in between.

Attaches to Form Example

Rule of thumb - ask which question the phrase answers. If it answers いつ ("when?"), use ごろ. If it answers どのくらい ("how much? how long?"), use ぐらい. 三時(さんじ) is a when; 三時間(さんじかん) is a how-long, and one character is the whole difference.

Note - the に sits after ごろ, never before it: 六時(ろくじ)ごろに, ❌六時(ろくじ)にごろ. And once ごろ is there, に is optional - it is dropped more often than not, because an approximate time is already loose.

～ごろ · Examples

毎朝(まいあさ)七時(しちじ)ごろ起(お)きます。
I get up around seven every morning.

昼(ひる)ごろから雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るそうですよ。
Apparently it's going to rain from around midday.

何時(なんじ)ごろそちらに着(つ)きますか。
Roughly what time will you get there?

桜(さくら)は四月(しがつ)の初(はじ)めごろに咲(さ)きます。
The cherry blossom comes out around the beginning of April.

子(こ)どものころ、この川(かわ)でよく泳(およ)いだものだ。
When I was small I used to swim in this river all the time.

～ごろ · Examples (cont.)

去年(きょねん)の今(いま)ごろは、まだ大阪(おおさか)に住(す)んでいました。
This time last year I was still living in Osaka.

夕方(ゆうがた)ごろから風(かぜ)が強(つよ)くなるので、洗濯物(せんたくもの)は早(はや)めに取(と)り込(こ)んでください。
The wind picks up from around evening, so bring the washing in early.

来月(らいげつ)の中(なか)ごろに引(ひ)っ越(こ)す予定(よてい)なので、それまでに荷物(にもつ)をまとめないといけません。
I'm due to move around the middle of next month, so I have to get my things packed before then.

～ごろ · Nuance & register

ごろ is register-neutral and extremely common in speech. It does, however, do something socially useful: it declines to pin anyone down. Saying 三時(さんじ)ごろ伺(うかが)います promises an afternoon visit without promising the minute, which is why it turns up in arrangements between friends and in service Japanese alike. The flip side is that a business appointment that genuinely starts at three is announced as 三時(さんじ)から - putting ごろ on a fixed start time reads as vagueness where precision was expected.

The ご/こ alternation is not two words. It is one word that voices when it is stuck straight onto a time expression: 今(いま)ごろ, 近(ちか)ごろ, 日(ひ)ごろ, 六時(ろくじ)ごろ. Left standing on its own - after の, after an adjective, after この／その／あの - it stays unvoiced: 学生(がくせい)のころ, 若(わか)いころ, あのころ. Both are written 頃(ころ) when written in kanji, though modern text usually leaves them in kana.

～ごろ · Nuance & register (cont.)

今(いま)ごろ deserves a line of its own, because it has an idiomatic second life. Literally "around now", it is also the word for too late: 今(いま)ごろ来(き)ても、もう終(お)わったよ - "turning up now, are you? It's over." Tone carries the difference, and the sting is real.

Finally, ごろ has quietly built a small family of vocabulary meaning "the right time for": 見(み)ごろ (best time to see it), 食(た)べごろ (perfectly ripe), 手(て)ごろ (a reasonable price or size). 桜(さくら)は今(いま)が見(み)ごろです is what the sign in the park says.

～ごろ · ごろ vs ぐらい・くらい

Rule of thumb - ごろ approximates a point; ぐらい approximates an amount. Clock times, dates and eras take ごろ. Durations, counts, prices, distances and sizes take ぐらい.

三時(さんじ)ごろ会社(かいしゃ)を出(で)ます。
I'll leave the office at about three.

三時間(さんじかん)ぐらいかかりました。
It took about three hours.

The pair differ by one character, and that character decides everything: 三時 is a place on the clock, 三時間 is a length. Everything countable behaves like the second one - 五人(ごにん)ぐらい, 千円(せんえん)ぐらい, 十(じゅう)キロぐらい - and none of them will take ごろ.

～ごろ · ごろ vs ぐらい・くらい (cont.)

Note - you will hear 三時(さんじ)ぐらいに in casual speech, and speakers do not treat it as an error any more. ごろ is still the standard choice for a clock time and the one an exam expects. What is unambiguously clumsy is stacking them: 三時(さんじ)ごろぐらい says the same thing twice.

～ごろ · ごろ vs 約(やく)・だいたい

Rule of thumb - ごろ is a suffix and goes after the time. 約(やく) is a prefix on a number and belongs to written and formal Japanese. だいたい is an ordinary adverb and floats freely.

電車(でんしゃ)は**約(やく)**十分(じゅっぷん)遅(おく)れています。
The train is running about ten minutes late.

会議(かいぎ)はだいたい五時(ごじ)ごろ終(お)わります。
The meeting will finish around five-ish.

～ごろ · ごろ vs 約(やく)・だいたい (cont.)

約(やく) attaches to a quantity, so it patterns with ぐらい, not with ごろ; you see it in timetables, reports and announcements, and it sounds stiff in conversation. だいたい is happy in front of almost anything and can sit in the same sentence as ごろ, as the second example shows - the two are not in competition. In written notices you will also meet 前後(ぜんご): 三時(さんじ)前後(ぜんご)に到着(とうちゃく)の予定(よてい), "scheduled to arrive around three", which is ごろ in a suit.

～ごろ · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 二時間(にじかん)ごろ待(ま)ちました。 → ✅ 二時間(にじかん)ぐらい待(ま)ちました。
    二時間(にじかん) is a length of time, not a point on the clock. Lengths take ぐらい.

  • ❌ 六時(ろくじ)にごろ帰(かえ)ります。 → ✅ 六時(ろくじ)ごろに帰(かえ)ります。
    ごろ bonds to the time word first; any particle comes after it. The に is optional, but it can only stand in that order.

  • ❌ 子(こ)どものごろは野球(やきゅう)が好(す)きでした。 → ✅ 子(こ)どものころは野球(やきゅう)が好(す)きでした。
    After の the word is standing on its own as a noun, so it does not voice.

  • ❌ 十時(じゅうじ)ごろぐらいに寝(ね)ます。 → ✅ 十時(じゅうじ)ごろ寝(ね)ます。
    Both words mean "about". Using them together is not wrong so much as sloppy - pick one.

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 三時(さんじ)に伺(うかが)います。 (b) 三時(さんじ)ごろ伺(うかが)います。

～おきに

Meaning · at intervals of - leaving a gap of X between one occurrence and the next

おきに describes something that repeats with a fixed gap in between. The word to hold on to is gap, not every, because that single idea explains why 十分(じゅっぷん)おきに means "every ten minutes" while 一日(いちにち)おきに means "every other day". English translates them differently; Japanese is saying the same thing both times.

～おきに · Formation

おきに is a suffix and needs a number in front of it. It attaches straight to a quantity - of time, of distance, or of countable things.

～おきに · Formation (cont.)

Attaches to Form Example
Minutes, hours Quantity + おきに 十分(じゅっぷん) → 十分(じゅっぷん)おきに
Days, weeks, months, years Quantity + おきに 一日(いちにち) → 一日(いちにち)おきに
Distance Quantity + おきに 五(ご)メートル → 五(ご)メートルおきに
Countable things Counter + おきに 一(ひと)つ → 一(ひと)つおきに
People, seats, rows Counter + おきに 一人(ひとり)おきに、一列(いちれつ)おきに
Before a noun Quantity + おき + の 一日(いちにち)おきの練習(れんしゅう)
Vague quantity 数(すう)～ + おきに 数分(すうふん)おきに、数(すう)メートルおきに
❌ Bare unit ❌日(ひ)おきに ✅一日(いちにち)おきに
❌ Before a noun with な ❌一日(いちにち)おきな ✅一日(いちにち)おきの

～おきに · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - おきに measures the space between two occurrences, never the length of the cycle. 一日(いちにち)おきに leaves one whole day empty: Monday, Wednesday, Friday.

Note - why do the English translations diverge? Because of what the unit is. With days, weeks and years, and with seats and people, the unit is a container the event sits inside, so a gap of one means one whole container left empty: 一(ひと)つおきに座(すわ)る is one seat used, one seat free. With minutes, hours and metres, the event takes no measurable time or space of its own, so a gap of ten minutes simply puts the events ten minutes apart. One definition, two arithmetics.

～おきに · Examples

バスは十分(じゅっぷん)おきに来(き)ます。
The buses come every ten minutes.

この薬(くすり)は一日(いちにち)おきに飲(の)んでください。
Take this medicine every other day.

一(ひと)つおきに席(せき)に座(すわ)ってください。
Please sit in every other seat.

祖母(そぼ)には一週間(いっしゅうかん)おきに電話(でんわ)しています。
I phone my grandmother every other week.

道(みち)の両側(りょうがわ)に、五(ご)メートルおきに木(き)が植(う)えてあります。
Trees are planted every five metres on both sides of the road.

～おきに · Examples (cont.)

この会(かい)は二年(にねん)おきに東京(とうきょう)で開(ひら)かれます。
This conference is held in Tokyo every third year.

係(かかり)の人(ひと)が三十分(さんじゅっぷん)おきに見回(みまわ)りに来(き)ますので、何(なに)かあったら声(こえ)をかけてください。
A member of staff comes round every thirty minutes, so give them a shout if anything's wrong.

去年(きょねん)の春(はる)から一日(いちにち)おきにジムに通(かよ)っていて、おかげで体重(たいじゅう)が五(ご)キロ減(へ)りました。
I've been going to the gym every other day since last spring, and I've lost five kilos as a result.

～おきに · Nuance & register

おきに is neutral and faintly precise-sounding - it belongs to timetables, instructions on a packet, and descriptions of routines. Nothing about it is formal or casual, and it does not change shape in polite speech.

It does have one real-world problem, and you should know about it rather than be caught by it: with days, weeks and years, speakers themselves disagree about what おきに means. By the rule above, 三日(みっか)おきに leaves three days empty and so happens once every four days - but a large minority of native speakers use it to mean once every three days. Everyone agrees about 一日(いちにち)おき ("every other day"); past that, confidence drops.

～おきに · Nuance & register (cont.)

The practical consequence is that when it genuinely matters, Japanese avoids the word. A pharmacist writes 二日(ふつか)に一回(いっかい), a timetable writes 十分(じゅっぷん)間隔(かんかく), and a coach says 中(なか)一日(いちにち)あけて. If you are giving instructions someone has to follow, say how many times per period and skip おきに entirely. If you are merely describing a rhythm, おきに is exactly right.

Before a noun, use の: 一日(いちにち)おきの練習(れんしゅう), 十分(じゅっぷん)おきのアナウンス. It is a noun-like word, not a な-adjective.

～おきに · おきに vs ごとに

Rule of thumb - ごとに names the cycle; おきに names the gap. So 三日(みっか)ごとに is day 1, day 4, day 7 - and 三日(みっか)おきに is day 1, day 5, day 9.

二日(ふつか)ごとに水(みず)をやります。
I water them every two days.

一日(いちにち)おきに水(みず)をやります。
I water them every other day.

Those two sentences describe the same schedule, which is the neatest way to feel the difference: a cycle of two days is a gap of one. Whenever you are unsure, convert to ごとに in your head and count.

～おきに · おきに vs ごとに (cont.)

With small units of time the two land in the same place - 十分(じゅっぷん)ごとに and 十分(じゅっぷん)おきに both describe buses ten minutes apart - which is why the distinction seems to vanish and then reappear the moment you switch to days.

ごとに also does something おきに cannot: it attaches to ordinary nouns and to verbs, meaning "each". 人(ひと)ごとに考(かんが)え方(かた)が違(ちが)う ("everyone thinks differently"), 会(あ)うごとに背(せ)が高(たか)くなる ("he's taller every time I see him"). おきに needs a number and always will.

～おきに · おきに vs 毎(まい)～

Rule of thumb - 毎(まい) means every single one, no gap. おきに exists precisely to describe the gaps that 毎(まい) denies.

毎週(まいしゅう)日曜日(にちようび)にテニスをします。
I play tennis every Sunday.

一週間(いっしゅうかん)おきにテニスをします。
I play tennis every other week.

The mechanical difference is just as important: 毎(まい) is a prefix on a bare unit - 毎日(まいにち), 毎週(まいしゅう), 毎月(まいつき), 毎年(まいとし), 毎回(まいかい) - and it will not take a number. ❌毎(まい)三日(みっか) does not exist; the Japanese for "every three days" has to be 三日(みっか)ごとに or 二日(ふつか)おきに.

～おきに · おきに vs ～に一回(いっかい)

Rule of thumb - ～に一回(いっかい) counts occurrences inside a period and is never ambiguous. When you need to be understood exactly, use it instead of おきに.

一日(いちにち)おきに走(はし)っています。
I go running every other day.

二日(ふつか)**に一回(いっかい)**走(はし)っています。
I go running once every two days.

Again the same schedule, said two ways. The second phrasing has no gap-versus-cycle trap in it at all, which is why hospitals, schools and instruction manuals prefer it. おきに is more compact and sounds more like a rhythm you live by; に一回 is what you write when someone will be counting.

～おきに · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 毎(まい)三日(みっか)掃除(そうじ)します。 → ✅ 三日(みっか)ごとに掃除(そうじ)します。
    毎(まい) cannot take a number. Use ごとに for a cycle, or 二日(ふつか)おきに for the same schedule expressed as a gap.

  • ❌ 一日(いちにち)おきに学校(がっこう)へ行(い)っています。（meaning "every day"） → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)学校(がっこう)へ行(い)っています。
    おきに always inserts a gap. If nothing is skipped, the word you want is 毎日(まいにち).

  • ❌ おきに十分(じゅっぷん)電車(でんしゃ)が来(き)ます。 → ✅ 十分(じゅっぷん)おきに電車(でんしゃ)が来(き)ます。
    おきに is a suffix. The quantity always comes first.

  • ❌ 一日(いちにち)おきな練習(れんしゅう)はつらいです。 → ✅ 一日(いちにち)おきの練習(れんしゅう)はつらいです。
    It behaves like a noun, so it links to the next noun with の, not な.

～おきに · Practice 5

～始める

Meaning · start doing something; begin to

始(はじ)める welded onto the stem of another verb marks the moment an action gets under way. It is the plain, unremarkable "start": no surprise, no drama, and equally usable for something you planned, something you decided on the spot, and a slow change in the weather.

～始める · Formation

It's six, so I'm going to start making dinner.

It's been three years now since I started studying Japanese.

退院(たいいん)した祖父(そふ)は、先週(せんしゅう)から少(すこ)しずつ歩(ある)き始(はじ)めました。
My grandfather is out of hospital and started walking a little from last week.

～始める · Nuance & register

Compound verbs of this shape are register-neutral. 書(か)き始(はじ)める is as ordinary in a text message as in a report, and the politeness of the sentence is decided at the very end - 書(か)き始(はじ)めた versus 書(か)き始(はじ)めました.

What makes 始(はじ)める the workhorse is that it has no attitude. It does not say the start was sudden, welcome, overdue or surprising; it just puts a mark on the timeline. That neutrality is also why it can appear in forms its rival ～出(だ)す cannot: you can request one (書(か)き始(はじ)めてください), propose one (食(た)べ始(はじ)めましょう), or plan one (来月(らいげつ)から通(かよ)い始(はじ)めます).

～始める · Nuance & register (cont.)

With する-nouns there are two routes and they are not quite the same. 勉強(べんきょう)し始(はじ)める treats the studying as an activity that has now begun; 勉強(べんきょう)を始(はじ)める treats it as a thing you have taken up. For events with a fixed shape - a meeting, a lesson, a ceremony - only the second works: you say 会議(かいぎ)を始(はじ)めます, never ❌会議(かいぎ)し始(はじ)めます.

In speech you will also hear the whole idea carried by 始(はじ)めた on its own - もう始(はじ)めた? - where context supplies the verb. That is ellipsis, not a different pattern.

～始める · ～始(はじ)める vs ～出(だ)す

Rule of thumb - 始(はじ)める is a neutral start; 出(だ)す is a sudden, unplanned one. If you chose to do it, or you are asking someone else to, it has to be 始(はじ)める.

来月(らいげつ)から水泳(すいえい)を習(なら)い始(はじ)めます。
I'm starting swimming lessons next month.

後(うし)ろの犬(いぬ)が急(きゅう)に走(はし)り出(だ)した。
The dog behind us suddenly took off running.

The first is a decision announced in advance, and ❌習(なら)い出(だ)します would be strange - you cannot schedule a surprise. The second is an event nobody controlled, which is exactly 出(だ)す's territory.

～始める · ～始(はじ)める vs ～出(だ)す (cont.)

The clearest test is the form of the verb. Requests, invitations and volitionals are closed to 出(だ)す: ❌書(か)き出(だ)してください, ❌歌(うた)い出(だ)そう. Their 始(はじ)める versions are unremarkable.

～始める · ～始(はじ)める vs 始(はじ)まる

Rule of thumb - 始(はじ)める needs somebody doing the starting; 始(はじ)まる is the thing starting by itself. The pair splits exactly as 開(あ)ける／開(あ)く does.

九時(くじ)にミーティングを始(はじ)めます。
We'll start the meeting at nine. (someone is starting it)

ミーティングは九時(くじ)に始(はじ)まります。
The meeting starts at nine. (that's the schedule)

Note the particles: を with 始(はじ)める, が／は with 始(はじ)まる. The compound suffix built on the first of these is the only one that exists - there is no ❌読(よ)み始(はじ)まる. If you want to say a process got under way on its own, either use 始(はじ)める anyway (雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り始(はじ)めた) or reach for 出(だ)す.

～始める · ～始(はじ)める vs ～てくる

Rule of thumb - 始(はじ)める points at the starting line; ～てくる traces a change moving towards now. Both fit gradual change, and they emphasise different halves of it.

葉(は)が赤(あか)くなり始(はじ)めました。
The leaves have started turning red.

葉(は)が赤(あか)くなってきました。
The leaves have been turning red.

The first says the process has a beginning and we have just passed it. The second says the change has been accumulating and here is where it has got to. In everyday speech about weather and seasons, ～てくる is the more common of the two, and the two happily combine: 寒(さむ)くなり始(はじ)めてきた is heard, though it is doing the job twice over.

～始める · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 本(ほん)を読(よ)む始(はじ)める → ✅ 本(ほん)を読(よ)み始(はじ)める
    The suffix attaches to the ます-stem, never to the dictionary form: 読(よ)みます → 読(よ)み + 始(はじ)める.

  • ❌ 手紙(てがみ)を書(か)き始(はじ)まりました。 → ✅ 手紙(てがみ)を書(か)き始(はじ)めました。
    始(はじ)まる is intransitive and cannot take the を object. The compound suffix is always 始(はじ)める.

  • ❌ 来週(らいしゅう)から運動(うんどう)始(はじ)めます。 → ✅ 来週(らいしゅう)から運動(うんどう)を始(はじ)めます。／運動(うんどう)し始(はじ)めます。
    A する-noun needs either を in front of the independent verb, or し to make the compound. It cannot sit bare against 始(はじ)める.

  • ❌ 寒(さむ)い始(はじ)めました。 → ✅ 寒(さむ)くなり始(はじ)めました。
    Adjectives have no ます-stem. Turn the adjective into the verb of change first - 寒(さむ)くなる - and attach the suffix to that.

～出す

Meaning · suddenly start doing; burst out doing

出(だ)す on the stem of another verb says the action broke out. Where ～始(はじ)める merely marks a beginning, ～出(だ)す adds abruptness: nobody saw it coming, nobody arranged it, and often nobody could have stopped it.

～出す · Nuance & register

The suffix is register-neutral - it is as at home in a novel as in a complaint to a friend - but it is never emotionally neutral. Something about the event was unexpected, and the speaker is registering that. This is why 出(だ)す collects the same adverbs over and over: 急(きゅう)に, いきなり, 突然(とつぜん).

The loss of control has one consequence learners rarely anticipate: used about your own deliberate action, 出(だ)す sounds as if you surprised yourself. 気(き)がついたら書(か)き出(だ)していた ("I looked up and I was already writing") is a good sentence; ❌ 明日(あした)から書(か)き出(だ)します is not, because you cannot schedule your own outburst.

言(い)い出(だ)す deserves separate mention. It means "to come out with", "to be the one who raises it", and it carries a whiff of blame: 言(い)い出(だ)したのは君(きみ)だよ - "you're the one who brought it up." That is the suffix doing exactly its usual job, applied to speech.

～出す · Nuance & register (cont.)

Finally, be aware that a handful of very common verbs ending in 出(だ)す are fully lexicalised and have nothing to do with suddenness: 思(おも)い出(だ)す (remember), 取(と)り出(だ)す (take out), 引(ひ)き出(だ)す (draw out), 貸(か)し出(だ)す (lend out), 見(み)つけ出(だ)す (track down). You learn these as words, not as this pattern.

～出す · ～出(だ)す vs ～始(はじ)める

Rule of thumb - same slot, different attitude. 始(はじ)める is available in every form of the verb; 出(だ)す is available only where nobody is in charge.

来週(らいしゅう)から朝(あさ)のジョギングを始(はじ)めます。
I'm taking up jogging in the mornings from next week.

信号(しんごう)が青(あお)になって、車(くるま)が一斉(いっせい)に走(はし)り出(だ)した。
The lights went green and the cars all pulled away together.

The first announces a plan; 出(だ)す cannot go there. The second describes something that simply happened, and 走(はし)り始(はじ)めた would drain the moment of its snap.

When the same verb takes either suffix, the difference is a threshold. 泣(な)き始(はじ)めた reports that crying commenced; 泣(な)き出(だ)した shows you the face crumpling.

～出す · ～出(だ)す vs 急(きゅう)に・いきなり

Rule of thumb - 急(きゅう)に is an adverb that can modify any event. 出(だ)す is a suffix that can only mark a beginning. They stack, and neither replaces the other.

急(きゅう)に電気(でんき)が消(き)えた。
The lights suddenly went out.

急(きゅう)に雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り出(だ)した。
It suddenly started to rain.

Going out is instantaneous, so there is no onset for 出(だ)す to mark and only the adverb is possible. Raining lasts, so both work - and the second sentence uses both at once, which is completely normal and not redundant: the adverb says it was sudden, the suffix says this was the start of something that then went on.

～出す · 出(だ)す "suddenly begin" vs 出(だ)す "outwards"

Rule of thumb - if the first verb is about handling or moving something, 出(だ)す is probably the literal "out". If it is about an activity that can start, it is the aspect marker.

ポケットからかぎを取(と)り出(だ)した。
I took the key out of my pocket.

犬(いぬ)が庭(にわ)から逃(に)げ出(だ)した。
The dog got out of the garden and ran off.

Nothing in the first sentence is sudden - the key came out, and that is all the suffix is contributing. The second sits on the border and carries a little of both: escaping is outward motion, and it is also something that happened without warning.

～出す · 出(だ)す "suddenly begin" vs 出(だ)す "outwards" (cont.)

The one to watch is 思(おも)い出(だ)す. It means remember, not "suddenly begin to think" - the memory comes out, it does not start. Treat it as a single word.

～出す · Common mistakes

  • ❌ みなさん、そろそろ歌(うた)い出(だ)してください。 → ✅ みなさん、そろそろ歌(うた)い始(はじ)めてください。
    A request assumes the listener is in control, which is precisely what 出(だ)す denies.

  • ❌ 来年(らいねん)からピアノを習(なら)い出(だ)します。 → ✅ 来年(らいねん)からピアノを習(なら)い始(はじ)めます。
    You cannot schedule a surprise. Plans announced in advance take 始(はじ)める.

  • ❌ 本(ほん)を読(よ)む出(だ)す → ✅ 本(ほん)を読(よ)み出(だ)す
    The suffix goes on the ます-stem, exactly as 始(はじ)める does.

  • ❌ 急(きゅう)に電気(でんき)が消(き)え出(だ)した。 → ✅ 急(きゅう)に電気(でんき)が消(き)えた。
    消(き)える happens in an instant, so it has no beginning for the suffix to mark. The adverb alone carries the suddenness.

～出す · Practice 1

1. 話(はなし)を聞(き)いたとき、彼(かれ)は急(きゅう)に笑(わら)い____た。

彼(かれ)は急(きゅう)に笑(わら)い出(だ)した。

Laughing is something that starts and then continues, and 急(きゅう)に tells you it was unplanned - both conditions for 出(だ)す. 笑(わら)い始(はじ)めた is possible but flatter; it reports a beginning without the burst.

～出す · Practice 2

2. 来月(らいげつ)から新(あたら)しい習(なら)い事(ごと)を（出(だ)します・始(はじ)めます）。

来月(らいげつ)から新(あたら)しい習(なら)い事(ごと)を始(はじ)めます。

This is a plan, stated in advance, about your own action. 出(だ)す is blocked in exactly that situation, because it means the action broke out of its own accord.

～出す · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "As soon as the film started, the person next to me began talking."

映画(えいが)が始(はじ)まると、となりの人(ひと)が話(はな)し出(だ)しました。

The talking was neither planned by the speaker nor welcome, and it went on - the classic conditions for 出(だ)す. Note that 始(はじ)まる earlier in the sentence is the ordinary intransitive verb, not this suffix.

～出す · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 準備(じゅんび)ができましたので、そろそろ食(た)べ出(だ)しましょう。

✅ 準備(じゅんび)ができましたので、そろそろ食(た)べ始(はじ)めましょう。

ましょう is an invitation, and an invitation cannot be built on a suffix that means "it happened without anyone deciding". Swap in 始(はじ)める, which is free in every form.

～出す · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 子(こ)どもが泣(な)き始(はじ)めた。 (b) 子(こ)どもが泣(な)き出(だ)した。

(a) records the fact: at some point the crying began. It fits a calm account - a nurse writing notes, or a parent explaining what happened at what time.

(b) puts you in the room. It says the crying broke out, probably without warning and probably to everyone's dismay. Both are correct Japanese about the same event; (b) is the one with a reaction built into it, which is also why it cannot be used to announce a plan.

急に

Meaning · suddenly, all at once - and, of an arrangement, at short notice

急(きゅう)に marks a change that arrived with no warning. Its second job is quieter and just as common: applied to plans and appointments it means unexpectedly, at the last minute, which is why it opens so many apologies.

急に · Usage

急(きゅう)に is the adverbial form of the な-adjective 急(きゅう)だ. That matters in practice, because in front of a noun it has to change shape: 急(きゅう)な用事(ようじ) ("something urgent that has come up"), 急(きゅう)な話(はなし) ("short notice"), 急(きゅう)な坂(さか) ("a steep hill" - the same word, applied to a slope). Only the に form modifies a verb.

Its position in the sentence is free; most often it sits immediately before the verb, or at the head of the clause when it colours the whole event. It clusters around three kinds of predicate:

急に · Usage (cont.)

  • change - 急(きゅう)に暗(くら)くなる,
    急(きゅう)に静(しず)かになる, 急(きゅう)に変(か)わる
  • starting and stopping - 急(きゅう)に降(ふ)り出(だ)す,
    急(きゅう)に止(と)まる, 急(きゅう)に消(き)える
  • arrangements - 急(きゅう)に決(き)まる,
    急(きゅう)に休(やす)む, 急(きゅう)に呼(よ)ばれる

It does not take degree adverbs. ❌とても急(きゅう)に is not said - suddenness is not a quantity you can have more of.

The word to keep separate is 急(いそ)いで, the て-form of the verb 急(いそ)ぐ. Same kanji, different reading, opposite kind of meaning: 急(きゅう)に is about the event surprising you, 急(いそ)いで is about you hurrying.

急に · Examples

空(そら)が急(きゅう)に暗(くら)くなった。
The sky went dark all of a sudden.

電車(でんしゃ)が急(きゅう)に止(と)まって、びっくりしました。
The train stopped suddenly and gave me a fright.

急(きゅう)に用事(ようじ)ができたので、今日(きょう)の約束(やくそく)はキャンセルさせてください。
Something's come up out of the blue, so please let me cancel today's plans.

先月(せんげつ)まで元気(げんき)だった祖父(そふ)が急(きゅう)に入院(にゅういん)することになって、家族(かぞく)みんなで病院(びょういん)に通(かよ)っています。
My grandfather was fine until last month and has suddenly ended up in hospital, so the whole family has been going in to see him.

急に · Nuance & register

急(きゅう)に is register-neutral and extremely frequent. It is equally at home in a weather report and in a text to a friend, and it carries no politeness of its own.

Its most useful social function is as the opening of an apology. When you have to change something at the last minute, Japanese says so first and explains afterwards: 急(きゅう)にすみません among friends, 急(きゅう)で申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありませんが at work, 急(きゅう)なお願(ねが)いで恐縮(きょうしゅく)ですが in a formal email. In all three the word is doing the same thing: acknowledging that the other person has had no time to prepare. Leaving it out makes the same request sound inconsiderate.

急に · Nuance & register (cont.)

Watch the shape when a noun follows. 急(きゅう)だ is a な-adjective, so it is 急(きゅう)な仕事(しごと), 急(きゅう)な変更(へんこう), 急(きゅう)な出張(しゅっちょう). The related noun 急(いそ)ぎ exists too and behaves differently: 急(いそ)ぎの用事(ようじ) joins with の, and it means "a job that must be done fast" rather than "a job that came up unexpectedly".

急に · 急(きゅう)に vs 突然(とつぜん)・いきなり

Rule of thumb - all three mean "suddenly". 急(きゅう)に is the everyday default; 突然(とつぜん) is stronger and more written; いきなり complains that there was no lead-up at all.

急(きゅう)に会議(かいぎ)が入(はい)りました。
A meeting's been put in my diary at short notice.

突然(とつぜん)、部屋(へや)の電気(でんき)が消(き)えた。
All at once the lights in the room went out.

いきなり大声(おおごえ)で話(はな)しかけられて、こわかった。
Someone started shouting at me out of nowhere, and it was frightening.

急に · 急(きゅう)に vs 突然(とつぜん)・いきなり (cont.)

突然(とつぜん) is dramatic. It suits accidents, disappearances and news, and it reads as slightly literary in ordinary chat. いきなり is the most colloquial and focuses on the missing preliminaries - no greeting, no warning, no run-up - which is why it so often carries irritation.

急(きゅう)に is the only one of the three that comfortably covers scheduling. 急(きゅう)に休(やす)みます is the normal way to say you are taking unplanned leave; ❌突然(とつぜん)休(やす)みます would sound as though you had vanished.

急に · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 急(きゅう)に来(き)てください。（meaning "come quickly"） → ✅ 急(いそ)いで来(き)てください。／すぐに来(き)てください。
    急(きゅう)に describes an event that surprises people. Telling someone to hurry needs 急(いそ)いで or すぐに.

  • ❌ 急(きゅう)に用事(ようじ)があるので、失礼(しつれい)します。 → ✅ 急(きゅう)な用事(ようじ)があるので、失礼(しつれい)します。
    In front of a noun the な-adjective needs its な. The に form can only modify a verb.

  • ❌ バスがとても急(きゅう)に止(と)まった。 → ✅ バスが急(きゅう)に止(と)まった。
    Suddenness has no degrees, so とても has nothing to intensify. If you want the event to sound more violent, change the verb, not the adverb.

急に · Practice 1

1. 山(やま)の天気(てんき)は____変(か)わるので、上着(うわぎ)を持(も)っていったほうがいいですよ。

山(やま)の天気(てんき)は急(きゅう)に変(か)わるので…

変(か)わる is a verb of change, which is where this adverb lives, and the point of the sentence is that the change gives you no warning. The に form is required because a verb follows.

急に · Practice 2

2. Translate into Japanese: "My plans changed at the last minute, so I couldn't go."

急(きゅう)に予定(よてい)が変(か)わって、行(い)けなくなりました。

This is the scheduling sense: the change came without notice. いきなり would sound like a complaint about somebody's manners, and 突然(とつぜん) would be dramatic for a diary clash.

急に · Practice 3

3. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 急(きゅう)に出(で)かけました。 (b) 急(いそ)いで出(で)かけました。

Same kanji, two different words. (a) is 急(きゅう)に - the leaving was unplanned. Something came up and you went out when nobody expected you to, and the sentence says nothing about how fast you moved.

(b) is 急(いそ)いで, the て-form of 急(いそ)ぐ - you left in a hurry. The trip may have been on your calendar for weeks; what the sentence reports is that you rushed.

～続ける

Meaning · keep doing; go on doing without stopping

続(つづ)ける welded onto a verb stem is the mirror image of ～始(はじ)める. Where that one marks the moment something starts, this one claims the stretch after it: the action ran on, and it did not break off. Very often the sentence is also saying that this took some doing.

～続ける · Formation

Attach 続(つづ)ける to the ます-stem. The compound is a る-verb.

～続ける · Formation (cont.)

Attaches to Form Example
Verb (う-verb) ます-stem + 続(つづ)ける 話(はな)します → 話(はな)し続(つづ)ける
Verb (る-verb) ます-stem + 続(つづ)ける 食(た)べます → 食(た)べ続(つづ)ける
Verb (する) し + 続(つづ)ける 練習(れんしゅう)する → 練習(れんしゅう)し続(つづ)ける

～続ける · Formation (cont.)

Attaches to Form Example
As an adverb 続(つづ)けて 三日(みっか)続(つづ)けて休(やす)む
❌ ❌ dictionary form + 続(つづ)ける ❌読(よ)む続(つづ)ける

Rule of thumb - 続(つづ)ける is transitive and needs someone doing the continuing; 続(つづ)く is intransitive and describes something that goes on by itself. The suffix pair splits the same way.

～続ける · Formation (cont.)

Note - the suffix covers two shapes of continuation, and both are normal. One is an unbroken action: 二時間(にじかん)話(はな)し続(つづ)けた. The other is a habit kept up over a long period: 十年(じゅうねん)通(かよ)い続(つづ)けている. What it cannot do is attach to something over in an instant - ❌着(つ)き続(つづ)ける of one arrival has nothing to stretch.

～続ける · Examples

彼(かれ)は一時間(いちじかん)も話(はな)し続(つづ)けた。
He talked for a solid hour.

駅(えき)の前(まえ)で三十分(さんじゅっぷん)も待(ま)ち続(つづ)けました。
I stood waiting outside the station for a full half hour.

毎日(まいにち)少(すこ)しずつ練習(れんしゅう)し続(つづ)ければ、きっと上手(じょうず)になりますよ。
If you keep practising a little every day, you're bound to get good at it.

祖母(そぼ)は六十年(ろくじゅうねん)同(おな)じ店(みせ)で働(はたら)き続(つづ)けています。
My grandmother has been working in the same shop for sixty years.

足(あし)が痛(いた)くても、ゴールまで走(はし)り続(つづ)けた。
My legs hurt, but I kept running all the way to the finish.

～続ける · Examples (cont.)

外(そと)が暗(くら)くなっても、兄(あに)は机(つくえ)に向(む)かって書(か)き続(つづ)けていた。
Even after it got dark outside, my brother stayed at his desk writing.

野菜(やさい)の値段(ねだん)が上(あ)がり続(つづ)けているので、買(か)い物(もの)が大変(たいへん)です。
Vegetable prices just keep going up, so shopping is hard work.

返事(へんじ)が来(こ)なくても、彼女(かのじょ)は毎週(まいしゅう)友(とも)だちに手紙(てがみ)を送(おく)り続(つづ)けました。
Even though no reply ever came, she went on sending her friend a letter every week.

～続ける · Nuance & register

The suffix is register-neutral, but it is rarely emotionally flat. Because it insists that something did not stop, it tends to pick up either admiration or exasperation from the surrounding sentence. 夢(ゆめ)を追(お)い続(つづ)ける is a compliment; 文句(もんく)を言(い)い続(つづ)ける is not. That is context, not grammar - but it is why the word is so common in speeches and so common in complaints.

The form you will use most is ～続(つづ)けている, which is how Japanese says "have been doing X for N years": 二十年(にじゅうねん)住(す)み続(つづ)けています. The plain past ～続(つづ)けた closes the stretch off, so it belongs to stories about finished periods.

～続ける · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two neighbours worth knowing. 続(つづ)けて works as a bare adverb meaning "in a row" or "carry on": 三日(みっか)続(つづ)けて雨(あめ)が降(ふ)った, どうぞ続(つづ)けてください. And with a する-noun, spoken Japanese often prefers the independent verb - 練習(れんしゅう)を続(つづ)ける rather than 練習(れんしゅう)し続(つづ)ける - especially when the activity has a name and a shape.

～続ける · ～続(つづ)ける vs ～続(つづ)く

Rule of thumb - if a person is keeping it going, 続(つづ)ける. If it simply goes on, 続(つづ)く. The particles follow: を with the first, が with the second.

雨(あめ)が三日(みっか)も降(ふ)り続(つづ)いている。
It's been raining for three days straight.

息子(むすこ)は夜中(よなか)までゲームをし続(つづ)けていた。
My son carried on playing games until the middle of the night.

With weather and other things nobody controls, both suffixes are heard, and 降(ふ)り続(つづ)く is the more usual - it is what a forecast says. With a human agent only 続(つづ)ける is possible; there is no ❌話(はな)し続(つづ)く.

The independent verbs split the same way, and this is the pair to memorise:

～続ける · ～続(つづ)ける vs ～続(つづ)く (cont.)

会議(かいぎ)を続(つづ)けましょう。
Let's carry on with the meeting.

会議(かいぎ)は六時(ろくじ)まで続(つづ)きました。
The meeting went on until six.

～続ける · ～続(つづ)ける vs ～ている

Rule of thumb - ている says the action is under way; 続(つづ)ける says it has not stopped. The first is a snapshot, the second is a claim about the whole stretch.

今(いま)、レポートを書(か)いています。
I'm writing the report right now.

朝(あさ)から同(おな)じレポートを書(か)き続(つづ)けています。
I've been writing the same report since this morning.

Both are true of the same moment, and only the second complains. Note also what ている can do that this suffix cannot: it covers habits (毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)っています) and resulting states (結婚(けっこん)しています). ❌結婚(けっこん)し続(つづ)けています is not a way to say you are married - it would mean you keep getting married.

～続ける · ～続(つづ)ける vs ずっと

Rule of thumb - ずっと is an adverb you drop into any sentence; 続(つづ)ける is a suffix that changes the verb. They stack happily, and ずっと reaches places the suffix cannot.

子(こ)どものころからずっとサッカーが好(す)きです。
I've loved football ever since I was a child.

好(す)き is not a verb, so there is no stem for 続(つづ)ける to attach to; ずっと has no such problem and works with adjectives, nouns and states alike. It also does duty as "a long way back" - ずっと前(まえ)に会(あ)いました - which has nothing to do with continuing at all.

Where both are available, ずっと reports the span while 続(つづ)ける insists on the unbrokenness, and putting them together is completely normal: ずっと待(ま)ち続(つづ)けていました.

～続ける · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 本(ほん)を読(よ)む続(つづ)ける → ✅ 本(ほん)を読(よ)み続(つづ)ける
    The suffix takes the ます-stem, never the dictionary form.

  • ❌ 会議(かいぎ)を続(つづ)きます。 → ✅ 会議(かいぎ)を続(つづ)けます。／会議(かいぎ)が続(つづ)きます。
    続(つづ)く is intransitive and cannot take a を object. Pick the verb that matches the particle.

  • ❌ 夜中(よなか)まで勉強(べんきょう)続(つづ)けました。 → ✅ 夜中(よなか)まで勉強(べんきょう)し続(つづ)けました。
    A する-noun needs the し to make the compound - or use the independent verb with を: 勉強(べんきょう)を続(つづ)けました.

  • ❌ この町(まち)に十年(じゅうねん)住(す)み続(つづ)けます。（meaning "I've lived here ten years"） → ✅ この町(まち)に十年(じゅうねん)住(す)み続(つづ)けています。
    A stretch that reaches the present needs ている. The plain 続(つづ)けます is a promise about the future.

～続ける · Practice 1

1. 三時間(さんじかん)以上(いじょう)歩(ある)き____て、足(あし)が痛(いた)くなりました。

三時間(さんじかん)以上(いじょう)歩(ある)き続(つづ)けて、足(あし)が痛(いた)くなりました。

The walking went on without a break for over three hours, which is exactly what this suffix claims. 歩(ある)いて alone would report the walking without saying it never stopped, and the sore feet would lose their explanation.

～続ける · Practice 2

2. すみません、話(はなし)を（続(つづ)けて・続(つづ)いて）ください。

話(はなし)を続(つづ)けてください。

You are asking a person to keep going, and the を marks what they are keeping going - both point to the transitive verb. 続(つづ)く would need 話(はなし)が続(つづ)く, "the conversation carries on by itself", which nobody can be asked to do.

～続ける · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "She has been teaching at the same school for twenty years."

彼女(かのじょ)は二十年(にじゅうねん)同(おな)じ学校(がっこう)で教(おし)え続(つづ)けています。

For a stretch that runs up to the present, the suffix takes ている. The plain past 教(おし)え続(つづ)けました would close the period off and imply she has stopped.

～続ける · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 去年(きょねん)から運動(うんどう)続(つづ)けていますが、なかなか痩(や)せません。

✅ 去年(きょねん)から運動(うんどう)を続(つづ)けています…（or 運動(うんどう)し続(つづ)けています）

A する-noun cannot sit bare against the suffix. Either insert を and use 続(つづ)ける as an independent verb, or insert し and build the compound. Both are natural; the を version is the more common in speech.

～続ける · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 一時間(いちじかん)待(ま)っていました。 (b) 一時間(いちじかん)待(ま)ち続(つづ)けました。

(a) simply reports that waiting was what you were doing for an hour. It is neutral, and it would be a perfectly ordinary answer to "what were you doing?"

(b) puts weight on the unbrokenness: you stayed there, you did not give up, and the hour felt like an hour. It invites sympathy or an apology, which is why it is so often what you say to the person who was late.

～終わる

Meaning · finish doing something; get to the end of it

終(お)わる on a verb stem closes the bracket that ～始(はじ)める opened. It says the action ran its course and reached its natural end - the book was read to the last page, the meal was eaten, the letter was written and signed.

～終わる · Formation

Attach 終(お)わる to the ます-stem. The compound is a う-verb.

～終わる · Examples (cont.)

試験(しけん)の問題(もんだい)を全部(ぜんぶ)解(と)き終(お)わったのに、名前(なまえ)を書(か)くのを忘(わす)れてしまいました。
I got through every question on the exam and then forgot to write my name.

When we've finished putting everything away, shall we get a coffee somewhere nearby?

～終わる · Nuance & register

The suffix is register-neutral and enormously frequent, above all in the form ～終(お)わったら, which is how Japanese sequences the tasks of an ordinary day.

Its close relative ～終(お)える means the same thing with a different feel. 終(お)える is genuinely transitive and sounds more deliberate and slightly more written: 読(よ)み終(お)える suggests you set out to get through the thing and did. 読(よ)み終(お)わる just notes that the reading came to an end. In conversation 終(お)わる is far more common; in a report or an essay 終(お)える often reads better.

～終わる · Nuance & register (cont.)

Around the independent verbs the picture is worth keeping straight. 終(お)わる is basically intransitive - 会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わる - and its everyday transitive partners are 終(お)わらせる ("get it finished") and 終(お)える ("bring it to a close"). There is one place where 終(お)わる does take を, and you will hear it constantly: the fixed announcement これで授業(じゅぎょう)を終(お)わります, 本日(ほんじつ)はこれで終(お)わります. Careful writers prefer 終(お)わりにします or 終了(しゅうりょう)します, but the を version is standard spoken Japanese in that formula.

～終わる · ～終(お)わる vs ～終(お)える

Rule of thumb - same meaning, different temperature. 終(お)わる is the neutral everyday form; 終(お)える is transitive, deliberate, and at home in writing.

レポートを書(か)き終(お)わりました。
I've finished writing the report.

レポートを書(か)き終(お)えました。
I have completed the report.

The second sounds like a person reporting an achievement, and it would be the better choice in an email to a client. Between friends it is faintly formal.

The one thing 終(お)える cannot do is describe something ending by itself: ❌授業(じゅぎょう)が終(お)える is impossible, because 終(お)える always needs someone bringing the thing to an end.

～終わる · ～終(お)わる vs ～てしまう

Rule of thumb - 終(お)わる is about reaching the end of a process. てしまう is about completion with an attitude - either "the whole lot" or "and I wish I hadn't".

ケーキを食(た)べ終(お)わってから、コーヒーを入(い)れた。
I made coffee after I'd finished the cake.

一人(ひとり)でケーキを食(た)べてしまった。
I went and ate the whole cake on my own.

The first is neutral sequencing. The second reports a completed act with a feeling attached - pride, or more often regret. てしまう also attaches happily to verbs with no duration at all (忘(わす)れてしまった, 落(お)としてしまった), which is exactly where 終(お)わる is impossible.

～終わる · ～終(お)わる vs 終(お)わる・終(お)わらせる

Rule of thumb - as a suffix it rides on another verb's stem. As an independent verb it needs a noun and が; to say that you finished something, the verb is 終(お)わらせる or 終(お)える.

授業(じゅぎょう)は三時(さんじ)に終(お)わります。
Class finishes at three.

今日(きょう)中(じゅう)にこの仕事(しごと)を終(お)わらせます。
I'll get this job finished before the day is out.

The first has no agent - three o'clock arrives and the class is over. The second has you in it, pushing. That is why a manager says 終(お)わらせてください and never ❌終(お)わってください when they mean "get it done"; 終(お)わってください would be asking the task to end of its own accord.

～終わる · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 本(ほん)を読(よ)む終(お)わる → ✅ 本(ほん)を読(よ)み終(お)わる
    The suffix goes on the ます-stem, like 始(はじ)める and 続(つづ)ける.

  • ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り終(お)わった。 → ✅ 雨(あめ)がやんだ。
    Rain does not complete anything; it stops. Uncontrolled processes with no finish line take やむ, 止(と)まる or 終(お)わる as an independent verb.

  • ❌ 掃除(そうじ)終(お)わりました。 → ✅ 掃除(そうじ)し終(お)わりました。／掃除(そうじ)が終(お)わりました。
    A する-noun needs either し to build the compound, or が in front of the independent verb.

  • ❌ 駅(えき)に着(つ)き終(お)わった。 → ✅ 駅(えき)に着(つ)いた。
    Arriving takes no time, so there is no process to finish. The plain past says everything.

Writing a report is a process with a clear end, so the suffix fits, and ～終(お)わったら is the standard way to sequence "once you've finished X, do Y". 書(か)いたら alone would not insist that the report is complete.

～ているところ

Meaning · right in the middle of doing it - at this exact stage

ところ is the ordinary noun for "place". Put after ている, the place it names is on a timeline rather than a map: the point you have reached in an action that is under way. N5 gave you ている for "is doing"; ているところ narrows that to this stage, right now, neither before nor after.

～ているところ · Examples

We're looking into that at the moment - please bear with us a little longer.

A customer turned up right as I was in the middle of a phone call.

My little brother got caught by our mother in the act of eating the cake.

～ているところ · Examples (cont.)

I was in the middle of tidying the room, so it's still a mess.

I got to the station a moment ago and I'm looking for a taxi now.

The manager was in the middle of explaining the new plan, so I saved my question for later.

～ているところ · Nuance & register

ているところです is the standard polite way to report status, and this is the single most useful thing to take from the page. On the phone, in an email, at a counter, 今(いま)確認(かくにん)しているところです ("I'm checking on that now") says far more than まだです: it tells the other person the work is actually under way, and it buys time without sounding evasive. Business Japanese leans on it heavily.

In casual speech the whole thing contracts. ている becomes てる and ところ becomes とこ: 今(いま)出(で)てるとこ, 向(む)かってるとこだよ. The だ usually disappears too. This is extremely common between friends and completely out of place in writing.

～ているところ · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two constructions grow out of the same phrase. With に or へ, ところ sets up an interruption - something arrives at the stage you were at: 出(で)かけようとしているところに電話(でんわ)が鳴(な)った. With を, it sets up being caught, usually by a verb of seeing or stopping: サボっているところを見(み)つかった. Both are worth recognising even before you produce them.

～ているところ · ～ているところ vs ～ている

Rule of thumb - ている is broad; ているところ is narrow. Adding ところ throws away the habit reading and the resulting-state reading and keeps only "in progress at this moment".

毎朝(まいあさ)六時(ろくじ)に走(はし)っています。
I run at six every morning.

今(いま)走(はし)っているところなので、あとでかけ直(なお)します。
I'm out running at the moment, so I'll call you back.

The first is a habit, and ❌毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)っているところです is impossible - a habit is not a stage you are standing in. The same goes for states: 窓(まど)が開(あ)いています ("the window is open") describes a result, and ❌開(あ)いているところです would be describing a window in the middle of opening itself.

～ているところ · ～ているところ vs ～ている (cont.)

Where both are possible, ところ is the emphatic one. It answers "have you done it yet?" with "I am doing it this second", and it is much harder to hear as an excuse.

食(た)べるところ vs 食(た)べているところ vs 食(た)べたところ

Rule of thumb - one noun, three stages. Dictionary form = about to. ている = in the middle of. た-form = just finished.

これから昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べるところです。
I'm just about to have lunch.

今(いま)昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べているところです。
I'm having lunch right now.

今(いま)昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べたところです。
I've just finished lunch.

食(た)べるところ vs 食(た)べているところ vs 食(た)べたところ (cont.)

Nothing changes but the form of the verb, and the three sentences point at three different minutes. Learn them as a set: the same ところ is doing the same job in all three, and only the tense of the verb moves you along the timeline.

～ているところ · ～ているところ vs ～ている間(あいだ)に

Rule of thumb - ているところ describes the subject's own stage, and usually ends the sentence. 間(あいだ) opens a window during which something else happens - and the に decides whether the other event fills the window or falls inside it.

The に is the entire difference, and it is worth slowing down for:

母(はは)が寝(ね)ている間(あいだ)、ずっとそばにいました。
I stayed beside my mother the whole time she was asleep.

母(はは)が寝(ね)ている間(あいだ)に、買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)ってきました。
While my mother was asleep, I nipped out and did the shopping.

～ているところ · ～ているところ vs ～ている間(あいだ)に (cont.)

Without に, the second clause must last as long as the window - hence ずっと, and hence verbs like 待(ま)っていた, 読(よ)んでいた. With に, the second clause is a one-off event that fits somewhere inside the window, and it is typically something finishable: going out, making a call, tidying up. Swap them and both sentences go wrong - ❌寝(ね)ている間(あいだ)、買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)った claims you shopped for the entire nap.

ているところ is doing something different again. It is not a time clause at all; it is a statement about where the subject has got to:

母(はは)は今(いま)寝(ね)ているところです。
My mother is asleep just at the moment.

～ているところ · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 毎日(まいにち)運動(うんどう)しているところです。 → ✅ 毎日(まいにち)運動(うんどう)しています。
    A habit has no "stage". ところ only works for something happening at the moment of speaking.

  • ❌ 窓(まど)が開(あ)いているところです。 → ✅ 窓(まど)が開(あ)いています。
    This ている is a resulting state, not an action in progress, and ところ cannot attach to it.

  • ❌ 今(いま)食(た)べるているところです。 → ✅ 今(いま)食(た)べているところです。
    The ている is built on the て-form, not the dictionary form. The dictionary form goes straight onto ところ and means "about to".

  • ❌ 父(ちち)は十年(じゅうねん)前(まえ)から同(おな)じ会社(かいしゃ)で働(はたら)いているところです。 → ✅ …働(はたら)いています。
    Ten years is not a stage you are standing in. Long-running situations take plain ている.

～ているところ · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 姉(あね)が料理(りょうり)をしている間(あいだ)、私(わたし)は皿(さら)を洗(あら)っていました。 (b) 姉(あね)が料理(りょうり)をしている間(あいだ)に、私(わたし)は買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)きました。

(a) has no に, so the two activities are the same length: for the whole time your sister was cooking, you were at the sink. Both clauses describe stretches, and neither has a single point attached to it.

(b) has に, which turns the cooking into a window and puts a one-off event inside it. The shopping trip started and finished somewhere in the middle of the cooking; it did not last the whole time and nobody claims it did.

That one syllable is the whole distinction. A verb that goes on (洗(あら)っていた, 待(ま)っていた) wants 間(あいだ); a verb that finishes (行(い)った, 電話(でんわ)した) wants 間(あいだ)に.

～ところ

Meaning · about to do; on the point of doing

With a verb in the dictionary form, ところ names the stage just before the action: you have not started, and you are about to. The same construction in the past - ～ところだった - becomes one of the most useful sentences in the language: "I very nearly did."

❌ Distant future ❌来月(らいげつ)行(い)くところです ✅来月(らいげつ)行(い)く予定(よてい)です

Rule of thumb - the dictionary form in front of ところ means the action has not begun. Not "I am doing it", not "I have done it" - the foot is still in the air.

～ところ · Formation (cont.)

Note - in this sense ところ normally ends the sentence with だ／です, or carries on with に／へ to introduce an interruption. It is not a free subordinate clause: to say "the phone rang as I was about to go to bed" you need 寝(ね)るところに, not a bare 寝(ね)るところ followed by a comma.

～ところ · Examples

今(いま)から出(で)かけるところです。
I'm just heading out now.

これから会議(かいぎ)が始(はじ)まるところです。
The meeting is about to start.

あぶない! もう少(すこ)しで転(ころ)ぶところだった。
Careful! I nearly went over.

お風呂(ふろ)に入(はい)るところだったので、電話(でんわ)に出(で)られませんでした。
I was about to get in the bath, so I couldn't pick up.

寝(ね)るところに友(とも)だちから電話(でんわ)がかかってきた。
Just as I was going to bed, a friend rang.

～ところ · Examples (cont.)

危(あや)うく終電(しゅうでん)を逃(のが)すところでした。
I very nearly missed the last train.

ちょうど家(いえ)を出(で)るところなので、あと十分(じゅっぷん)くらいで着(つ)くと思(おも)います。
I'm walking out the door right now, so I should be there in about ten minutes.

説明(せつめい)を聞(き)かずに帰(かえ)るところでしたが、同僚(どうりょう)が教(おし)えてくれて助(たす)かりました。
I was about to go home without hearing the explanation, but a colleague filled me in - a lucky escape.

～ところ · Nuance & register

In its present-tense use, ところです is a status report about the very near future, and it is the natural answer to 今(いま)何(なに)してる? or もう出(で)た?. In casual speech it contracts to とこ and drops the copula: 今(いま)出(で)るとこ. Both registers are extremely common; what you will not hear is this pattern in a formal written notice, where a schedule would be stated with times instead.

The past form is where the real value is. ～ところだった means it nearly happened and it didn't, and it almost always carries relief. It collects a fixed set of adverbs - もう少(すこ)しで, 危(あや)うく, あぶなく - and it is how Japanese says all of these:

  • 忘(わす)れるところでした - "I almost forgot."
  • 遅刻(ちこく)するところだった - "I nearly ended up late."
  • 買(か)ってしまうところだった - "I almost bought it."

～ところ · Nuance & register (cont.)

Note that the verb stays in the dictionary form even though the sentence is about the past. The past lives in だった／でした at the end; the verb has to stay un-happened, because the whole point is that it never happened.

One thing this pattern does not do is announce plans. It has a horizon of a few minutes. For anything further out, the words are つもり and 予定(よてい).

～ところ · 出(で)るところ vs 出(で)たところ

Rule of thumb - the form of the verb walks you across the event. Dictionary form: not started. た-form: just finished. The ところ is identical in both.

今(いま)、家(いえ)を出(で)るところです。
I'm just about to leave the house.

今(いま)、家(いえ)を出(で)たところです。
I've just left the house.

Said into a phone, these two put you in completely different places: still indoors, or already on the street. The middle member of the set - 出(で)ているところ - is built on ている and covers the stretch in between.

～ところ · 出(で)るところ vs 出(で)たところ (cont.)

This is why the form in front of ところ deserves more care than any other choice on this page. The word never changes; the verb carries all the information.

～ところ · ～ところだ vs ～つもり・～予定(よてい)

Rule of thumb - ところ reaches a few minutes into the future. つもり is an intention and 予定(よてい) is a schedule, and both stretch as far as you like.

来月(らいげつ)、京都(きょうと)へ行(い)くつもりです。
I'm planning to go to Kyoto next month.

今(いま)駅(えき)へ行(い)くところです。
I'm on my way to the station this minute.

❌来月(らいげつ)京都(きょうと)へ行(い)くところです is wrong, and audibly so: ところ claims you are on the very brink of the action, and you cannot be on the brink of something a month away.

～ところ · ～ところだ vs ～つもり・～予定(よてい) (cont.)

The reverse substitution is not an error so much as a loss. 今(いま)行(い)くつもりです reports your intention and leaves open whether you have moved; 今(いま)行(い)くところです says you are already on your feet.

～ところ · ～ところだった vs ～そうになった

Rule of thumb - both mean "almost". ところだった is a whole situation you narrowly avoided; そうになった is a physical event that nearly happened to you.

もう少(すこ)しで遅刻(ちこく)するところでした。
I very nearly ended up late.

階段(かいだん)で転(ころ)びそうになりました。
I almost fell on the stairs.

The mechanics differ too: そうになる attaches to the ます-stem (転(ころ)び, 落(お)ち, 泣(な)き), while ところ takes the dictionary form. And their coverage differs. そうになる wants something on the verge of physically occurring - falling, dropping, crying, crashing. ところだった is happy with outcomes you avoided by deciding differently: 危(あや)うく高(たか)いほうを買(か)うところだった, "I almost bought the expensive one." そうになる cannot say that.

～ところ · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 今(いま)出(で)かけますところです。 → ✅ 今(いま)出(で)かけるところです。
    ところ is a noun, and a noun takes the plain form in front of it. The politeness is already there in です.

  • ❌ 来年(らいねん)、アメリカに留学(りゅうがく)するところです。 → ✅ 来年(らいねん)、アメリカに留学(りゅうがく)する**予定(よてい)**です。
    A year is far outside the range of this pattern. Distant plans take 予定(よてい) or つもり.

  • ❌ もう少(すこ)しで財布(さいふ)を忘(わす)れたところでした。 → ✅ もう少(すこ)しで財布(さいふ)を忘(わす)れるところでした。
    The verb stays in the dictionary form: the forgetting never happened. Only でした moves into the past.

  • ❌ 今(いま)食(た)べているところだったので、後(あと)で行(い)きます。（meaning "I was about to eat"） → ✅ 今(いま)食(た)べるところだったので…
    ている puts you in the middle of the meal. If the food is untouched, the dictionary form is the one you want.

もう少(すこ)しで is the giveaway: it introduces something that came close to happening and then did not, and the pattern for that is ～るところだった. Note that the verb stays in the dictionary form because the collision never took place.

The call had not been made, so the verb is in the dictionary form, and the past sits in でした. This is a very common thing to say when someone contacts you first, and it is friendlier than 電話(でんわ)しようと思(おも)っていました because it claims you were already in motion.

～ところ · Practice 4

✅ 来月(らいげつ)、東京(とうきょう)に引(ひ)っ越(こ)す予定(よてい)です。（or 引(ひ)っ越(こ)すつもりです）

ところ means you are on the brink of the action, within minutes. A move next month is a plan, so it needs 予定(よてい) for a fixed arrangement or つもり for an intention.

～ところ · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 財布(さいふ)を忘(わす)れるところでした。 (b) 財布(さいふ)を忘(わす)れてしまいました。

(a) is a near miss with a happy ending: the wallet is in your pocket. You noticed in time, and the sentence is coloured by relief - this is what you say as you turn back from the door with it in your hand.

(b) is the disaster itself. The wallet is on the kitchen table and you are on the train. てしまう reports a completed action you regret, where ところでした reports one that never happened at all.

～たところ

Meaning · have just done it - standing at the moment immediately after

With the verb in the た-form, ところ names the stage right after the action finished. The emphasis is on right: this is a claim about the clock, not about how fresh the event feels. Minutes ago, not last month.

Rule of thumb - たところ pins you to the moment immediately after the action. If you can put たった今(いま) in front of the sentence, it fits. If you would have to put 先月(せんげつ) there, it does not.

～たところ · Formation (cont.)

Note - ～たところ has a second, unrelated life as a connective, and you will meet it in reading before you produce it: 店(みせ)に電話(でんわ)したところ、もう閉(し)まっていました - "when I rang the shop, it turned out to be closed." That version has no だ, sits at a comma, and belongs to formal writing. The last contrast section below separates the two.

～たところ · Examples

たった今(いま)うちに帰(かえ)ったところです。
I've literally just got home.

今(いま)起(お)きたところなので、まだ顔(かお)も洗(あら)っていません。
I've only just woken up - I haven't even washed my face yet.

ちょうど会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わったところです。
The meeting has just this minute finished.

メールは今(いま)送(おく)ったところですので、確認(かくにん)してみてください。
I've just sent the email, so please check for it.

駅(えき)を出(で)たところで、高校(こうこう)の友(とも)だちにばったり会(あ)った。
I'd just come out of the station when I ran straight into an old school friend.

～たところ · Examples (cont.)

お湯(ゆ)がわいたところだから、お茶(ちゃ)を入(い)れましょうか。
The water's just boiled - shall I make some tea?

引(ひ)っ越(こ)しの荷物(にもつ)を運(はこ)び終(お)わったところに、大家(おおや)さんが来(き)ました。
The landlord turned up just as we'd finished carrying in the boxes.

さっき銀行(ぎんこう)から戻(もど)って、今(いま)やっと書類(しょるい)を全部(ぜんぶ)出(だ)したところです。
I got back from the bank a while ago, and I've only just now handed in all the paperwork.

～たところ · Nuance & register

たところです is the polite way to report that something is done this instant, and it is a workplace staple. Asked whether you have sent the file, 今(いま)送(おく)ったところです is better than 送(おく)りました because it tells the other person to look now. In casual speech it shortens to たとこ: ごめん、今(いま)起(お)きたとこ.

The construction is objective. It is measured against the clock, and it will not stretch. That is its whole personality, and it is exactly what separates it from たばかり, which is measured against how the speaker feels. Because of that, たところ has no emotional colour of its own: it neither excuses nor boasts, it just reports where on the timeline you are standing.

～たところ · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two structural limits follow from the same fact. たところ cannot modify a noun - there is no ❌作(つく)ったところの料理(りょうり) - because it describes a moment rather than a property of the thing. And it has no negative form: "I haven't eaten yet" is まだ食(た)べていません, never ❌食(た)べなかったところです.

For narration in the past, use たところだった: 部屋(へや)に入(はい)ったとき、彼(かれ)は電話(でんわ)を切(き)ったところだった.

～たところ · ～たところ vs ～たばかり

Rule of thumb - たところ measures with a clock; たばかり measures with a feeling. Both translate as "just", and only one of them will stretch to last year.

今(いま)駅(えき)に着(つ)いたところです。
I've just arrived at the station.

去年(きょねん)日本(にほん)に来(き)たばかりです。
I only came to Japan last year.

The second sentence is impossible with ところ: ❌去年(きょねん)来(き)たところです. A year is not a moment. ばかり survives it because the speaker is not reporting the clock - they are saying the arrival is still recent as far as they are concerned, which is why it works as an excuse for not knowing their way around.

～たところ · ～たところ vs ～たばかり (cont.)

The second difference is structural and easy to test. ばかり modifies nouns freely; ところ does not:

買(か)ったばかりのパソコンが壊(こわ)れてしまった。
The computer I'd only just bought has broken.

❌買(か)ったところのパソコン is not Japanese. If a noun follows, ばかり is your only option.

～たところ · ～たところ vs ～たあとで

Rule of thumb - たところ is a statement about now and ends the sentence. ～たあとで is a connector and always has another clause after it.

仕事(しごと)が終(お)わったところです。
I've just finished work.

仕事(しごと)が終(お)わったあとで、飲(の)みに行(い)きます。
After work I'm going out for a drink.

The first sentence is complete; the second would be unfinished without its second half. And they carry different information: あとで says nothing at all about how recent the event is, while たところ says nothing about what happens next.

～たところ · ～たところ vs ～たあとで (cont.)

When you do want to hang a following clause on たところ, it needs a particle - に, へ or で - and the meaning becomes "just as I had done X, Y happened": 席(せき)に座(すわ)ったところに先生(せんせい)が入(はい)ってきた.

～たところ · ～たところだ vs ～たところ、…

Rule of thumb - with だ／です it means "just finished". Standing at a comma with no copula, it is a formal connective meaning "when I did X, it turned out that Y".

課長(かちょう)に聞(き)いたところです。
I've just this minute asked the section chief.

課長(かちょう)に聞(き)いたところ、会議(かいぎ)は中止(ちゅうし)になったそうです。
I asked the section chief, and apparently the meeting has been cancelled.

The connective version has three tells: no だ, a comma, and a second clause that reports something discovered as a result. It belongs to written and formal Japanese - reports, business email, news - and in speech people say 聞(き)いてみたら instead. You need to recognise it so that a sentence like the second one does not read to you as "I have just asked."

～たところ · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 去年(きょねん)この会社(かいしゃ)に入(はい)ったところです。 → ✅ 去年(きょねん)この会社(かいしゃ)に入(はい)ったばかりです。
    A year ago is not "just now" on any clock. Subjective recency is ばかり's job.

  • ❌ これは先週(せんしゅう)買(か)ったところの自転車(じてんしゃ)です。 → ✅ これは先週(せんしゅう)買(か)ったばかりの自転車(じてんしゃ)です。
    ところ cannot modify a noun. When a noun follows, only ばかり works.

  • ❌ 今(いま)昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べるところです。（meaning "I've just eaten"） → ✅ 今(いま)昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べたところです。
    The dictionary form means you have not started. One character decides whether the food is gone.

  • ❌ まだ返事(へんじ)が来(こ)なかったところです。 → ✅ まだ返事(へんじ)が来(き)ていません。
    There is no negative version of this pattern. "Not yet" is まだ～ていません.

Two problems, one fix. ところ cannot sit in front of a noun, and last month is too far back for it anyway. ばかり handles both: it takes の before a noun and it stretches to cover anything the speaker still thinks of as recent.

～たところ · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 日本(にほん)に来(き)たところです。 (b) 日本(にほん)に来(き)たばかりです。

(a) means you have this moment arrived - you are at the airport, or you have just walked out of it, and the suitcase is still in your hand. It is a statement about the clock and it will not stretch past a few minutes.

(b) means you are new here. It might be a week, it might be six months; what it reports is that the arrival is still recent from where you stand. This is the one you use to explain why your Japanese is shaky or why you got lost, and (a) would be strange in that role.

～たばかり

Meaning · only just did it - recent as far as the speaker is concerned

たばかり also translates as "just", but it is measuring something quite different from たところ. ところ reads the clock. ばかり reads the speaker: it says the event is still recent to them, and that judgement stretches. Five minutes, five weeks, sometimes five years - as long as the speaker still feels new to it.

Rule of thumb - recency here is relative to the thing. A bus that left five minutes ago, a job started last month and a building put up five years ago can all take ばかり, because "new" means something different for each. That flexibility is exactly what たところ refuses.

Note - the noun-modifying form is the practical reason to learn this pattern. 生(う)まれたばかりの赤(あか)ちゃん, 習(なら)ったばかりの漢字(かんじ), 買(か)ったばかりの靴(くつ) - all completely ordinary, and none of them can be built with ところ. Do not drop the の.

～たばかり · Examples

さっき昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べたばかりです。
I only had lunch a little while ago.

この会社(かいしゃ)に入(はい)ったばかりで、まだ何(なに)もわかりません。
I've only just joined this company, so I don't know anything yet.

焼(や)いたばかりのパンはやっぱりおいしいね。
Bread straight out of the oven really is good, isn't it.

生(う)まれたばかりの赤(あか)ちゃんは、一日(いちにち)中(じゅう)寝(ね)ています。
A newborn baby sleeps all day long.

習(なら)ったばかりの漢字(かんじ)を、もう忘(わす)れてしまいました。
I've already forgotten the kanji I only just learned.

～たばかり · Examples (cont.)

去年(きょねん)買(か)ったばかりのパソコンなのに、もう動(うご)かなくなった。
I only bought this computer last year and it's already stopped working.

この町(まち)に引(ひ)っ越(こ)してきたばかりなので、この辺(へん)のことはまだよく知(し)らないんです。
I've only just moved to this town, so I don't really know the area yet.

日本(にほん)に来(き)たばかりのころは、コンビニで買(か)い物(もの)をするだけでも緊張(きんちょう)しました。
When I'd only just arrived in Japan, even shopping in a convenience store made me nervous.

～たばかり · Nuance & register

たばかり is register-neutral, and its most characteristic job is explaining yourself. ～たばかりなので is how Japanese excuses inexperience, disorder or ignorance without apologising outright: 始(はじ)めたばかりなので、まだ下手(へた)です, 引(ひ)っ越(こ)したばかりで、部屋(へや)が片付(かたづ)いていません. It buys the speaker some room, and because the recency is subjective, nobody argues with it.

In casual speech it lengthens rather than shortens: ばっかり and ばっか are both extremely common - 今(いま)起(お)きたばっかりだよ - and both are firmly spoken-only.

～たばかり · Nuance & register (cont.)

Be aware that the same word ばかり has a much more frequent meaning, "only, nothing but": 肉(にく)ばかり食(た)べる, 文句(もんく)ばかり言(い)う, 寝(ね)てばかりいる. The two are told apart by what comes in front. A た-form gives you "just did"; a noun or a て-form gives you "nothing but". Getting this wrong produces a sentence that means the opposite of what you intended, so it is worth a moment's care.

～たばかり · ～たばかり vs ～たところ

Rule of thumb - ところ is measured on the clock; ばかり is measured by the speaker. In the few minutes after an event both are available; outside that window only ばかり survives.

入学(にゅうがく)したばかりなので、友(とも)だちがまだ少(すく)ないです。
I've only just started at the school, so I don't have many friends yet.

会議(かいぎ)は今(いま)終(お)わったところです。
The meeting finished just now.

Try swapping them. ❌入学(にゅうがく)したところなので would claim you enrolled minutes ago, which makes nonsense of the excuse. And 終(お)わったばかりです, while perfectly grammatical, shifts the emphasis from the timetable to how it feels - closer to "it's barely over" than to "it finished at 3.02".

～たばかり · ～たばかり vs ～たところ (cont.)

The second difference is the one that decides most sentences in practice: only ばかり can sit in front of a noun. 届(とど)いたばかりの荷物(にもつ) is normal; ❌届(とど)いたところの荷物(にもつ) does not exist.

～たばかり · ～たばかり vs ばかり ("only, nothing but")

Rule of thumb - look left. た-form + ばかり = "just did". Noun + ばかり, or て-form + ばかり + いる = "nothing but".

弟(おとうと)は新(あたら)しいゲームを買(か)ったばかりだ。
My little brother has only just bought the new game.

弟(おとうと)はゲームばかりしている。
My little brother does nothing but play games.

Same four syllables, and the two sentences do not even share a topic: the first is about when, the second about proportion. The ている version is the one to watch, because it looks so close to the pattern on this page: 泣(な)いてばかりいる means "does nothing but cry", while 泣(な)いたばかりだ would mean "has only just cried".

～たばかり · ～たばかり vs 最近(さいきん)

Rule of thumb - 最近(さいきん) is an adverb about a period; たばかり attaches to a single completed event. They combine happily, but they are not substitutes.

最近(さいきん)、よく映画(えいが)を見(み)に行(い)きます。
I've been going to the cinema a lot lately.

この店(みせ)は先月(せんげつ)開(ひら)いたばかりです。
This shop only opened last month.

最近(さいきん) can cover a habit spread over weeks, which ばかり cannot do at all - there is no single event for it to attach to. Conversely ばかり pins the recency to one thing that happened, which is why it, and not 最近(さいきん), can go in front of a noun.

～たばかり · ～たばかり vs 最近(さいきん) (cont.)

Putting them together is completely normal and adds emphasis: 最近(さいきん)始(はじ)めたばかりの趣味(しゅみ)です, "it's a hobby I've only recently taken up."

～たばかり · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 日本(にほん)に来(く)るばかりです。 → ✅ 日本(にほん)に来(き)たばかりです。
    This pattern is built on the た-form. The dictionary form gives you an entirely different, much rarer construction.

  • ❌ 買(か)ったばかり車(くるま)を見(み)せてください。 → ✅ 買(か)ったばかりの車(くるま)を見(み)せてください。
    In front of a noun, ばかり needs の. Leaving it out is the single most common slip with this pattern.

  • ❌ 兄(あに)は寝(ね)たばかりいます。 → ✅ 兄(あに)は寝(ね)てばかりいます。
    "Does nothing but sleep" is built on the て-form plus いる. With the た-form the sentence would mean he had only just fallen asleep.

～たばかり · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 日本(にほん)に来(き)てばかりです。（meaning "I've only just come to Japan"） → ✅ 日本(にほん)に来(き)たばかりです。
    The て-form pushes you into the "nothing but" meaning, which here would be something like "all I do is come to Japan".

～たばかり · Practice 1

1. この町(まち)に引(ひ)っ越(こ)して来(き)____ばかりなので、まだ道(みち)がよくわかりません。

引(ひ)っ越(こ)して来(き)たばかりなので…

The pattern needs the た-form. Note what the sentence is doing: the move might have been a month ago, and ばかり is being used to excuse not knowing the streets yet - a job the clock-bound たところ could not do.

～たばかり · Practice 2

2. これは五年(ごねん)前(まえ)に建(た)った（ところ・ばかり）のビルです。

これは五年(ごねん)前(まえ)に建(た)ったばかりのビルです。

Two reasons, and either one settles it. Five years is far outside ところ's range, and ところ cannot modify a noun in the first place. For a building, five years old genuinely counts as new - which is exactly the kind of judgement ばかり is built to express.

～たばかり · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "These are cakes I've just baked - please help yourself."

焼(や)いたばかりのケーキです。どうぞ召(め)し上(あ)がってください。

A noun follows, so the form must be たばかりの with the の in place. This is the everyday way to say "freshly made", and it is one of the commonest uses of the pattern.

～たばかり · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 息子(むすこ)は勉強(べんきょう)しないで、遊(あそ)んだばかりいます。

✅ 息子(むすこ)は勉強(べんきょう)しないで、遊(あそ)んでばかりいます。

"Does nothing but play" is the other ばかり, and it is built on the て-form plus いる. With the た-form the sentence would say he had only just started playing, which is not a complaint at all.

～たばかり · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)を始(はじ)めたばかりです。 (b) 新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)を始(はじ)めたところです。

(a) says you are new to the job. It could be your third day or your third month; what the sentence claims is that you have not settled in yet, and it usually leads into an explanation - you are still learning, you cannot answer that question without asking someone.

(b) reads the clock. It says the starting happened moments ago, which for a job is odd unless you are describing this morning: "I've just this minute started." Native speakers would understand it as the literal instant, and would probably have expected (a).
