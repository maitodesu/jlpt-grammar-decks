こと - nominaliser

Meaning · turns a verb, an adjective or a whole clause into a noun, so the sentence can talk about it

Japanese has no infinitive and no "-ing" noun. When you need an action to behave like a thing - to be liked, to be important, to be decided at a meeting - you put こと on the end of the clause. What comes out is an ordinary noun and takes が, を, は or です like any other.

こと - nominaliser · Formation

こと attaches to the plain form. Only な-adjectives and nouns change shape first, and they change in the way they always do before a noun.

Rule of thumb - never ❌学生(がくせい)だこと. A noun needs である in front of こと, and in everyday Japanese most speakers avoid the problem altogether by using 学生(がくせい)だということ, which is the clause-packaging pattern treated separately at this level.

Note - こと is also an ordinary noun meaning "thing, matter", and it keeps that job here: 日本(にほん)のことを知(し)りたい ("I want to know about Japan"), 田中(たなか)さんのこと ("the Tanaka business", or simply Tanaka as a topic). The nominaliser is the same word doing the same thing to a clause instead of a noun.

It was settled at yesterday's meeting that the manager is going away on business next week.

こと - nominaliser · Nuance & register

The nominaliser itself carries no politeness. What it carries is a slight lean towards the written and the abstract, which is the one thing that separates it from its rival の. こと names a fact, a rule, a plan, a proposition - something you can think about, decide, promise or announce. の names an event you are watching, hearing or living through. Where both are grammatical, の is the more spoken of the two, and a page of conversation will contain many more of them.

こと never contracts. の shrinks to ん before です, and the packaging form ということ shrinks to ってこと in casual speech, but plain こと stays as it is in every register.

趣味(しゅみ)は…ことです deserves a line of its own because it is the first thing you will need it for. Self-introductions, job interviews and profile forms are full of it, and there の is not an option at all - the reason is in the Contrast section.

こと - nominaliser · Nuance & register (cont.)

One more こと exists and it looks identical on the page. A verb plus a bare こと and a full stop, with nothing after it, is not a noun phrase at all: it is a written instruction, the こと of classroom notices and exam papers, and it is treated as its own point at N3. The test is mechanical: if a particle or です follows the こと, you are looking at the nominaliser on this page. If the sentence stops dead, you are looking at a rule.

こと - nominaliser · こと vs の

Rule of thumb - の for what you see, hear and take part in; こと for facts, plans and propositions. Where either would work, の is the spoken choice - but three situations take the decision out of your hands.

Verbs of direct perception require の. 見(み)る, 聞(き)く, 聞(き)こえる, 見(み)える, 感(かん)じる are watching an event happen, and an event is not a fact.

外(そと)で雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り出(だ)したのに気(き)がつかなかった。
I didn't notice that it had started raining outside.

A nominalised clause standing in front of だ／です requires こと. 趣味(しゅみ)は走(はし)ることです is right; ❌走(はし)るのです is not available, because that string already means something else - the explanatory んです.

こと - nominaliser · こと vs の (cont.)

A set of fixed frames requires こと. ことができる, たことがある, ことにする, ことになる are frozen expressions and admit no substitution: ❌泳(およ)ぐのができる.

Everywhere else both are grammatical. There is one pair worth memorising because the two versions genuinely mean different things:

電話(でんわ)するのを忘(わす)れた。
I forgot to ring.

電話(でんわ)したことを忘(わす)れた。
I forgot that I had rung.

The first is an action that never happened; the second is a fact that slipped your mind. That is the difference between the two words in miniature.

こと - nominaliser · ことができる vs the potential form

Rule of thumb - the same meaning at two lengths. The potential form (話(はな)せる) is the default in speech; ことができる is longer, flatter and more official, and it is what notices and manuals use.

ここで切符(きっぷ)を買(か)うことができます。
Tickets may be purchased here.

ここで切符(きっぷ)が買(か)えます。
You can buy tickets here.

Both are correct and mean the same thing. The こと in ことができる is frozen in place - it cannot become の, and it cannot be dropped - which is a good reminder that こと is not always a free choice.

こと - nominaliser · Nominalising こと vs the instruction こと

Rule of thumb - look at what comes after the こと. A particle or です means it is a noun. A full stop and nothing else means it is a rule.

使(つか)った皿(さら)を洗(あら)うことが、今週(こんしゅう)の係(かかり)の仕事(しごと)です。
Washing the dishes we've used is this week's duty.

使(つか)った皿(さら)は自分(じぶん)で洗(あら)うこと。
Wash your own dishes.

The first sentence talks about the washing-up as a thing; the second orders it. The second has no politeness level and no addressee - it is the voice of a notice on a kitchen wall - and that impersonal instruction こと is a separate grammar point you meet at N3. Nothing may follow it, so the moment you see が, を, は or です after the こと, you are back on this page.

こと - nominaliser · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 彼(かれ)が学生(がくせい)だことを知(し)らなかった。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)が学生(がくせい)だということを知(し)らなかった。
    A plain noun cannot sit in front of こと with だ on it. Everyday Japanese uses だということ; formal writing uses であること.

  • ❌ 部屋(へや)が静(しず)かことが一番(いちばん)大事(だいじ)です。 → ✅ 部屋(へや)が静(しず)かなことが一番(いちばん)大事(だいじ)です。
    な-adjectives keep their な in front of こと, exactly as they do in front of any other noun.

  • ❌ 隣(となり)の人(ひと)がピアノを弾(ひ)いていることが聞(き)こえます。 → ✅ …弾(ひ)いているのが聞(き)こえます。
    You are hearing an event, not a fact. Verbs of direct perception take の and nothing else.

こと - nominaliser · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 私(わたし)の仕事(しごと)は子(こ)どもに英語(えいご)を教(おし)えるのです。 → ✅ …教(おし)えることです。
    In front of です, only こと works. With の the sentence turns into the explanatory んです and stops saying what you meant.

  • ❌ 朝(あさ)早(はや)く起(お)きること苦手(にがて)です。 → ✅ 朝(あさ)早(はや)く起(お)きることが苦手(にがて)です。
    Once the clause has become a noun it needs a particle like any other noun. The nominaliser does not replace が or を; it makes room for one.

こと - nominaliser · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference: (a) 鍵(かぎ)をかけるのを忘(わす)れました。 (b) 鍵(かぎ)をかけたことを忘(わす)れました。

(a) says the locking never happened. の names the act itself, and 忘(わす)れる reports that you failed to carry it out - the door is unlocked.

(b) says the locking did happen, and the memory of it is what went missing. こと names the fact that you locked up, which is why the past form かけた is possible here and impossible in (a). The door is locked; you are just about to go back and check it.

～ということ - packaging a clause as a noun

Meaning · takes a whole sentence and hands it on as a single noun - "the fact that ～", "that ～"

こと on its own nominalises a verb phrase. ということ nominalises a finished statement: the quotative という wraps the clause up with its own ending intact, だ and all, and こと then turns the parcel into a noun the rest of the sentence can take as its subject or object.

～ということ - packaging a clause as a noun · Formation

という attaches to a plain-form clause exactly as it does after 言(い)う and 思(おも)う. Nouns and な-adjectives keep their だ, which is the whole reason the pattern exists.

Rule of thumb - the だ is what tells the two nominalisers apart. Plain こと cannot follow だ (❌中止(ちゅうし)だこと), so a clause ending in a noun or a な-adjective needs ということ.

Note - the same という sits inside a family of expressions built the same way: という話(はなし), という意味(いみ), という理由(りゆう). こと is simply the most general noun you can put there, which is why this one turns up far more often than the others.

～ということ - packaging a clause as a noun · Nuance & register

ということ is register-neutral but noticeably heavy. It is four syllables of scaffolding, and Japanese drops it whenever the sentence can stand without it: 来(こ)ないと聞(き)きました says the same thing as 来(こ)ないということを聞(き)きました in half the space. Reach for the full form when the clause is long, when it is doing something a bare と cannot do (taking が, taking は, sitting in front of です), or when you want the content held up for inspection rather than slipped past.

In casual speech it contracts to ってこと, and this is extremely common: 明日(あした)休(やす)みだってこと、聞(き)いてる? The contraction is conversational only - it would look wrong in a report - but it is what you will actually hear, and learners who only know the long form miss it entirely.

～ということ - packaging a clause as a noun · Nuance & register (cont.)

Because the pattern begins with a quotative, it leans towards content that came from somewhere: something said, written, decided, noticed or worked out. That is not a rule - 暑(あつ)いということ above is nobody's utterance - but it explains why the pattern feels natural after 聞(き)く, 知(し)る, 伝(つた)える, 意味(いみ)する and awkward after a verb of direct perception.

One warning about the shape. Put だ／です on the end of this string and let the sentence stop there, and you have a different pattern altogether: the hearsay ということだ, "I hear that ～", which is a separate point at N3. On this page the ということ is always followed by something - a particle, or a predicate that takes it as an argument. The next section sets the two side by side.

～ということ - packaging a clause as a noun · ということ vs こと

Rule of thumb - こと nominalises an action. ということ nominalises a statement. If the clause ends in a noun or a な-adjective, only ということ is available, because plain こと cannot follow だ.

毎日(まいにち)走(はし)ることは体(からだ)にいいです。
Running every day is good for you.

彼(かれ)が毎日(まいにち)走(はし)っているということは、誰(だれ)も知(し)りませんでした。
Nobody knew that he ran every day.

～ということ - packaging a clause as a noun · ということ vs こと (cont.)

The first names an activity; the second names a piece of information about somebody. Where the clause is a complete statement with a subject in it, the longer form is the natural one. Note the difference this makes with a noun in front: 事故(じこ)のことを聞(き)きました is "I heard about the accident" - a topic - while 事故(じこ)だったということを聞(き)きました is "I heard that it was an accident", a proposition.

ということ vs ということだ

Rule of thumb - look at what comes after it. A particle means it is a noun and the sentence is still going. だ／です closing the sentence means it is a report: "I hear that ～".

工事(こうじ)は来月(らいげつ)終(お)わるということを、係(かかり)の人(ひと)に確認(かくにん)しました。
I checked with the person in charge that the work finishes next month.

工事(こうじ)は来月(らいげつ)終(お)わるということです。
The work finishes next month, I gather.

ということ vs ということだ (cont.)

Same eleven characters, two different grammars. In the first the ということ is the object of 確認(かくにん)する - it is a noun, and it could equally take が or は. In the second nothing follows, です closes the sentence, and the whole frame has become a sentence-final marker that puts the information at arm's length; that hearsay use is treated at N3. The one place the test needs care is a cleft frame - 問題(もんだい)なのは…ということです - where the sentence does end on です but the のは in front makes it plainly the nominaliser again.

ということ vs the bare quotative と

Rule of thumb - と feeds a clause straight into 言(い)う, 思(おも)う, 聞(き)く. ということ makes it a noun first, so it can take a particle or be the subject.

林(はやし)さんは行(い)かないと言(い)っていました。
Hayashi said he wasn't going.

林(はやし)さんが行(い)かないということは、みんなに伝(つた)えました。
I've told everyone that Hayashi isn't going.

With 言(い)う and 思(おも)う, と is shorter and better, and a learner who writes ということ every time sounds laboured. But と cannot be marked with は or が, and it cannot be the subject of an adjective, so the moment the clause needs to move around the sentence, the full nominaliser is the only option.

～ということ - packaging a clause as a noun · Common mistakes

  • ❌（meaning "I heard that it was cancelled"）中止(ちゅうし)のことを聞(き)きました。 → ✅ 中止(ちゅうし)だということを聞(き)きました。
    中止(ちゅうし)のこと is a topic - "about the cancellation". To report the statement itself you need the clause packaged with という.

  • ❌ この町(まち)は静(しず)かということを知(し)りませんでした。 → ✅ この町(まち)は静(しず)かだということを知(し)りませんでした。
    A な-adjective keeps its だ in front of という. This is the opposite of what happens before plain こと, where the same word takes な.

  • ❌ 高(たか)いだということが問題(もんだい)です。 → ✅ 高(たか)いということが問題(もんだい)です。
    い-adjectives never take だ. Only nouns and な-adjectives do.

  • ❌ 彼(かれ)が来(こ)ないということだを知(し)らなかった。 → ✅ 彼(かれ)が来(こ)ないということを知(し)らなかった。
    The だ belongs to the sentence-final hearsay pattern. Inside a sentence, where a particle follows, the noun ends at こと.

  • ❌ 子(こ)どもが泣(な)いているということを見(み)ました。 → ✅ 子(こ)どもが泣(な)いているのを見(み)ました。
    You cannot watch a proposition. Verbs of direct perception take の, never こと and never ということ.

～やすい

Meaning · easy to do - or, with some verbs, liable to happen

～やすい attaches to a verb and produces an い-adjective. What that adjective describes is the thing, not the person: the pen is easy to write with, the white shirt is quick to get dirty. You already met this machinery at N5 with ～すぎる - same ます-stem, same い-adjective coming out the other end.

～やすい · Formation

Cut the verb back to its ます-stem and add やすい. The result conjugates exactly like 高(たか)い.

Attaches to Form Example
Verb (う-verb) ます-stem + やすい 使(つか)う → 使(つか)いやすい
Verb (る-verb) ます-stem + やすい 覚(おぼ)える → 覚(おぼ)えやすい

Verb (来(く)る) 来(き) + やすい 来(く)る → 来(き)やすい

Note - う-verb / る-verb are the labels used throughout this book; you will also meet them as 五段(ごだん)/Group 1 and 一段(いちだん)/Group 2 elsewhere.

～やすい · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - the thing that is easy takes は or が, never を. Adding やすい turns the verb into an adjective, and adjectives do not take objects: この本(ほん)は読(よ)みやすい, not ❌この本(ほん)を読(よ)みやすい.

～やすい · Examples

このペンは書(か)きやすい。
This pen is easy to write with.

ひらがなは覚(おぼ)えやすいです。
Hiragana is easy to learn.

白(しろ)いシャツは汚(よご)れやすい。
White shirts get dirty easily.

先生(せんせい)の説明(せつめい)は分(わ)かりやすかったです。
The teacher's explanation was easy to follow.

疲(つか)れているときは間違(まちが)えやすいから、気(き)をつけてください。
You slip up easily when you're tired, so please be careful.

～やすい · Examples (cont.)

この説明書(せつめいしょ)は字(じ)が小(ちい)さくて、あまり読(よ)みやすくない。
The print in this manual is small, so it isn't very easy to read.

These shoes are light and easy to walk in, so I always wear them when I travel.

春(はる)は天気(てんき)が変(か)わりやすいですから、かさを持(も)って出(で)かけたほうがいいですよ。
The weather turns quickly in spring, so you'd do well to take an umbrella out with you.

～やすい · Nuance & register

The two meanings are not two words. Which one a listener hears is decided by the verb underneath.

  • A verb someone chooses to do - 書(か)く, 使(つか)う,
    歩(ある)く, 飲(の)む - gives ease. The thing has a property that makes the action comfortable.
  • A verb that just happens - 汚(よご)れる, 割(わ)れる,
    忘(わす)れる, 太(ふと)る, 変(か)わる - gives propensity. Nobody is finding anything easy; the event simply occurs readily.

The propensity reading is often, but not always, a complaint. 壊(こわ)れやすい is a defect; 覚(おぼ)えやすい is a compliment. Do not assume やすい is the positive one and にくい the negative one - each takes its colour from the verb.

～やすい is register-neutral. It is equally at home in casual speech （このアプリ、使(つか)いやすいね）, in polite conversation, and in written product copy, where 持(も)ちやすい・洗(あら)いやすい・落(お)ちやすい are everyday advertising vocabulary.

～やすい · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two derived forms are worth having now. やすくなる means "become easier" or "start happening more readily": 漢字(かんじ)を覚(おぼ)えてから、新聞(しんぶん)が読(よ)みやすくなりました。 And やすさ is its noun - the さ nominaliser applied to the whole compound: この車(くるま)は運転(うんてん)のしやすさで人気(にんき)があります。

～やすい · ～やすい vs ～にくい

Rule of thumb - they are opposites in meaning but not perfect mirrors in use. Negating a やすい word by swapping in にくい is safe when the verb is a happening; it can shift the sense when the verb is a choice.

この皿(さら)は割(わ)れやすいから、気(き)をつけて。
These plates break easily, so be careful.

この皿(さら)は丈夫(じょうぶ)で割(わ)れにくいです。
These plates are sturdy and don't break easily.

That pair is a clean mirror. Now compare 来(き)やすい, an everyday word meaning "easy to get to", with 来(き)にくい, which a listener will usually take socially - awkwardness about turning up - rather than as a statement about distance. The lesson is to learn the useful compounds as words, not to generate every possible one mechanically.

～やすい · ～やすい vs 簡単(かんたん)だ

Rule of thumb - 簡単(かんたん)だ judges the task; ～やすい credits a thing with making the task go smoothly.

図(ず)があるので、この問題(もんだい)は考(かんが)えやすいです。
There's a diagram, so this question is easy to think through.

Compare この問題(もんだい)は簡単(かんたん)です, which says the question is not difficult. The やすい sentence says nothing about its difficulty - it says the diagram makes the thinking go smoothly. That gap matters when you write a review: このアプリは簡単(かんたん)です sounds like the app barely does anything, while 使(つか)いやすい praises how it was built.

～やすい · ～やすい vs the potential form

Rule of thumb - the potential form asks can you at all?; ～やすい asks how smoothly?

この字(じ)は小(ちい)さいですが、読(よ)みやすいです。
The characters are small, but they're easy to read.

読(よ)めます is a yes-or-no ability. やすい is a comfort rating, and a sentence can carry both at once: 眼鏡(めがね)をかければ読(よ)めますが、読(よ)みやすくはありません。 "I can read it with glasses on, but it isn't comfortable reading."

～やすい · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 使(つか)うやすい → ✅ 使(つか)いやすい
    やすい attaches to the ます-stem, not the dictionary form. 使(つか)います → 使(つか)い + やすい.

  • ❌ 読(よ)みやすいでした → ✅ 読(よ)みやすかったです
    The compound is an い-adjective, so the past tense is built on its い, and です simply rides on top without changing.

  • ❌ この本(ほん)を読(よ)みやすいです。 → ✅ この本(ほん)は読(よ)みやすいです。
    Once やすい is attached there is no verb left to take an object, so the former object becomes the topic or the subject.

  • ❌ うちは来(こ)やすいです。 → ✅ うちは来(き)やすいです。
    来(く)る contributes its ます-stem here, the same 来(き) you see in 来(き)ます - not the ない-form stem 来(こ).

～やすい · Practice 1

1. このスプーンは小(ちい)さいので、子(こ)どもでも使(つか)____です。

使(つか)いやすいです

The stem is 使(つか)い, taken from 使(つか)います, and やすい goes straight onto it. The spoon has a property that makes using it comfortable, which is the ease reading.

～やすい · Practice 2

2. この紙(かみ)はうすいので、（破(やぶ)れやすい・破(やぶ)れにくい）です。

この紙(かみ)はうすいので、破(やぶ)れやすいです。

Thin paper tears readily, so you want the propensity reading. Note that 破(やぶ)れる is intransitive, so nobody is finding anything easy here - the tearing happens by itself.

～やすい · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "This town is quiet and easy to live in."

この町(まち)は静(しず)かで住(す)みやすいです。

住(す)む gives the stem 住(す)み, and a な-adjective links onward with で. 住(す)みやすい is the ordinary way to praise a place as liveable.

～やすい · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: この辞書(じしょ)を引(ひ)きやすいじゃないです。

✅ この辞書(じしょ)は引(ひ)きやすくないです。

There are two faults. The object particle を cannot survive, because the compound is an adjective and adjectives take no object; and じゃない is the negative for nouns and な-adjectives, while やすい needs やすくない.

～やすい · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) この皿(さら)は割(わ)りやすい。 (b) この皿(さら)は割(わ)れやすい。

The difference lives in the verb, not in やすい. (a) uses transitive 割(わ)る, so somebody is deliberately breaking the plate and finding it easy - the sort of remark you would make while smashing old crockery on purpose.

(b) uses intransitive 割(わ)れる, so nothing is deliberate: the plate is fragile and breaks of its own accord. This is the sentence you actually want when warning someone to handle it carefully.

～にくい

Meaning · hard to do - the action meets resistance, physical or social

～にくい is built the same way as ～やすい, from the ます-stem, and it comes out as an い-adjective describing the thing. But it is not simply "not easy". にくい says there is something in the way: the meat is tough, the handle is the wrong shape, the request is embarrassing to make.

The room is dark and the writing is hard to read.

～にくい · Examples (cont.)

Apparently this umbrella stands up to wind and doesn't break easily.

新(あたら)しいスマホは画面(がめん)が大(おお)きすぎて、片手(かたて)では使(つか)いにくいです。
The new phone's screen is too big, so it's awkward to use one-handed.

先輩(せんぱい)には少(すこ)し頼(たの)みにくかったので、同(おな)じクラスの友(とも)だちに手伝(てつだ)ってもらいました。
It felt a bit awkward to ask my senior, so I got a classmate to help me instead.

～にくい · Nuance & register

にくい covers three kinds of resistance, and it is worth naming them because learners tend to notice only the first.

  1. Physical - the shape, size or state of a thing fights the action: 切(き)りにくい, 持(も)ちにくい, 歩(ある)きにくい. 2. Social or emotional - nothing physical is stopping you; it is simply uncomfortable: 言(い)いにくい, 聞(き)きにくい, 断(ことわ)りにくい. This is one of the most useful things にくい does, and it has no neat English equivalent. 3. Resistance to a spontaneous event - with a verb that happens by itself, にくい means the thing holds out against it: 汚(よご)れにくい, 冷(さ)めにくい, 太(ふと)りにくい. This sense is a selling point, and packaging copy is full of it.

～にくい · Nuance & register (cont.)

The social sense earns its keep as a softener. Opening a difficult request with 言(い)いにくいんですが or お願(ねが)いしにくいのですが tells the listener you know you are imposing, and it is standard in polite adult speech at any level of formality.

にくい itself is register-neutral: no politeness, no gender or age marking. In casual speech people often reach for づらい instead (see below), but にくい is never wrong and is the safer choice in writing.

Two derived forms carry over from やすい. にくくなる - "become harder": 年(とし)を取(と)ると、新(あたら)しい言葉(ことば)が覚(おぼ)えにくくなります。 And the noun in さ: この道具(どうぐ)は使(つか)いにくさが問題(もんだい)です。

～にくい · ～にくい vs ～づらい

Rule of thumb - づらい is about the speaker's own discomfort while doing something on purpose. にくい is about the situation, and it can also describe a thing resisting an event nobody is doing.

づらい is 辛(つら)い, "painful", in suffix form, and that origin still shows: it reports how the action feels to the person performing it.

足(あし)が痛(いた)くて、階段(かいだん)が上(のぼ)りづらいです。
My leg hurts, so the stairs are hard going.

このドアは重(おも)くて開(あ)けにくいです。
This door is heavy and hard to open.

～にくい · ～にくい vs ～づらい (cont.)

Both would be understood either way round, and for volitional actions （言(い)いにくい／言(い)いづらい） the two are close to interchangeable, with づらい a shade more subjective and more colloquial. The clear split is at sense 3 above: for a thing resisting a spontaneous event, にくい is the settled choice. 壊(こわ)れづらい is increasingly heard in casual speech, but many speakers still prefer 壊(こわ)れにくい, and it is what you will see in print.

～にくい · ～にくい vs ～がたい

Rule of thumb - がたい means almost impossible to bring oneself to, it is formal and written, and it attaches to a short fixed list of verbs.

その話(はなし)は信(しん)じがたい。
That story is hard to credit.

Compare 信(しん)じにくい, which is the everyday spoken version and sounds milder. がたい lives on a handful of verbs of thought and feeling - 信(しん)じる, 想像(そうぞう)する, 理解(りかい)する, 忘(わす)れる - and never on a physical action: ❌切(き)りがたい is not Japanese, while 切(き)りにくい is ordinary. Treat がたい as vocabulary to recognise rather than a productive pattern.

～にくい · ～にくい vs the negative potential

Rule of thumb - にくい says the action is possible but unpleasant. The negative potential says it cannot be done at all.

この字(じ)は小(ちい)さくて読(よ)みにくいですが、読(よ)めます。
This print is small and hard on the eyes, but I can read it.

Swap in 読(よ)めません and the sentence contradicts itself. This distinction is worth practising because English uses "can't read this" loosely for both. If a magnifying glass would solve it, you want にくい.

～にくい · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 食(た)べるにくい → ✅ 食(た)べにくい
    Like やすい and すぎる, にくい attaches to the ます-stem, never the dictionary form.

  • ❌ この本(ほん)は難(むずか)しにくいです。 → ✅ この本(ほん)は読(よ)みにくいです。
    にくい attaches to verbs only. There is no way to hang it on an adjective; if you want "hard", the adjective 難(むずか)しい already says it.

  • ❌ 使(つか)いにくいでした → ✅ 使(つか)いにくかったです
    The whole compound is an い-adjective, so the past tense is formed on its い.

  • ❌ この靴(くつ)を歩(ある)きにくいです。 → ✅ この靴(くつ)は歩(ある)きにくいです。
    There is no verb left to take an object once にくい is attached, so the thing being described becomes the topic.

～にくい · Practice 1

1. このコップは口(くち)が小(ちい)さいので、洗(あら)____です。

洗(あら)いにくいです

The stem is 洗(あら)い, from 洗(あら)います. A narrow opening physically gets in the way of washing, which is exactly the resistance にくい reports.

～にくい · Practice 2

2. この服(ふく)は色(いろ)が濃(こ)いので、汚(よご)れが（目立(めだ)ちやすい・目立(めだ)ちにくい）です。

汚(よご)れが目立(めだ)ちにくいです。

Dark colours hide marks, so the stains resist standing out. This is the third sense: a thing holding out against something that happens by itself, which is the one advertising copy uses constantly.

～にくい · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "That's a hard thing to ask my boss."

それは部長(ぶちょう)には聞(き)きにくいことです。

Nothing physical prevents the question; the obstacle is social, and that is precisely what にくい conveys. 聞(き)きづらい would also work here and sounds slightly more personal.

～にくい · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: この机(つくえ)は重(おも)くて運(はこ)ぶにくいでした。

✅ この机(つくえ)は重(おも)くて運(はこ)びにくかったです。

Two faults in one short sentence: にくい needs the ます-stem 運(はこ)び, not the dictionary form, and the past of an い-adjective is にくかった, never にくいでした.

～にくい · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) この質問(しつもん)は答(こた)えにくいです。 (b) この質問(しつもん)には答(こた)えられません。

(a) says an answer is available but uncomfortable to give - you know the answer and would rather not say it, or the question is delicate. It leaves the door open and often precedes an answer anyway.

(b) shuts the door: you cannot answer, whether from ignorance or from a rule that forbids it. Choosing (b) when you mean (a) sounds far more final than you probably intend, which is why (a) is the diplomatic option.

～さ

Meaning · turns an adjective into a noun - the height, the weight, the difficulty of it

At N5 you learned to nominalise a whole clause with の. さ does something narrower and more mechanical: it takes a single adjective and gives you the noun for the dimension it measures. 高(たか)い is "high"; 高(たか)さ is "height" - a scale that short things have too.

な-adjective stem (no な, no だ) + さ 便利(べんり)だ → 便利(べんり)さ

～さ · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - whatever you would put in front of くない (for い-adjectives) or in front of です (for な-adjectives) is what さ attaches to. If you can still see an い or a な, you have cut too little.

A さ-noun is an ordinary noun from that moment on. It takes が, を, の, に like any other: 重(おも)さを測(はか)る、寒(さむ)さに驚(おどろ)く、 広(ひろ)さが足(た)りない。

Note - さ is productive: given an adjective, you can generally build its さ-noun and be understood. The exceptions are な-adjectives that are already nouns in their own right - 健康(けんこう), 自由(じゆう), 安心(あんしん) - where the bare word normally does the job and the さ-form sounds laboured.

～さ · Examples

この山(やま)の高(たか)さは三千(さんぜん)メートルです。
This mountain is three thousand metres high.

テーブルの長(なが)さを測(はか)ってください。
Please measure the length of the table.

この荷物(にもつ)の重(おも)さはどのくらいですか。
How much does this parcel weigh?

今年(ことし)の夏(なつ)の暑(あつ)さは特別(とくべつ)でした。
The heat this summer was something else.

先生(せんせい)のやさしさに感謝(かんしゃ)しています。
I'm grateful for my teacher's kindness.

～さ · Examples (cont.)

この町(まち)のよさは、人(ひと)が親切(しんせつ)なところです。
What's good about this town is that the people are kind.

日本語(にほんご)を勉強(べんきょう)してみて、漢字(かんじ)の難(むずか)しさがやっと分(わ)かりました。
Now that I've tried studying Japanese, I finally understand how hard kanji are.

この部屋(へや)は広(ひろ)さが十分(じゅうぶん)ですが、窓(まど)が少(すく)なくて暗(くら)いです。
This room is big enough, but it has few windows and it's dark.

～さ · Nuance & register

The single most useful thing to understand about さ is that it is neutral. 高(たか)さ does not mean "tallness" in the sense of being tall; it names the scale, and a low thing has one too. That is why この机(つくえ)の高(たか)さは七十(ななじゅう)センチです is perfectly ordinary Japanese about an unremarkable desk. The same goes for 長(なが)さ, 重(おも)さ, 広(ひろ)さ, 深(ふか)さ, 速(はや)さ - these are the words you use to ask for a measurement.

With adjectives that judge rather than measure - 難(むずか)しさ, 楽(たの)しさ, 大切(たいせつ)さ, やさしさ - the noun names how much or the quality of being so, and it tends to turn up after verbs of realising and feeling: 大切(たいせつ)さに気(き)づく、楽(たの)しさを知(し)る。

さ is register-neutral, and it is if anything commoner in writing than in speech: product specifications, recipes, weather reports and essays all lean on it. Nothing about it sounds stiff in conversation either.

～さ · Nuance & register (cont.)

Note - do not confuse this suffix with the casual sentence-final particle さ, as in そんなの関係(かんけい)ないさ. That one attaches to a finished sentence, is markedly casual, and leans male in most speech. Two unrelated words that happen to share a syllable.

～さ · ～さ vs ～み

Rule of thumb - さ is productive and measures. み is not productive: it belongs to a fixed set of established words, and it names something felt or concrete rather than a quantity.

This is the pair learners most want to treat as a free choice, and it is not one. You may build a さ-noun from any adjective; you may only use the み-nouns that already exist.

この石(いし)の重(おも)さは五(ご)キロです。
This stone weighs five kilos.

部長(ぶちょう)の言葉(ことば)には重(おも)みがあります。
The manager's words carry weight.

～さ · ～さ vs ～み (cont.)

The established pairs are worth learning as vocabulary: 深(ふか)さ is a depth in metres while 深(ふか)み is a deep spot or a richness of character; 甘(あま)さ is a degree of sweetness while 甘(あま)み is the taste itself; 楽(たの)しさ is how enjoyable something is while 楽(たの)しみ has become a plain noun meaning something you look forward to. Some adjectives have only the み form in common use - the noun for hurting is 痛(いた)み, not 痛(いた)さ, unless you specifically mean the degree of the pain.

Anything outside that set is not available: ❌新(あたら)しみ, ❌難(むずか)しみ, ❌広(ひろ)み. When in doubt, use さ.

～さ · ～さ vs こと／の

Rule of thumb - さ nominalises one adjective and names its degree. こと and の nominalise a whole clause and name the fact.

川(かわ)の深(ふか)さに驚(おどろ)きました。
I was taken aback by how deep the river was.

Compare 川(かわ)が深(ふか)いことに驚(おどろ)きました, "I was surprised at the fact that the river was deep." The さ version takes the depth for granted and points at its degree; the こと version presents the depth as news. The さ one is also the only option once you attach a number: 深(ふか)さは二(に)メートルです, never ❌深(ふか)いことは二(に)メートルです.

Because さ only takes adjectives, a verb has no さ-form at all: for "swimming", you still need 泳(およ)ぐこと or 泳(およ)ぐの.

～さ · ～さ vs leaving the adjective alone

Rule of thumb - use さ only when the sentence needs a noun in that slot. If the adjective can simply be the predicate, leave it as an adjective.

Learners who have just met さ tend to sprinkle it everywhere, and the result is a sentence that says the same thing twice:

この山(やま)は高(たか)いです。
This mountain is high.

❌この山(やま)の高(たか)さは高(たか)いです is not a stronger version of that sentence; it is a broken one, roughly "the height of this mountain is high." Use さ when a noun is genuinely required - as the object of 測(はか)る, after に, before です with a number, or as the topic of a comparison: この山(やま)の高(たか)さは富士山(ふじさん)と同(おな)じくらいです。

～さ · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 高(たか)いさ → ✅ 高(たか)さ
    The final い of an い-adjective is replaced, not kept.

  • ❌ 静(しず)かなさ／静(しず)かださ → ✅ 静(しず)かさ
    な-adjectives contribute their bare stem. Neither な nor だ survives.

  • ❌ この町(まち)のいさ → ✅ この町(まち)のよさ
    いい is irregular everywhere, and さ is no exception - it builds on the older stem よ, exactly as よくない and よかった do.

  • ❌ 旅行(りょこう)の楽(たの)しみを友(とも)だちに話(はな)しました。（meaning "I told them how fun the trip was"）→ ✅ 旅行(りょこう)の楽(たの)しさを友(とも)だちに話(はな)しました。
    楽(たの)しみ is a separate word meaning something you are looking forward to, so the sentence with み says you talked about your anticipation.

～さ · Practice 1

1. このプールの深(ふか)____は二(に)メートルです。

深(ふか)さは二(に)メートルです

A number of metres needs a noun in front of です, and that noun is the measurement word. Drop the い of 深(ふか)い and add さ.

～さ · Practice 2

2. 来週(らいしゅう)の旅行(りょこう)が（楽(たの)しさ・楽(たの)しみ）です。

来週(らいしゅう)の旅行(りょこう)が楽(たの)しみです。

This is the fixed lexical み word, meaning something you look forward to, and it is the only natural choice here. 楽(たの)しさ would name how enjoyable the trip was, which you cannot yet know about next week.

～さ · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "I was surprised at the size of this city."

驚(おどろ)く takes に, and に needs a noun in front of it, so the adjective has to be nominalised. 大(おお)きい drops its い and takes さ.

～さ · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: この荷物(にもつ)の重(おも)いさを教(おし)えてください。

✅ この荷物(にもつ)の重(おも)さを教(おし)えてください。

The い of 重(おも)い is replaced by さ, not followed by it. A useful check: the finished noun should end in exactly one さ and contain no い from the adjective ending.

～さ · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) この石(いし)の重(おも)さ (b) この石(いし)の重(おも)み

(a) is the measurable weight, the thing a scale reports in kilos. It is neutral and works for a pebble as well as a boulder.

(b) is the felt heaviness - the sense of substance or gravity the stone gives you when you pick it up, and by extension the impressiveness of something. It is one of the established み words, and it is a judgement rather than a measurement. You cannot generate new み nouns this way; where no fixed み word exists, さ is what you use.

～方 - way of doing

Meaning · the way of doing something - how to do it, or the manner in which someone does it

Read かた. Attach it to a verb's ます-stem and you get a noun: 作(つく)り方(かた) "how to make it", 話(はな)し方(かた) "the way someone speaks". This is the single most productive noun-building suffix at N4, and it turns any verb you already know into something you can ask about, teach, learn and criticise.

～方 - way of doing · Formation

Attaches to Form Example
Verb (う-verb) ます-stem + 方(かた) 使(つか)う → 使(つか)い方(かた)
Verb (る-verb) ます-stem + 方(かた) 開(あ)ける → 開(あ)け方(かた)
Verb (する) the noun is 仕方(しかた) する → 仕方(しかた)
Noun + する Noun + の + 仕方(しかた) 予約(よやく)する → 予約(よやく)の仕方(しかた)
Verb (来(く)る) 来(き) + 方(かた) 来(く)る → 来(き)方(かた)（rare）

Rule of thumb - the object particle を becomes の. 漢字(かんじ)を書(か)く → 漢字(かんじ)の書(か)き方(かた). Nothing in the phrase is a verb any more, so nothing can take an object.

～方 - way of doing · Formation (cont.)

A destination に／へ usually survives, with の added after it: 空港(くうこう)へ行(い)く → 空港(くうこう)への行(い)き方(かた). Some fixed pairs simply drop the particle instead - バスに乗(の)る is normally バスの乗(の)り方(かた) - so learn the common ones as phrases.

The finished word is an ordinary noun and behaves like one: 使(つか)い方(かた)が分(わ)からない、作(つく)り方(かた)を習(なら)う、話(はな)し方(かた)で分(わ)かる。

Note - 方 is only one of the things a ます-stem can do. The bare stem is itself a noun-maker, and a good deal of everyday vocabulary is built that way: 動(うご)く → 動(うご)き, 始(はじ)まる → 始(はじ)まり, 帰(かえ)る → 帰(かえ)り, 考(かんが)える → 考(かんが)え, 休(やす)む → 休(やす)み. Not every verb yields a usable one, so treat these as words to collect rather than forms to generate - but when you meet a noun that looks like a ます-stem, it usually is one.

～方 - way of doing · Examples

この機械(きかい)の**使(つか)い方(かた)**を教(おし)えてください。
Please show me how to use this machine.

この漢字(かんじ)の**読(よ)み方(かた)**が分(わ)かりません。
I don't know how to read this kanji.

カレーの**作(つく)り方(かた)**は意外(いがい)と簡単(かんたん)です。
Curry is easier to make than you'd think.

彼女(かのじょ)の**話(はな)し方(かた)**はとてもていねいです。
The way she speaks is very polite.

空港(くうこう)への**行(い)き方(かた)**をスマホで調(しら)べた。
I looked up how to get to the airport on my phone.

～方 - way of doing · Examples (cont.)

おはしの**持(も)ち方(かた)**は子(こ)どものときに習(なら)いました。
I learned how to hold chopsticks when I was a child.

パソコンの調子(ちょうし)が悪(わる)いので、修理(しゅうり)の**仕方(しかた)**をネットで探(さが)しています。
My computer is playing up, so I'm hunting online for how to repair it.

同(おな)じことを伝(つた)えても、**言(い)い方(かた)**によって相手(あいて)の気持(きも)ちは変(か)わります。
Even when you convey the same thing, how you put it changes how the other person feels.

～方 - way of doing · Nuance & register

～方 splits into two closely related senses, and it is worth knowing which one you are producing.

  • Method - 作(つく)り方(かた),
    使(つか)い方(かた), 行(い)き方(かた), 直(なお)し方(かた). These answer "how do I…?" and are what you ask a shop assistant or a recipe.
  • Manner - 話(はな)し方(かた),
    歩(ある)き方(かた), 食(た)べ方(かた), 笑(わら)い方(かた). These describe someone's personal style and almost always come with an evaluation attached: 食(た)べ方(かた)がきれいですね。

Because the manner sense evaluates a person, it can carry criticism. 言(い)い方(かた)が悪(わる)かった - "I put that badly" - is a standard apology, and あの人(ひと)の話(はな)し方(かた)はちょっと… is a soft way of saying you dislike how someone talks. Nothing about the suffix itself is rude; the judgement comes from the adjective you pair it with.

～方 - way of doing · Nuance & register (cont.)

The suffix is register-neutral and equally normal in speech and writing. 使(つか)い方(かた)を教(おし)えていただけますか is a polite request; 使(つか)い方(かた)分(わ)かる? is what you say to a friend. Only the surrounding grammar changes.

One high-frequency idiom: 仕方(しかた)がない - "it can't be helped", "there's nothing for it". In casual speech this contracts to 仕方(しかた)ない or, very commonly, しょうがない. Learn it as a set phrase; the 方 inside it is the same word, but the meaning of the whole is fixed.

～方 - way of doing · ～方(かた) vs 方法(ほうほう)

Rule of thumb - ～方 is built out of a specific verb and is everyday. 方法(ほうほう) is a free-standing noun meaning "method", and it is more abstract and more formal.

この問題(もんだい)の**解(と)き方(かた)**を教(おし)えてください。
Please show me how to solve this problem.

Compare この問題(もんだい)を解決(かいけつ)する方法(ほうほう)を考(かんが)えましょう - "let's think of a way to resolve this issue." The 方 version is about the concrete steps of one action; 方法(ほうほう) takes a whole clause in front of it and suits reports, meetings and instructions. In a manual you will often see both: 支払(しはら)い方法(ほうほう) for the section heading and 払(はら)い方(かた) in the shop.

～方 - way of doing · ～方(かた) vs どうやって

Rule of thumb - どうやって asks the question. ～方 packages the answer as a noun you can then do things to.

このお茶(ちゃ)の**いれ方(かた)**は少(すこ)し難(むずか)しいです。
The way you brew this tea is a little tricky.

You could ask このお茶(ちゃ)はどうやっていれますか and get the same information, but only the noun can be the subject of 難(むずか)しい, the object of 習(なら)う, or the thing you 忘(わす)れた. That is the whole point of nominalising: どうやって is a question word and cannot sit in a noun slot.

～方 - way of doing · 方(かた) vs 方(ほう)

Rule of thumb - after a ます-stem, 方 is always かた. The ほう reading belongs to the comparison word ～の方(ほう)が, which follows a noun or a plain-form clause, not a stem.

バスの**乗(の)り方(かた)**が分(わ)からなくて、駅員(えきいん)さんに聞(き)きました。
I didn't know how to ride the bus, so I asked a station attendant.

Set that against 電車(でんしゃ)の方(ほう)が速(はや)いです, "the train is faster", where 方 means "side" and is read ほう. There is a third word here too: 方 read かた as a free noun is the polite word for a person - あの方(かた)はどなたですか. Same character, three jobs; the stem test sorts out the suffix instantly.

～方 - way of doing · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 読(よ)む方(かた) → ✅ 読(よ)み方(かた)
    方 attaches to the ます-stem, like やすい and すぎる. The dictionary form before 方 produces the ほう comparison word instead, which means something else entirely.

  • ❌ 漢字(かんじ)を書(か)き方(かた) → ✅ 漢字(かんじ)の書(か)き方(かた)
    The compound is a noun, so its former object is attached with の.

  • ❌ 運転(うんてん)し方(かた) → ✅ 運転(うんてん)の仕方(しかた)
    A noun + する verb uses the lexicalised noun 仕方(しかた), linked with の.

  • ❌ この言葉(ことば)の読(よ)み方(ほう)を教(おし)えてください。 → ✅ …読(よ)み方(かた)…
    A written mistake becomes a spoken one the moment you read it aloud. On a ます-stem the reading is かた, always.

The stem of 作(つく)る is 作(つく)り, from 作(つく)ります, and 方 goes straight onto it. Note the の linking it to the dish, where the plain verb would have taken を.

～方 - way of doing · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 彼(かれ)の字(じ)の書(か)き方(かた)はきれいです。 (b) 彼(かれ)は字(じ)を書(か)くのが上手(じょうず)です。

(b) uses the N5 nominaliser の on the whole action and rates his ability at it. It says he is good at writing, without committing to anything about what the result looks like. Use 方 when the way of doing it is the point, and の when the doing is.

～がる

Meaning · to show signs of feeling something - how you report another person's inner state

Japanese does not let you simply announce what somebody else feels. ～がる solves that: it takes an adjective of emotion or sensation and turns it into a verb meaning "behaves as though X". You are no longer claiming to know what is in their head; you are describing what you can see them doing.

～がる · Formation

がる attaches to the adjective stem and produces an ordinary う-verb.

Attaches to Form Example
い-adjective drop い + がる 寒(さむ)い → 寒(さむ)がる
な-adjective stem + がる 嫌(いや)だ → 嫌(いや)がる
ほしい ほし + がる ほしい → ほしがる
～たい たい → たがる 行(い)きたい → 行(い)きたがる

Once formed, it conjugates as a う-verb like 作(つく)る:

～がる · Formation (cont.)

Verb form Example
Polite 怖(こわ)がります
Negative 怖(こわ)がらない
Past 怖(こわ)がった
て-form 怖(こわ)がって
Progressive / current state 怖(こわ)がっている
Person noun 怖(こわ)がり、寒(さむ)がり、恥(は)ずかしがり屋(や)

Rule of thumb - がる makes a transitive verb, so what the feeling is aimed at takes を, not the が the original adjective wanted: おもちゃがほしい → おもちゃをほしがる.

～がる · Formation (cont.)

Note - がる only attaches to adjectives describing how someone feels: 寒(さむ)い, 暑(あつ)い, 痛(いた)い, 怖(こわ)い, うれしい, 悲(かな)しい, さびしい, 恥(は)ずかしい, うらやましい, ほしい, 嫌(いや)だ, 残念(ざんねん)だ. It cannot attach to an adjective describing a thing from outside: there is no ❌高(たか)がる or ❌赤(あか)がる.

～がる · Examples

子(こ)どもが寒(さむ)がっています。
The children are feeling the cold.

妹(いもうと)は犬(いぬ)を怖(こわ)がります。
My little sister is afraid of dogs.

父(ちち)は新(あたら)しい時計(とけい)をほしがっている。
My father's had his eye on a new watch.

息子(むすこ)は注射(ちゅうしゃ)のあとで腕(うで)を痛(いた)がっていました。
My son was making a fuss about his arm after the injection.

猫(ねこ)はおふろを嫌(いや)がるので、洗(あら)うのが大変(たいへん)です。
Cats hate baths, so washing them is hard work.

～がる · Examples (cont.)

みんなが不思議(ふしぎ)がっていたので、理由(りゆう)を説明(せつめい)しました。
Everyone seemed puzzled, so I explained the reason.

山田(やまだ)さんは一人(ひとり)で住(す)んでいるので、ときどきさびしがります。
Yamada lives on his own, so he gets lonely from time to time.

先輩(せんぱい)は留学(りゅうがく)の話(はなし)を聞(き)いて、ずいぶんうらやましがっていました。
My senior sounded thoroughly envious when he heard about the exchange programme.

～がる · Nuance & register

The heart of がる is evidence. 痛(いた)がっている does not mean "is in pain"; it means the person is behaving in a way that shows pain - wincing, holding the spot, saying it hurts. That is why the pattern belongs to third persons: you can see other people's behaviour, but you feel your own sensations directly.

Bare がる versus がっている. The plain form describes a habit or a general truth: 子(こ)どもは注射(ちゅうしゃ)を怖(こわ)がる is "children are afraid of injections", a statement about children at large. For what someone is doing right now, use がっている.

First person. You would not normally say ❌私(わたし)は今(いま)寒(さむ)がっています - for your own present feeling, the plain adjective 寒(さむ)いです is what you want. But がる is fine about yourself when you step outside your own experience: describing a habit （私(わたし)は昔(むかし)から暗(くら)いところを怖(こわ)がります）or looking back on your younger self.

～がる · Nuance & register (cont.)

Tone. Because it reports behaviour, がる can slide into "making a fuss about", and with some verbs it tends to sound faintly critical or amused. That tendency, plus the fact that it is a bald description of someone's inner life, makes it a poor fit for talking about a superior. Prefer a hedge: 部長(ぶちょう)は残念(ざんねん)そうでした, or 残念(ざんねん)だとおっしゃっていました.

The がり nouns. Drop the verb ending and you get a noun for the kind of person: 寒(さむ)がり (someone who feels the cold), 暑(あつ)がり, 怖(こわ)がり, and with 屋 attached, さびしがり屋(や) and 恥(は)ずかしがり屋(や). These are everyday self-descriptions - 私(わたし)、寒(さむ)がりなんです is a perfectly normal thing to say in January.

がる on たい gives たがる, which is common enough to get its own treatment; everything above about evidence, third persons and politeness applies there too.

～がる · ～がる vs ～そうだ

Rule of thumb - そうだ is a guess from how something looks at a glance. がる reports what someone is actually doing.

彼(かれ)は上着(うわぎ)をぬいで、ずっと暑(あつ)がっています。
He's taken his jacket off and has been hot the whole time.

Compare 彼(かれ)は暑(あつ)そうです, "he looks hot" - one look, one inference, no claim about behaviour over time. Two further differences follow from this. そうだ works on things as well as people （このケーキ、おいしそう）, while がる needs someone with feelings; and そうだ is a much softer thing to say about a superior, which is why it is the polite way out.

～がる · ～がる vs the plain adjective

Rule of thumb - in a plain main-clause statement, an emotion adjective about a third person needs がる or a hedge. About yourself, use the bare adjective.

留学生(りゅうがくせい)のリーさんは国(くに)の料理(りょうり)が食(た)べられなくて、さびしがっています。
Lee, the exchange student, can't get food from home and is feeling homesick.

A flat ❌リーさんはさびしいです claims direct access to Lee's feelings. The escapes are がる, or one of the reporting frames you already have: さびしいそうです, さびしいと言(い)っています, さびしいようです. Inside a subordinate clause the restriction lifts entirely - さびしい人(ひと)は連絡(れんらく)してください is fine.

～がる · ほしがる vs ほしい

Rule of thumb - ほしい is an い-adjective and takes が; ほしがる is a verb and takes を. The particle change is the giveaway that the word class changed.

娘(むすめ)は自分(じぶん)のパソコンをほしがっています。
My daughter wants a computer of her own.

Set that against 私(わたし)は自分(じぶん)のパソコンがほしいです for your own want. The same asymmetry you met with たい at N5 is at work: the adjective is for the inside view, the がる verb for the outside one. And as with たい, ほしがっている is the normal shape for a want someone has right now, while bare ほしがる suits a habit.

～がる · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 寒(さむ)いがる → ✅ 寒(さむ)がる
    The final い is replaced, exactly as it is for さ and すぎる.

  • ❌ 弟(おとうと)はおもちゃがほしがっています。 → ✅ 弟(おとうと)はおもちゃをほしがっています。
    がる builds a transitive verb, and transitive verbs take を. Leaving が in place is the single commonest slip with this pattern.

  • ❌ この店(みせ)は高(たか)がる。 → ✅ この店(みせ)は高(たか)いです。
    がる needs an adjective about someone's feelings. 高(たか)い describes a price, not an emotion, so there is nothing for がる to report.

  • ❌ 私(わたし)は今(いま)寒(さむ)がっています。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は今(いま)寒(さむ)いです。
    You do not need evidence for your own present sensation. Reserve がる for other people, or for yourself viewed as a type of person.

～たがる

Meaning · someone else wants to do something - as read off their behaviour

～たがる is がる applied to たい. At N5 you learned that plain ～たい is only for your own wishes and for asking about the listener's; ～たがる is the form that lets you talk about everybody else. It is a verb, not an adjective, and it reports a want the way an observer sees it: from the outside, in what the person does.

～たがる · Formation

Take the ます-stem, add たがる. Equivalently: build the たい form and swap たい for たがる.

Attaches to Form Example
Verb (う-verb) ます-stem + たがる 飲(の)む → 飲(の)みたがる
Verb (る-verb) ます-stem + たがる 出(で)かける → 出(で)かけたがる
Verb (する) し + たがる 運動(うんどう)する → 運動(うんどう)したがる
Verb (来(く)る) 来(き) + たがる 来(く)る → 来(き)たがる

Rule of thumb - the object takes を, never the が that たい permits. 水(みず)が飲(の)みたい, but 水(みず)を飲(の)みたがっている. たい is an adjective; たがる is a verb, and verbs take を.

～たがる · Examples

弟(おとうと)は外(そと)で遊(あそ)びたがっています。
My little brother wants to play outside.

猫(ねこ)が部屋(へや)に入(はい)りたがっている。
The cat wants to get into the room.

My son won't eat his vegetables.

Children want to know about everything - that's just how they are.

彼(かれ)は自分(じぶん)のことをあまり話(はな)したがらない。
He isn't very forthcoming about himself.

～たがる · Examples (cont.)

My father won't go to the doctor, so the whole family is worried.

Everyone in the class had been wanting to go to Kyoto, so I planned a weekend trip.

～たがる · Nuance & register

The negative is the workhorse. ～たがらない is at least as common as the positive, and it does a job English handles with "won't": 薬(くすり)を飲(の)みたがらない is not a neutral report of an absent desire - it says the person is visibly resisting. Doctors, parents and teachers use it constantly.

Bare form versus ～たがっている. As with がる, the plain form states a habit or a general truth - 彼(かれ)はいつも新(あたら)しいことを試(ため)したがる - while たがっている reports what someone wants at this moment. If you are describing what is going on in front of you, ている is almost always the right choice, and leaving it off is a common learner slip.

～たがる · Nuance & register (cont.)

Tone. Because it describes someone from outside, たがる can carry a faint edge - "keeps wanting to", "is always trying to". That edge is what powers 目立(めだ)ちたがり屋(や), "attention seeker", and it is why the pattern is a bad idea about anyone you are being polite to. Do not say ❌部長(ぶちょう)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)りたがっています. Say instead 部長(ぶちょう)は早(はや)く帰(かえ)りたいとおっしゃっていました, or お帰(かえ)りになりたいようです.

Yourself. たがる about your own present wish is wrong - you do not need to observe yourself. It does become available when you describe your own character from a distance: 私(わたし)は昔(むかし)から人前(ひとまえ)で歌(うた)いたがらない性格(せいかく)です。

Register. The pattern itself is neutral: no politeness marking, no gender or age association, equally at home in speech and writing. What is marked is who you point it at.

～たがる · ～たがる vs ～たい

Rule of thumb - たい for me and for you; たがる for everyone else. Three things change at once: the person, the particle, and the word class.

妹(いもうと)はピアノを習(なら)いたがっています。
My little sister wants to take piano lessons.

Say 私(わたし)はピアノが習(なら)いたいです about yourself and every piece of the sentence differs: が is allowed, and the polite form is です on an adjective rather than ます on a verb. Mixing the two produces ❌習(なら)いたがりたいです or ❌習(なら)いたがるです, both of which come from forgetting that たがる is a verb.

The restriction is on plain main-clause statements only. Inside a modifying clause, plain たい about a third person is fine: 参加(さんか)したい人(ひと)は受付(うけつけ)に来(き)てください。

～たがる · ～たがっている vs ～たがる

Rule of thumb - たがっている for a want someone has now; bare たがる for a habit or a general truth about a group.

赤(あか)ちゃんがだっこされたがっています。
The baby wants to be picked up.

Compare 赤(あか)ちゃんは夜(よる)になるとだっこされたがる, "babies want to be held once evening comes" - a pattern of behaviour rather than a report on this baby right now. The generic use is where you will most often meet the bare form, frequently rounded off with ものだ.

～たがる · ～たがる vs ～たいそうです・～たいと言(い)っています

Rule of thumb - たがる says you saw it. The reporting frames say you heard it, and they are the polite route upward.

田中(たなか)さんは会議(かいぎ)に出(で)たがっていません。
Tanaka doesn't want to sit in on the meeting.

That sentence claims to read Tanaka from his conduct, which is fine among peers and rude about a superior. The alternatives shift the source of the information: 出(で)たくないそうです ("I hear he doesn't"), 出(で)たくないと言(い)っています ("he says he doesn't"), 出(で)たくないようです ("he seems not to"). All three are softer, and the last two are what you use in a workplace when the person outranks you.

～たがる · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 行(い)くたがる → ✅ 行(い)きたがる
    たがる builds on the ます-stem, exactly like たい does.

  • ❌ 子(こ)どもがジュースが飲(の)みたがっています。 → ✅ 子(こ)どもがジュースを飲(の)みたがっています。
    The が option belongs to たい. Once the word is a verb, the object must be marked with を.

  • ❌ 弟(おとうと)は行(い)きたがったです。 → ✅ 弟(おとうと)は行(い)きたがっていました。
    Learners carry たい's adjective endings across. たがる is a う-verb: the past polite is 行(い)きたがりました, and for a past state, 行(い)きたがっていました.

  • ❌ 先生(せんせい)は新(あたら)しい辞書(じしょ)を買(か)いたがっています。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)は新(あたら)しい辞書(じしょ)を買(か)いたいとおっしゃっていました。
    Grammatical, but it inspects a teacher's motives out loud. Report what they said instead.

The negative of a う-verb, and the form that carries the "won't, resists it" flavour English gets from "doesn't want to". 使(つか)いたがらない is the plain equivalent.

～たがる · Practice 4

～たがる · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 彼(かれ)はもっと話(はな)したがっています。 (b) 彼(かれ)はもっと話(はな)したいと言(い)っています。

(a) is your reading of his behaviour: he keeps leaning in, he has not finished, you can tell he wants to keep going. Nobody has necessarily said anything, and the judgement is yours - which is why it would be presumptuous about a superior.

(b) hands the claim back to him. He said it; you are relaying it. This version is safe in any company, and it is what you would write in a message to a manager.

～がする

Meaning · a sensation reaches you - a smell, a sound, a taste, a feeling

する is not always "do". With a small set of sensation nouns it means something closer to "there is" or "it comes to me": 音(おと)がする, "there's a sound"; においがする, "there's a smell". Nobody is performing anything. The sensation arrives, and the noun naming it is the subject.

～がする · Formation

Sensation noun + が + する. There is no conjugating of the noun; only する moves.

Attaches to Form Example
Sound 音(おと)・声(こえ) + がする 変(へん)な音(おと)がする
Smell におい・香(かお)り + がする いいにおいがする
Taste 味(あじ) + がする 甘(あま)い味(あじ)がする
Feeling, hunch 気(き)・感(かん)じ + がする 気(き)がする
Bodily sensation 寒気(さむけ)・頭痛(ずつう)・めまい + がする めまいがする
Sense of a presence 気配(けはい) + がする 人(ひと)の気配(けはい)がする

する is irregular and carries the whole conjugation:

～がする · Formation (cont.)

Verb form Example
Polite 音(おと)がします
Negative 音(おと)がしない
Past 音(おと)がした
て-form 音(おと)がして
Ongoing 音(おと)がしている

Rule of thumb - the sensation is the subject, so the particle is が. Never ❌においをする. Nothing is being done to the smell; the smell is what is there.

～がする · Formation (cont.)

Note - the list of nouns that work this way is short and fixed. Sight is not on it: for something visible you use 見(み)える, never ❌山(やま)がする. Pain is not on it either - it is 頭(あたま)が痛(いた)いです or 痛(いた)みがあります, though the noun 頭痛(ずつう) does take がする.

～がする · Examples

だれかの声(こえ)がする。
I can hear someone's voice.

台所(だいどころ)からいいにおいがします。
There's a lovely smell coming from the kitchen.

このスープは少(すこ)し甘(あま)い味(あじ)がします。
This soup tastes slightly sweet.

外(そと)で変(へん)な音(おと)がしたので、窓(まど)を開(あ)けてみました。
There was an odd noise outside, so I opened the window to look.

朝(あさ)から寒気(さむけ)がするので、今日(きょう)は会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)みます。
I've had chills since this morning, so I'm taking the day off work.

～がする · Examples (cont.)

どこかでお会(あ)いしたような気(き)がします。
I have a feeling we've met somewhere before.

この部屋(へや)は古(ふる)い本(ほん)のにおいがして、なんだか落(お)ち着(つ)きます。
This room smells of old books, and something about that is calming.

さっきから上(うえ)の階(かい)で人(ひと)が歩(ある)いている音(おと)がして、なかなか眠(ねむ)れません。
I've been hearing someone walking about upstairs for a while now, and I can't get to sleep.

～がする · Nuance & register

The defining feature of がする is that the sensation is unbidden and often unidentified. 音(おと)がする is what you say when something made a noise and you do not yet know what: you noticed, you did not investigate. That is why it pairs so naturally with 変(へん)な, 大(おお)きな and だれかの - words that admit you have not pinned the source down.

The noun can be modified freely, and this is where most of the expressive work happens: レモンの味(あじ)がする, 焦(こ)げたにおいがする, 子(こ)どもの声(こえ)がする. A の phrase in front means "the smell/taste/sound of that thing", which is how you say something tastes like something else.

Tense matters more than usual here. がした reports one moment - a bang, a whiff. がする in the plain present covers a sensation happening now or as a general property （このお菓子(かし)はバターのにおいがする）. がしている is available for something clearly ongoing, but Japanese reaches for the plain present far more readily than English does with "is smelling".

～がする · Nuance & register (cont.)

気がする deserves separate attention. It is one of the most used expressions in the spoken language: どこかで聞(き)いたことがあるような気(き)がする, 「忘(わす)れ物(もの)をしたような気(き)がする」. It reports a hunch you cannot justify, and because it commits you to nothing it is very useful for softening - 少(すこ)し高(たか)いような気(き)がします is a gentler way to say something is overpriced.

The pattern is register-neutral: identical in casual and polite speech, and completely standard in writing. The medical uses - 寒気(さむけ)がする, 吐(は)き気(け)がする, めまいがする - are the phrases you actually need at a clinic, and they are worth memorising as units.

～がする · ～がする vs 聞(き)こえる・見(み)える

Rule of thumb - 聞(き)こえる says you heard a particular thing. がする says there was a sound. And there is no visual がする: for sight, only 見(み)える.

夜中(よなか)に猫(ねこ)の声(こえ)がして、目(め)が覚(さ)めました。
A cat's cry in the night woke me up.

Compare 猫(ねこ)の声(こえ)が聞(き)こえました, "I could hear a cat." Both are correct, and the difference is where the sentence puts you. 聞こえる is about your ears reaching the sound and implies you made it out; がする is about the sound being there at all, and sits comfortably with not knowing what it was. This is also why 気配(けはい)がする works while ❌気配(けはい)が聞(き)こえる does not - a presence is not a thing you hear.

～がする · 気(き)がする vs と思(おも)う

Rule of thumb - 気(き)がする is a hunch with no grounds behind it. と思(おも)う is an opinion you would defend.

今日(きょう)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)りそうな気(き)がします。
I've got a feeling it's going to rain today.

Say 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ると思(おも)います instead and you sound as though you checked the forecast. 気がする explicitly declines to give a reason, which makes it the safer form when you are half-remembering something or disagreeing gently with someone senior.

～がする · ～がする vs ～をしている

Rule of thumb - がする is a sensation coming to you. をしている describes a property a thing has - its colour, its shape, its expression.

この石(いし)は変(へん)わった形(かたち)をしています。
This stone has an unusual shape.

Same verb, opposite direction, opposite particle. 色(いろ)をしている, 形(かたち)をしている, 顔(かお)をしている are the three you will actually meet: 青(あお)い目(め)をした猫(ねこ), 悲(かな)しそうな顔(かお)をしていた. Sensations never take を, and appearances never take が in this construction.

～がする · Common mistakes

  • ❌ いいにおいをします。 → ✅ いいにおいがします。
    The smell is the subject of the sentence, not an object someone acts on.

  • ❌ この花(はな)はいいにおいがあります。 → ✅ この花(はな)はいいにおいがします。
    English "has a nice smell" pushes learners towards ある. Japanese treats a perceived smell as something that does - it する.

  • ❌ 昨日(きのう)から頭痛(ずつう)をしています。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)から頭痛(ずつう)がします。
    Same particle error in a medical setting, where being understood matters most.

  • ❌ あそこに大(おお)きい建物(たてもの)がします。 → ✅ あそこに大(おお)きい建物(たてもの)が見(み)えます。
    がする covers hearing, smell, taste and bodily feeling - never sight.

～がする · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 下(した)の階(かい)で音(おと)がします。 (b) 下(した)の階(かい)の音(おと)が聞(き)こえます。

～のは○○だ - cleft sentences

Meaning · singles out one part of a sentence and puts it in the spotlight - "the one who did it was…", "it was last year that…"

Take a sentence, nominalise all of it except the piece you care about, and put that piece after は…だ. 弟(おとうと)が窓(まど)を割(わ)った becomes 窓(まど)を割(わ)ったのは弟(おとうと)です - "the one who broke the window was my brother." Everything before のは is treated as already agreed; everything after it is the answer.

～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · Formation

The clause takes the plain form and then の, exactly as with the N5 nominaliser. The focused element follows は, with だ or です closing the sentence.

Attaches to Form Example
Verb (plain) dictionary form + のは…だ 作(つく)る → 作(つく)るのは母(はは)です
Verb (past) た-form + のは…だ 落(お)とした → 落(お)としたのは弟(おとうと)です
Verb (negative) ない-form + のは…だ 来(こ)なかった → 来(こ)なかったのは田中(たなか)さんです
い-adjective plain form + のは…だ 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いのはこちらです
な-adjective ～な + のは…だ 好(す)きだ → 好(す)きなのは秋(あき)です
Focus = a reason …のは…からです 休(やす)んだのは熱(ねつ)があったからです

～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - the subject inside the の-clause takes が, never は. The sentence already has its は, and it belongs to the のは frame: 私(わたし)が買(か)ったのはこの本(ほん)です.

When the focus is a time, a place or a person, the particle that element would have carried is normally dropped: 会(あ)ったのは駅(えき)です. Keeping it - 駅(えき)ででした - is possible in careful writing but sounds heavy, and learners are safer without it. A reason is the exception: it keeps から and adds です, as in the last row above.

Note - the の here is a light noun meaning "the one / the thing / the time". You can often replace it with the specific noun for extra clarity: 割(わ)った人(ひと)は弟(おとうと)です, 日本(にほん)に来(き)たときは去年(きょねん)でした. の is the general-purpose version, and it is what you reach for when no particular noun suggests itself.

この絵(え)をかいたのは妹(いもうと)です。
My little sister is the one who drew this picture.

The season I like is autumn.

It was last spring that I came to Japan.

I'm the one at fault. I really am sorry.

最初(さいしょ)に気(き)がついたのは田中(たなか)さんでした。
Tanaka was the one who noticed first.

昨日(きのう)休(やす)んだのは熱(ねつ)があったからです。
The reason I was off yesterday is that I had a temperature.

私(わたし)が毎朝(まいあさ)飲(の)むのはコーヒーではなく紅茶(こうちゃ)です。
What I drink every morning isn't coffee but tea.

The only person who objected at the meeting was Yamada; everyone else was in favour.

～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · Nuance & register

A cleft splits a sentence into two halves that do different jobs. The の-clause is the part you and your listener are already taking for granted - somebody broke the window, I came to Japan at some point. The part after は is the news, and because the frame exists only to deliver it, the emphasis is much stronger than in an ordinary sentence.

That makes clefts the natural answer to a question word. Ask だれが割(わ)ったんですか and 割(わ)ったのは弟(おとうと)です answers it exactly; ask いつ来(き)ましたか and 来(き)たのは先週(せんしゅう)です does the same. It is equally the form for correcting someone, because the frame lets you accept everything except the one piece you are disputing: 私(わたし)が言(い)ったのはそういう意味(いみ)ではありません。

～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · Nuance & register (cont.)

The pattern is register-neutral. In polite speech the ending is です／でした; in casual speech it becomes だ, or vanishes altogether - 落(お)としたの、弟(おとうと)? is a perfectly ordinary question between friends. It is if anything even commoner in writing, where 大切(たいせつ)なのは… and 問題(もんだい)なのは… open paragraphs constantly.

One shape worth learning whole: 大切(たいせつ)なのは…ことです. When the focus itself is an action rather than a thing, it has to be nominalised too, and there the nominaliser must be こと, not の - see the next section.

～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · ～のは○○だ vs plain が focus

Rule of thumb - が presents its noun as the news. The cleft does that and states out loud what everyone is presupposing, which makes it more emphatic and more exclusive.

昨日(きのう)遅(おく)れたのは私(わたし)です。
The one who was late yesterday was me.

Compare 昨日(きのう)私(わたし)が遅(おく)れました, which simply reports the event with the subject highlighted. The cleft takes for granted that somebody was late - it is the sentence you use when that is already on the table - and it carries a clear implication that nobody else was. That exclusivity is why clefts are so useful for owning up, for correcting, and for singling one item out of a list.

～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · の vs こと as the nominaliser

Rule of thumb - の when the focus is a person, thing, time or place. こと when what you are naming is itself an action or a proposition, and always when the nominalised clause sits before だ／です as the predicate.

For a cleft that identifies an entity, only の works: 落(お)としたのは弟(おとうと)です, never ❌落(お)としたことは弟(おとうと)です. A person is not an event, so こと has nothing to name.

Turn the sentence round, though, and the requirement flips:

私(わたし)の趣味(しゅみ)は本(ほん)を読(よ)むことです。
My hobby is reading.

～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · の vs こと as the nominaliser (cont.)

Here the nominalised clause is the predicate, and ❌読(よ)むのです is not available - that string already means something else entirely, the explanatory んです. The same rule governs 大切(たいせつ)なのは毎日(まいにち)続(つづ)けることです.

Two more places where the choice is fixed, both worth memorising. Before a verb of direct perception - 見(み)る, 聞(き)く, 聞(き)こえる, 見(み)える - only の is possible, because you witness an event rather than a fact: 子(こ)どもが泣(な)いているのを聞(き)きました. And a set of idioms takes こと and nothing else: ことができる, たことがある.

～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · ～のは○○だ vs ～んです

Rule of thumb - both contain の, and they do opposite things. んです explains the whole situation. のは…だ isolates one element of it.

来週(らいしゅう)出張(しゅっちょう)するのは大阪(おおさか)です。
The place I'm travelling to next week is Osaka.

Set that beside 来週(らいしゅう)大阪(おおさか)に出張(しゅっちょう)するんです, "the thing is, I'm off to Osaka next week" - an explanation offered as background, with no single element picked out. If you can point at one word and say "that is the answer", you want the cleft; if the whole sentence is the answer, you want んです.

～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 私(わたし)は一番(いちばん)好(す)きなのは夏(なつ)です。 → ✅ 私(わたし)が一番(いちばん)好(す)きなのは夏(なつ)です。
    The subject sits inside the nominalised clause, and a clause like that takes が. Two は in one sentence leaves the first one homeless.

  • ❌ 静(しず)かだのは朝(あさ)だけです。 → ✅ 静(しず)かなのは朝(あさ)だけです。
    な-adjectives take な before a nominalising の, the same shape they use before ので and のに.

  • ❌ 大切(たいせつ)なのは練習(れんしゅう)するのです。 → ✅ 大切(たいせつ)なのは練習(れんしゅう)することです。
    A nominalised clause standing before です must use こと. With の the sentence turns into an explanatory んです and stops meaning what you wanted.

  • ❌ 皿(さら)を割(わ)ったことは猫(ねこ)です。 → ✅ 皿(さら)を割(わ)ったのは猫(ねこ)です。
    When the focus is a creature, a thing or a time, the nominaliser is の. こと would be naming an event, which cannot equal a cat.

～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · Practice 1

1. この料理(りょうり)を作(つく)った____父(ちち)です。

作(つく)ったのは父(ちち)です

The cook is a person, so the light noun の is the one you want, followed by は to mark everything before it as shared background. The past form 作(つく)った goes straight in front of の.

～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · Practice 2

2. 私(わたし)の夢(ゆめ)は世界(せかい)を旅行(りょこう)する（の・こと）です。

私(わたし)の夢(ゆめ)は世界(せかい)を旅行(りょこう)することです。

A nominalised clause immediately in front of です has to use こと. Writing するのです would produce the explanatory んです pattern, which says something completely different.

～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "It was in Osaka that we first met."

初(はじ)めて会(あ)ったのは大阪(おおさか)でした。

The meeting is presupposed and the place is the news, which is exactly what a cleft is for. Note that the で you would use in 大阪(おおさか)で会(あ)いました is dropped once the place moves into the focus slot.

～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)学校(がっこう)を休(やす)んだのは頭(あたま)が痛(いた)かったです。

✅ 昨日(きのう)学校(がっこう)を休(やす)んだのは頭(あたま)が痛(いた)かったからです。

When the element in focus is a reason rather than a thing, the clause has to be closed with から before です. Without it the sentence claims that the act of staying home was a headache.

～のは○○だ - cleft sentences · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 弟(おとうと)が皿(さら)を落(お)としました。 (b) 皿(さら)を落(お)としたのは弟(おとうと)です。

(a) reports the event as a whole, with the subject marked as the new piece of information. It works as an answer to "what happened?" and does not assume the listener knows anything yet.

(b) assumes the broken plate is already common knowledge and supplies the missing name. It answers "who did it?", it excludes everyone else, and depending on the situation it can sound like an accusation or like clearing someone's name. Use it when the event is settled and only the identity is in question.
