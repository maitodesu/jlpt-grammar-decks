Transitive & intransitive verbs

Meaning · paired verbs built on one root - one for "somebody does it to something", one for "it happens"

Japanese keeps two separate verbs where English is happy with one. 開(あ)ける is open something; 開(あ)く is (something) opens. English uses the word "open" for both and lets the sentence sort it out. Japanese does not: picking the wrong half of the pair forces the wrong particle, and the sentence stops being Japanese. This is vocabulary, but it behaves like grammar, which is why it gets a page.

The vocabulary word for the transitive half is 他動詞(たどうし) and for the intransitive half 自動詞(じどうし). You will see both in dictionaries.

Transitive & intransitive verbs · Nuance & register

Transitivity carries no politeness of its own. Both halves of a pair are equally usable in casual speech, in ます speech and in writing, and nothing about the choice makes you sound formal or rough. What it does carry is whether a person is in the picture, and that has social weight.

Japanese reaches for the intransitive far more often than English does. Where English says "we've decided the date" or "they found the key", ordinary Japanese says 日(ひ)にちが決(き)まりました and 鍵(かぎ)が見(み)つかりました. No one is named, no one is credited, and the sentence sounds neutral and adult rather than evasive. English speakers who default to the transitive end up sounding as though they are claiming credit for everything.

Transitive & intransitive verbs · Nuance & register (cont.)

The same choice is how you take or dodge responsibility. お皿(さら)が 割(わ)れました reports an event; お皿(さら)を割(わ)りました admits you did it. If you broke it and you are apologising, the intransitive will read as squirming. If you are simply telling someone the plate is in pieces, the transitive drags in a culprit nobody asked about.

Each half has its own resulting-state construction. The intransitive takes ～ている (閉(し)まっている, "is shut"); the transitive takes ～てある (閉(し)めてある, "has been shut"). That pairing has a page of its own, but it is worth knowing now that it exists - mixing the halves there is the single commonest N4 error.

A few verbs work both ways with no change of shape. 開(ひら)く, 増(ま)す and 吹(ふ)く each take が or を depending on the sentence. They are the exception; assume a pair until you find out otherwise.

開(あ)く vs 開(あ)ける - the particle follows the verb

Rule of thumb - 開(あ)く takes が and no object at all. 開(あ)ける takes を and needs somebody doing the opening. You cannot mix and match.

風(かぜ)で窓(まど)が開(あ)いた。
The window blew open in the wind.

暑(あつ)かったので窓(まど)を開(あ)けた。
It was hot, so I opened the window.

In the first sentence nothing has an agent - the wind is a cause, marked with で, not a doer. In the second there is a person, and the window is what they acted on. Writing ❌窓(まど)を開(あ)いた is the most frequent transitivity error there is, and it happens because English lets "opened" cover both.

開(あ)く vs 開(あ)ける - the particle follows the verb (cont.)

The same test works on every pair. Ask: does the sentence have somebody doing it to something?

話(はな)し合(あ)いは十時(じゅうじ)まで続(つづ)いた。
The discussion went on until ten.

反対(はんたい)されても、彼(かれ)は研究(けんきゅう)を続(つづ)けた。
Even when people objected, he carried on with his research.

Transitive & intransitive verbs · The intransitive vs the passive

Rule of thumb - the intransitive says it happened. The passive says somebody did it, and it landed on me.

English translates both with "was broken", which is exactly why the distinction is invisible to learners.

自転車(じてんしゃ)が壊(こわ)れた。
My bike broke.

自転車(じてんしゃ)を壊(こわ)された。
Somebody went and broke my bike.

Transitive & intransitive verbs · The intransitive vs the passive (cont.)

The first is bad luck: the bike gave out. The second has a culprit in it, unnamed but definitely there, and the speaker is the injured party - which is why the object keeps を even though the verb is passive. Choosing the passive where the intransitive would do makes you sound aggrieved. Choosing the intransitive when someone really did wreck your things makes you sound oddly forgiving.

財布(さいふ)が見(み)つかりました。
My wallet turned up.

財布(さいふ)を盗(ぬす)まれました。
My wallet was stolen.

Where English wants a person and Japanese does not

Rule of thumb - when no one needs naming, Japanese states the event with the intransitive rather than reaching for a passive.

English has no intransitive verb for "decide", so it borrows the passive: "the date has been decided". Japanese has 決(き)まる sitting there ready, so the passive is unnecessary and would sound heavy.

来月(らいげつ)の予定(よてい)が決(き)まりました。
Next month's schedule has been settled.

なくした鍵(かぎ)が机(つくえ)の下(した)から見(み)つかりました。
The key I lost turned up under the desk.

Where English wants a person and Japanese does not (cont.)

This is the reason so much everyday Japanese has no visible subject. 連絡(れんらく)が入(はい)りました ("word came in"), 準備(じゅんび)が 整(ととの)いました ("everything's ready"), 会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わりました ("the meeting's over") - all intransitive, all agentless, all completely standard in a workplace where naming who did what would be beside the point.

Transitive & intransitive verbs · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 窓(まど)を開(あ)きました。 → ✅ 窓(まど)を開(あ)けました。／窓(まど)が開(あ)きました。
    開(あ)く is intransitive and cannot take a を object. Either switch the verb or switch the particle - but the two must agree.

  • ❌ 電気(でんき)が消(け)しました。 → ✅ 電気(でんき)を消(け)しました。／電気(でんき)が消(き)えました。
    Same error in the other direction. Note the readings too: 消(け)す and 消(き)える share a kanji and not a stem.

  • ❌ 授業(じゅぎょう)を始(はじ)まります。 → ✅ 授業(じゅぎょう)を始(はじ)めます。
    A teacher starting a lesson is doing something to the lesson. 始(はじ)まる would need 授業(じゅぎょう)が and no actor.

Transitive & intransitive verbs · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ かばんから財布(さいふ)を落(お)ちました。 → ✅ かばんから財布(さいふ)を落(お)としました。
    Dropping something is transitive. 落(お)ちる is what the wallet does on its own: 財布(さいふ)が落(お)ちました.

  • ❌ ドアが閉(し)めています。 → ✅ ドアが閉(し)まっています。
    With が you need the intransitive. 閉(し)めている would mean somebody is in the middle of closing it, and that person would need を.

Transitive & intransitive verbs · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) パスワードを変(か)えました。 (b) パスワードが変(か)わりました。

(a) uses the transitive: somebody - normally the speaker - deliberately changed the password. It answers "what did you do?" and it puts a person on record.

(b) uses the intransitive: the password is now different, and the sentence says nothing about who or why. It is what a system message says, and it is also what you say when you want the change reported without anyone being credited or blamed.

～てある

Meaning · something has been done, on purpose, and the result is still there

～てある takes a transitive verb and reports the state it left behind. Somebody did it - that is built into the meaning - but the sentence deliberately declines to say who. What you are describing is the leftover: the note is written, the seats are booked, the beer is in the fridge, and none of that happened by itself.

～てある · Formation

ある is the ordinary existence verb, so it conjugates exactly as you already know it: 書(か)いてある → 書(か)いてあります → 書(か)いてあった → 書(か)いてない.

Rule of thumb - ～てある only takes verbs that have an object. If the verb takes が and describes something happening by itself, it wants ～ている instead, and there is no ❌倒(たお)れてある.

Note - the verb also has to leave a result you can point at, arranged by someone for a reason. 貼(は)る, 置(お)く, 書(か)く, 入(い)れる, 決(き)める and 予約(よやく)する all qualify. Verbs whose result disappears into the doer - 食(た)べる, 見(み)る, 聞(き)く - sound odd with てある unless the situation genuinely makes them preparation.

～てある · Nuance & register

～てある is register-neutral. てあります is its polite form and there is no casual contraction of the auxiliary itself; what does contract is the negative, where 書(か)いてありません becomes 書(か)いてない in everyday speech. 書(か)いてない is extremely common and entirely normal - do not read it as a different pattern.

The invisible person is the whole point. ～てある is how Japanese says "this was seen to" without naming an agent, so it turns up constantly in notices, in handovers, and in the answer to "is everything ready?". Because the agent is implied rather than stated, てある also carries a faint sense of for you or for later: エアコンが付(つ)けてあります is not just a fact about the room, it tells you someone thought of you.

～てある · Nuance & register (cont.)

It quietly commits you to intention. Saying 窓(まど)が開(あ)けてあります claims that somebody opened the window on purpose. If the window is simply open and you have no idea why, that claim is wrong and you want 開(あ)いています instead.

It reads as reporting, not as doing. てある is stative, like ある itself, so it does not take commands, invitations or intentions: there is no ❌置(お)いてあってください and no ❌置(お)いてあろう. When you need to ask somebody to arrange something, the pattern you want is ～ておく.

On signs and in formal writing you will also meet the passive - 禁煙(きんえん)と書(か)かれています. It says almost the same thing but sounds more official and more distant. ～てある is the everyday version.

～てある · ～てある vs ～ている

Rule of thumb - the transitive verb takes てある; the intransitive verb takes ている. Both describe the same room. てある adds that a person arranged it that way.

This is the pair the whole transitivity system exists for. Take 閉(し)まる (intransitive) and 閉(し)める (transitive):

門(もん)が閉(し)まっている。
The gate is shut.

門(もん)が閉(し)めてある。
The gate has been shut. (somebody closed it, deliberately)

～てある · ～てある vs ～ている (cont.)

You are looking at the same gate. The first sentence describes it and stops. The second says a human being is behind the situation - which matters, because it tells you the gate is shut for a reason and possibly that you should not open it.

There is a third member of the set, and forgetting it is where learners come unstuck. The transitive verb with ている is not a state at all - it is the action in progress:

係(かかり)の人(ひと)が門(もん)を閉(し)めている。
The attendant is closing the gate.

So: 閉(し)まっている = shut, nobody in view. 閉(し)めてある = shut, somebody saw to it. 閉(し)めている = somebody is closing it right now. Three sentences, one gate, and the verb plus the auxiliary decide which you get.

～てある · ～てある vs ～ておく

Rule of thumb - ～ておく is the doing; ～てある is what you find afterwards. You do the ておく, and later somebody walks in and reports the てある.

明日(あした)飲(の)む分(ぶん)のコーヒーを買(か)っておいた。
I bought the coffee for tomorrow ahead of time.

明日(あした)飲(の)む分(ぶん)のコーヒーは買(か)ってある。
Tomorrow's coffee is bought and waiting.

The world is identical; the camera angle is not. The first is a report of your action and answers "what did you do?". The second is a report of the state of the kitchen and answers "are we out of coffee?".

～てある · ～てある vs ～ておく (cont.)

The grammatical consequence is sharper than the feel. ておく is a live action verb and behaves like one - 買(か)っておこう, 買(か)っておいてください, 買(か)っておかなきゃ. てある can do none of that, because a state cannot be volunteered for or ordered.

～てある · が vs を with ～てある

Rule of thumb - both occur. が presents the state; を keeps the speaker's own action in view. が is much commoner, and it is the safe answer.

ホワイトボードに予定(よてい)が書(か)いてある。
The schedule is up on the whiteboard.

ホワイトボードに予定(よてい)を書(か)いてある。
I've written the schedule up on the whiteboard.

With が, the object has been promoted to subject and the sentence is pure description - this is what you say when you notice something, or when you tell somebody where to look. With を, the sentence keeps its object and therefore keeps a doer hovering behind it, normally the speaker; the effect shades towards "I've taken care of it", which is ておく territory.

～てある · が vs を with ～てある (cont.)

If you are unsure, use が. The を version is fine but narrow, and it sounds wrong when the speaker clearly did not do the deed: ❌誰(だれ)かが窓(まど)を開(あ)けてある.

～てある · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 木(き)が倒(たお)れてある。 → ✅ 木(き)が倒(たお)れている。
    倒(たお)れる is intransitive, and nobody knocks a tree over on purpose. Events without an arranger take ている.

  • ❌ 窓(まど)が開(あ)いてある。 → ✅ 窓(まど)が開(あ)けてある。
    てある needs the transitive half of the pair. 開(あ)く + てある mixes the two systems and is simply not a form.

  • ❌ ドアを閉(し)めてあってください。 → ✅ ドアを閉(し)めておいてください。
    てある is a state and cannot be requested. Asking somebody to arrange something is ～ておく.

  • ❌ この本(ほん)はもう読(よ)んであります。（meaning "I've already read it"）→ ✅ この本(ほん)はもう読(よ)みました。
    てある reports a prepared state that someone can now make use of, not your personal experience of having done something. Reading a book leaves no arranged result for anyone to find.

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) かぎが掛(か)かっています。 (b) かぎが掛(か)けてあります。

(a) uses the intransitive 掛(か)かる: the door is locked, and that is all the sentence claims. It might have locked itself; you are just describing what you found.

(b) uses the transitive 掛(か)ける with てある: somebody locked it, on purpose, and the lock is still on. It implies a decision - the room was secured, and probably for a reason you should respect.

～てみる

Meaning · do something and see what happens - try it out

～てみる does not mean "attempt". The action really is carried out; what is uncertain is the result. You eat the thing to find out what it tastes like, you press the button to find out what it does, you ask the question to find out what the answer is. English "try" covers this and a second meaning that ～てみる cannot express, which is where most of the trouble comes from.

I thought it was nowhere to be found, but when I opened the desk drawer to check, the lost key was sitting right there.

～てみる · Nuance & register

～てみる is register-neutral in itself, but it is one of the most useful politeness tools in the language, because it converts an instruction into an invitation. 読(よ)んでください tells somebody to read; 読(よ)んでみてください offers them the chance and leaves the verdict to them. Upward, the same idea takes a longer shape: ～てみていただけますか, ～てみてはいかがでしょうか.

The past form invites a follow-up. ～てみた almost always wants a result after it, because the whole point was to find something out: 食(た)べてみたけど、思(おも)ったより甘(あま)かった. A bare 食(た)べてみました with nothing after it sounds unfinished, like telling half a story.

It needs an action you control. Weather, feelings and involuntary events cannot be sampled: ❌雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ってみる, ❌疲(つか)れてみる, ❌わかってみる. If the subject cannot decide to do it, てみる is out.

～てみる · Nuance & register (cont.)

It can sound presumptuous in the wrong direction. Suggesting that a superior "give something a try" implies you know better and that they are the one experimenting. To a customer or a boss, prefer 一度(いちど)ご覧(らん)ください or a plain polite request over ～てみてください for anything they are obliged to do.

Do not confuse it with ～てみせる. That is a separate auxiliary meaning "do it and show you" or, with a volitional, "I'll manage it come what may": 今度(こんど)こそ合格(ごうかく)してみせる.

～てみる · ～てみる vs ～ようとする

Rule of thumb - てみる means the action happened and you are waiting on the outcome. ようとする means you were making the effort, and it may never have come off at all.

その薬(くすり)を飲(の)んでみたが、ぜんぜん効(き)かなかった。
I tried the medicine, but it didn't do a thing.

その薬(くすり)を飲(の)もうとしたが、苦(にが)くて飲(の)めなかった。
I tried to take the medicine, but it was too bitter to get down.

In the first sentence the medicine went down; only its effect disappointed. In the second it never got past the speaker's lips. English uses "tried" for both, so learners reach for てみる in situations where the action failed to occur - and that is a contradiction, because てみる presupposes it occurred.

～てみる · ～てみる vs ～ようとする (cont.)

A quick test: if you can add "…and here is what I found out", you want てみる. If you have to add "…but I couldn't", you want ようとする.

～てみる · ～てみる vs ～たことがある

Rule of thumb - てみる is the occasion when you did it to find out. たことがある is the bare fact that at some point in your life you have done it.

なっとうを食(た)べてみました。
I gave natto a try.

なっとうを食(た)べたことがあります。
I've eaten natto before.

English "I've tried natto" is the second of these, not the first, and that mismatch sends learners the wrong way constantly. The first sentence is an event with a time and a purpose behind it; the second is a line on your CV of experiences.

The two stack happily when you mean both at once:

～てみる · ～てみる vs ～たことがある (cont.)

一度(いちど)だけ馬(うま)に乗(の)ってみたことがあります。
I've had a go at riding a horse exactly once.

～てみる · ～てみてください vs ～てください

Rule of thumb - てください tells. てみてください offers. Same request, completely different pressure on the listener.

このボタンを押(お)してください。
Press this button.

うまく動(うご)かないときは、このボタンを押(お)してみてください。
If it isn't working properly, try pressing this button.

The second version admits that the speaker is not certain it will help, which is exactly what makes it feel considerate: the listener is being given an option, not an order. That is also its limit. For something the listener genuinely has to do - a form to sign, a passport to hand over - てみて is wrong, because it suggests the outcome is up for discussion: ❌パスポートを見(み)せてみてください.

～てみる · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 食(た)べるてみる → ✅ 食(た)べてみる
    みる attaches to the て-form, never to the dictionary form.

  • ❌ 漢字(かんじ)を書(か)いて見(み)ました。 → ✅ 漢字(かんじ)を書(か)いてみました。
    The auxiliary is written in kana. Using the kanji turns it back into "looked at", which is not what you mean.

  • ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ってみます。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るかもしれません。
    You can only sample something you choose to do. Weather does not experiment with itself.

  • ❌ 昨日(きのう)、新(あたら)しいカメラを買(か)ってみました。（as a plain report）→ ✅ 昨日(きのう)、新(あたら)しいカメラを買(か)いました。
    てみる adds "to see how it would go", so on an ordinary purchase it sounds as though you bought the camera as an experiment and are still deciding.

～てみる · Practice 1

1. おいしいかどうかわからないので、少(すこ)し食(た)べ____ます。

少(すこ)し食(た)べてみます。

The reason given is that the outcome is unknown, which is precisely the job of てみる: you carry out the action in order to find out. Plain 食(た)べます would lose the "and then I'll know" that the first clause sets up.

～てみる · Practice 2

The window never moved, so the action did not take place - and てみる requires that it did. ようとする is the pattern for an effort that came to nothing.

～てみる · Practice 3

てみたい is てみる plus the desire ending, and it is the natural way to say you want to sample an experience rather than simply wanting a thing. 住(す)みたい alone would state a settled wish to live there; 住(す)んでみたい keeps the sense of finding out what it is like.

～てみる · Practice 4

～てみる · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 京都(きょうと)に行(い)ったことがあります。 (b) 京都(きょうと)に行(い)ってみました。

(a) is a statement about your past experience: at some point you have been to Kyoto. It carries no information about why you went or how it turned out, and it is the right answer to "have you ever been?".

(b) is about one particular occasion, and it frames the trip as something you did to see what Kyoto was like. It practically demands a follow-up - どうでしたか - because the whole point of てみる is the verdict that comes after.

～ておく / ～とく

Meaning · do it now so that later is taken care of - or leave it as it stands

～ておく frames an action as setup. The speaker is not merely doing the thing; they are doing it now so that some later moment goes smoothly. Out of that comes a second, quieter use: if something is already in a useful state, ～ておく means deliberately leaving it there instead of undoing it.

～ておく / ～とく · Nuance & register

とく is not slang; it is how people talk. やっとく, 見(み)とく, 言(い)っとくけど, 覚(おぼ)えとけ - the contraction runs across every age and both genders, and refusing to use it will make your casual speech sound stiff. It stays out of writing, out of ます speech and out of exams, but it belongs in your ear from day one.

As a request, ておいて carries an edge. It hands somebody a task and closes the topic - 「じゃ、よろしく。予約(よやく)しといて」 is what a manager says to a junior. Aimed upward it is too brisk; use 予約(よやく)しておいていただけますか or 予約(よやく)をお願(ねが)いできますでしょうか instead.

There is a self-interested tinge to it. ておく says the action clears your own path, so it can sound slightly calculating when the beneficiary is somebody else: 「言(い)っておきますが…」 is a common preface meaning "just so we're clear", and it is a mild warning shot, not a favour.

～ておく / ～とく · Nuance & register (cont.)

The negative splits two ways. 買(か)っておかない is "I'm not going to buy it in advance"; 買(か)わないでおく is "I'm going to deliberately not buy it (and leave things that way)". The second is the one people mean when they choose to hold back - 聞(き)かないでおく, "I'll not ask".

In polite speech it is completely ordinary, and it appears constantly in service Japanese: お預(あず)かりしておきます, 確認(かくにん)しておきます. Nothing about ておく is casual by itself - only the contraction is.

The two jobs of ～ておく - get it done ahead, or leave it as it is

Rule of thumb - if the て-verb changes something, ておく means do it now for later. If the て-verb has already produced the state you want, ておく means don't undo it.

会議(かいぎ)の前(まえ)に席(せき)を決(き)めておく。
We'll settle the seating before the meeting.

暑(あつ)いので、窓(まど)は開(あ)けておいてください。
It's hot, so please leave the window open.

There is only one grammar here. The "leave it" reading appears when the state is already in place and closing it again would take an action - so telling somebody 開(あ)けておいて is telling them to keep choosing the open window. English needs two different verbs, "set up" and "leave", where Japanese needs one.

The two jobs of ～ておく - get it done ahead, or leave it as it is (cont.)

The clearest case of the second reading is the fixed phrase そのままにしておく:

散(ち)らかっているけど、今日(きょう)はそのままにしておこう。
It's a mess, but let's just leave it for today.

～ておく / ～とく · ～ておく vs ～てある

Rule of thumb - ておく is what you do; てある is what somebody finds afterwards. ておく is a live action verb, てある is a state.

飲(の)み物(もの)を用意(ようい)しておきました。
I got the drinks ready.

飲(の)み物(もの)は用意(ようい)してあります。
The drinks are ready - somebody's seen to it.

The first answers "what did you do?"; the second answers "is everything sorted?". The grammatical fallout is the sharper half of the distinction. Because ておく is an action, it takes everything an action takes - 買(か)っておこう, 買(か)っておいてください, 買(か)っておかなければ. てある takes none of them, so the moment you need a command, an invitation or an intention, ておく is your only option.

～ておく / ～とく · ～ておく vs ～てある (cont.)

Note also the particles. ておく keeps the original verb's frame, so the object stays を: 窓(まど)を開(あ)けておく. てある normally promotes that object to が: 窓(まど)が開(あ)けてある.

～ておく / ～とく · ～ておく vs ～てしまう

Rule of thumb - ておく looks forward: it's done so the future is easier. てしまう looks at the finish line: it's done, over, off the list.

夜(よる)のうちにレポートを書(か)いておく。
I'll write the report tonight so it's ready.

夜(よる)のうちにレポートを書(か)いてしまう。
I'll get the report finished off tonight.

In practice the same evening produces both sentences, but the reason differs. ておく is chosen because something comes next - the report is needed tomorrow. てしまう is chosen because you want the thing behind you, complete, with nothing left over.

～ておく / ～とく · ～ておく vs ～てしまう (cont.)

Two further separations. てしまう can carry regret, and ておく never can: there is no way to say ておく about something you wish had not happened. And てしまう insists on completion - you finish the whole report - whereas ておく is satisfied with whatever preparation is enough: 少(すこ)しだけ書(か)いておく is fine, while ❌少(すこ)しだけ書(か)いてしまう fights itself.

～ておく / ～とく · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 買(か)うておく → ✅ 買(か)っておく
    おく attaches to the て-form, not the dictionary form.

  • ❌ まだホテルを予約(よやく)しておきません。 → ✅ まだホテルを予約(よやく)していません。
    "I haven't booked yet" is the plain ていない. The negative of ておく means you have decided against preparing, which is a different claim.

  • ❌ 窓(まど)が開(あ)けておく。 → ✅ 窓(まど)を開(あ)けておく。
    ておく does not change the verb's particles. The object of a transitive verb stays on を; promotion to が belongs to てある.

  • ❌ 先生(せんせい)、これを読(よ)んどいてください。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)、こちらを読(よ)んでおいていただけますか。
    Two problems at once: どく is a casual contraction, and ておいてください hands out tasks. Neither goes upward.

～ておく / ～とく · Practice 1

1. 友(とも)だちが来(く)る前(まえ)に、部屋(へや)を片(かた)づけて____。

The tidying is being done because something is coming afterwards, which is exactly the preparation reading of ておく. Plain 片(かた)づけます would report the action with no forward-looking purpose attached.

～ておく / ～とく · Practice 3

The charging exists only to serve the trip that follows, which is what ておく marks. In speech this would very often come out as 充電(じゅうでん)しとく, and the meaning is identical.

～ておく / ～とく · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 旅行(りょこう)のことはまだ家族(かぞく)に話(はな)しておきません。

～ておく / ～とく · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 電気(でんき)をつけておきます。 (b) 電気(でんき)をつけます。

(b) simply reports switching the light on. It says nothing about why, and it could be about the next second.

(a) says the light is being switched on for what comes later - so that the person arriving home tonight is not fumbling in the dark, or so that the room is ready for the guests. Depending on the situation it can also mean the light is already on and you are leaving it that way rather than turning it off.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう

Meaning · finish something off completely - or do something you wish you hadn't

～てしまう carries two loads at once, and learners usually notice only one of them. The first is completion: the action is taken all the way to the end, with nothing left over. The second is regret: it happened, it cannot be undone, and you are not pleased about it. The same three syllables do both jobs, and the situation tells you which.

その本(ほん)、おもしろくて一日(いちにち)で読(よ)んじゃった。
That book was so good I got through it in a day.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Examples (cont.)

急(いそ)いでいて、まだ送(おく)っていない大事(だいじ)なメールを消(け)してしまいました。
I was in a rush and deleted an important email I hadn't even sent yet.

練習(れんしゅう)を休(やす)んでいるうちに、弾(ひ)き方(かた)を忘(わす)れてしまった。
I took a break from practising and ended up forgetting how to play.

昼(ひる)までにこの仕事(しごと)を終(お)わらせてしまいたいんですが、手伝(てつだ)っていただけませんか。
I'd like to get this job finished off before lunch - could you give me a hand?

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Nuance & register

ちゃった is one of the most-heard endings in casual Japanese. It is neutral between men and women and across ages, and it is the default in conversation: 寝坊(ねぼう)しちゃった, 壊(こわ)しちゃった, 買(か)っちゃった. Polite speech uses the full てしまいました, and business apologies stretch it further: 遅(おく)れてしまい、申(もう)し訳(わけ)ございません.

ちまう / じまう is a different register again - rough, blunt, distinctly masculine and slightly old-fashioned. You will meet it in films and manga and in older men's speech. Recognise it; do not adopt it.

Regret here is softer than "regret" sounds in English. てしまう does not mean you are wracked with guilt; it means this is not how I wanted it. That is why it is the standard way to own up to something. 「壊(こわ)しちゃいました」 sounds honest and human, while a flat 「壊(こわ)しました」 can come across as oddly cold, as though you broke it and do not much mind.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Nuance & register (cont.)

The completion reading is the one that survives into plans. With the volitional, with たい, and in invitations, only the "get it finished" sense is available: やってしまおう, 片(かた)づけてしまいたい. Nobody volunteers to regret something.

Write しまう in kana. The kanji 仕舞(しま)う belongs to the independent verb "put away, stow" - which is the image the auxiliary comes from: you finish with the thing and shut it away.

In neutral writing it is an intrusion. A news report or a lab record uses the plain past, because てしまう editorialises. Seeing it in an otherwise flat text tells you the writer has an opinion.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Completion vs regret - telling them apart

Rule of thumb - look at the verb and at whether you chose it. A verb you control, plus a quantity or a deadline, gives completion. An outcome that happened to you gives regret.

三日(みっか)でこのドラマを全部(ぜんぶ)見(み)てしまった。
I got through the whole drama in three days.

母(はは)にもらった大切(たいせつ)なお皿(さら)を割(わ)ってしまった。
I broke the plate my mother gave me.

The first sentence has a time span and a total, and watching a drama is something you decide to do - so てしまう measures the finish. The second has a valued object and a bad outcome, so it measures the damage.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Completion vs regret - telling them apart (cont.)

Certain verbs point one way almost automatically. 忘(わす)れる, 転(ころ)ぶ, 落(お)とす, 遅(おく)れる, 寝坊(ねぼう)する, 死(し)ぬ - nobody plans these, so てしまう on them is regret. Meanwhile 全部(ぜんぶ), 最後(さいご)まで, 一日(いちにち)で and a volitional ending pull hard towards completion.

And sometimes both readings sit in one sentence. 全部(ぜんぶ)食(た)べちゃった can be a satisfied "I polished off the lot" or a sheepish "oh no, I've eaten all of it". Japanese speakers hear no ambiguity because the tone of voice and the situation settle it - which is the honest answer to "how do I tell?".

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · ～てしまう vs ～終(お)わる

Rule of thumb - ～終(お)わる is the neutral fact that the action reached its end. ～てしまう adds an attitude: relief, thoroughness, or dismay.

この本(ほん)を読(よ)み終(お)わったら、貸(か)してください。
Once you've finished reading this book, lend it to me.

貸(か)した本(ほん)を一晩(ひとばん)で読(よ)んでしまったそうだ。
Apparently he polished off the book I lent him in a single night.

Three practical differences. 終(お)わる attaches to the ます-stem (読(よ)み終(お)わる), not the て-form. It is purely aspectual and can never mean regret. And it needs a verb with a natural finishing point, so ❌忘(わす)れ終(お)わる and ❌死(し)に終(お)わる are impossible, while てしまう takes both quite happily.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · ～てしまった vs the plain past

Rule of thumb - the plain past reports. てしまった comments.

さいふをなくした。
I lost my wallet.

さいふをなくしてしまった。
I've gone and lost my wallet.

Both are true and both are grammatical; the second tells you how the speaker feels about it. In apologies the auxiliary is close to obligatory, because leaving it out makes the event sound like weather:

待(ま)たせてしまって、すみませんでした。
I'm sorry to have kept you waiting.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · ～てしまった vs the plain past (cont.)

The reverse holds too. Where nothing is regrettable and nothing is being finished off, adding てしまう is a mistake of tone rather than of grammar: 昨日(きのう)映画(えいが)を見(み)てしまいました would leave a listener wondering what went wrong at the cinema.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 食(た)べるてしまう → ✅ 食(た)べてしまう
    しまう attaches to the て-form, not the dictionary form.

  • ❌ 読(よ)んでちゃった → ✅ 読(よ)んじゃった
    A で て-form contracts to じゃう, not ちゃう. The rule follows the same voicing split you already learned for the て-form itself.

  • ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、遅(おく)れちゃってすみません。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、遅(おく)れてしまい、申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません。
    The contraction is casual. An apology to a superior needs the full form, and ideally the stiffer 申(もう)し訳(わけ)ありません as well.

  • ❌ 宿題(しゅくだい)を忘(わす)れ終(お)わった。 → ✅ 宿題(しゅくだい)を忘(わす)れてしまった。
    終(お)わる needs an action with a finish line, and forgetting has none. This is also exactly the meaning てしまう exists for.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Practice 1

Leaving the umbrella behind was an accident with a result the speaker regrets, so てしまう is what carries that. The plain 置(お)いてきました would be a flat report with no sense that anything went wrong.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Practice 2

終(お)わる describes an action coming to its end as a fact and is odd in the volitional - you do not decide to reach an endpoint, you decide to get something finished. てしまおう is the standard way to say "let's knock this out".

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Practice 3

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Practice 4

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) この漫画(まんが)を全部(ぜんぶ)読(よ)みました。 (b) この漫画(まんが)を全部(ぜんぶ)読(よ)んでしまいました。

(a) is a plain statement of fact: you have read the whole series, and the sentence takes no position on it.

(b) adds the speaker's attitude. Depending on the situation it reads either as "I got through the lot" - thoroughness, maybe a little pride at the speed - or as "I've gone and read them all", the tone you would use if you had meant to ration them or should have been studying instead.

～ていく

Meaning · do it and go - and, on the time axis, keep changing from now on

One idea sits behind everything ～ていく does: movement away from where the speaker is standing. Physically, that means the subject does the て-verb and then leaves, or takes something along. On the time axis, "away from here" becomes "on into the future", so a change that starts now and carries forward also takes ～ていく.

～ていく · Formation

いく is the ordinary motion verb and conjugates as it always does, including the irregular て-form 行(い)って.

My place is a long way from the station, so I make a point of buying lunch on the way.

日本語(にほんご)は時間(じかん)がかかるが、毎日(まいにち)少(すこ)しずつ続(つづ)けていけば、いつか話(はな)せるようになる。
Japanese takes time, but if you keep at it a little each day, one day you'll be able to speak it.

～ていく · Nuance & register

～ていく is register-neutral. ていきます is its polite form and nothing about either sounds stiff or rough.

The casual contraction is てく. 持(も)ってく, 連(つ)れてく, やってく - the い simply drops. The past goes further in relaxed speech, where 持(も)っていった becomes 持(も)ってった. Both belong to conversation only.

Everything depends on where the speaker imagines standing. If you are at home and the party is elsewhere, you 持(も)っていく the wine. If you are already at the party, the same bottle 持(も)ってくる - and the mistake of choosing by the map rather than by the viewpoint is the classic one. The speaker's position can also be a place they merely identify with: a receptionist says お持(も)ちします about their own building.

～ていく · Nuance & register (cont.)

In speeches and public commitments ～ていきます is close to a fixed formula. 「これからも努力(どりょく)していきます」, 「安全(あんぜん)を守(まも)って いきます」 - the auxiliary projects the promise forward, and leaving it out makes the sentence sound like a one-off rather than a commitment.

A past ていった in the change sense is perfectly normal. It simply places the viewpoint at a past moment and lets the change run forward from there: 人(ひと)が少(すこ)しずつ減(へ)っていった. The auxiliary is about direction, not about tense.

～ていく · ～ていく vs ～てくる - which way from the speaker

Rule of thumb - いく = away from me. くる = towards me. The choice is not about the map; it is about where the speaker is standing when they say it.

おみやげを持(も)っていく。
I'll take a present with me. (to where they are)

おみやげを持(も)ってくる。
I'll bring a present back. (to where we are)

The same split runs through every pair built this way: 連(つ)れていく / 連(つ)れてくる for people, 送(おく)っていく / 送(おく)ってくる for seeing somebody off or home, 走(はし)っていく / 走(はし)ってくる for a figure receding or approaching.

～ていく · ～ていく vs ～てくる - which way from the speaker (cont.)

犬(いぬ)が門(もん)のほうへ走(はし)っていった。
The dog ran off towards the gate.

If you are on the phone to somebody in another city and you say you will visit, Japanese takes their position as the destination, so the verb is 行(い)く where English says "I'll come" - the same instinct that makes ていく the right auxiliary there.

～ていく · ～ていく vs ～に行(い)く

Rule of thumb - ～に行(い)く: you go in order to do it, and the doing happens at the far end. ～ていく: you do it first, then go.

パンを買(か)いに行(い)く。
I'm going out to buy bread.

パンを買(か)っていく。
I'll pick up bread on the way.

The order of events is the whole difference. In the first, the going comes first and the shop is the destination. In the second, the buying comes first - at a shop you pass - and then you continue to wherever you were actually headed, bread in hand.

～ていく · ～ていく vs ～に行(い)く (cont.)

Note the forms as well: ～に行(い)く attaches to the ます-stem (買(か)い + に), while ～ていく attaches to the て-form. Mixing them up produces ❌買(か)ってに行(い)く, which is not a form at all.

～ていく · "Do and go" vs "keep on" - telling the two ～ていく apart

Rule of thumb - a concrete action somebody performs gives the physical reading. A verb of change (なる, 増(ふ)える, 減(へ)る, 変(か)わる) or of continued activity (続(つづ)ける, がんばる) gives the time-axis reading.

子(こ)どもを保育園(ほいくえん)に送(おく)っていった。
I dropped the kids off at nursery on my way.

町(まち)の様子(ようす)は少(すこ)しずつ変(か)わっていった。
The look of the town changed little by little.

There is no journey anywhere in the second sentence. Nothing physically goes anywhere; what moves is time, and the change is being followed forward from the moment the speaker has in mind.

～ていく · "Do and go" vs "keep on" - telling the two ～ていく apart (cont.)

The adverbs are a reliable clue. これから, 今後(こんご), だんだん and 少(すこ)しずつ point forward and pull the time reading; a destination marked with に or へ pulls the physical one.

人口(じんこう)はこれからも減(へ)っていくと言(い)われている。
The population is said to be set on continuing to fall.

～ていく · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 持(も)つていく → ✅ 持(も)っていく
    いく attaches to the て-form. 持(も)つ is a つ verb, so the て-form is 持(も)って.

  • ❌（arriving at the host's house）ケーキを持(も)っていきました。 → ✅ ケーキを持(も)ってきました。
    You are already at the destination, so the cake has come to where you both are. Deixis is judged from where you stand as you speak, not from where you set off.

  • ❌ 来週(らいしゅう)から寒(さむ)くなってきます。 → ✅ 来週(らいしゅう)から寒(さむ)くなっていきます。
    A change running into the future takes ていく. てくる looks back at a change that has already reached the present.

  • ❌ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていきました。（meaning "it started raining"）→ ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ってきました。
    Something newly arriving at you is てくる. ていく would suggest the rain was moving off away from the speaker.

～てくる

Meaning · do it and come back - and, on the time axis, a change that has reached now

～てくる is the mirror image of ～ていく: movement towards where the speaker is. Three everyday uses fall out of that one idea. Someone does the て-verb while approaching; or they go off, do it, and return; or - because "towards here" on the time axis means "up to now" - a change has been building and has just arrived at the present.

～てくる · Nuance & register

～てくる is register-neutral, and てきます is its ordinary polite form. The humble equivalent てまいります belongs to business and announcements - 行(い)ってまいります, 確認(かくにん)してまいります - and sounds noticeably formal.

The round trip is why 「行(い)ってきます」 is the phrase you say leaving the house. Literally "I'll go and come back", it promises a return, which is exactly what the household reply 「行(い)ってらっしゃい」 answers. Any errand can take the same shape: 見(み)てきます ("I'll go and have a look"), 聞(き)いてきます ("I'll go and ask").

The change reading normally surfaces in the past. てきた is far commoner than てくる here, because you notice a change only once it has arrived: 眠(ねむ)くなってきた, 見(み)えてきた, 慣(な)れてきた. The state it announces is present even though the verb is past - this catches learners out regularly.

～てくる · Nuance & register (cont.)

With verbs of feeling and perception it means a feeling emerging. 思(おも)えてくる, 気(き)がしてくる, 腹(はら)が立(た)ってくる - the sensation was not there, and now it is welling up. This is one of the most natural-sounding uses and one learners almost never reach for.

There is no tidy contraction to match ていく → てく, because てくる is already short. What you will hear instead is てくる stacked with other auxiliaries in casual speech - 買(か)ってきちゃった, 買(か)ってきといて - and the request form clipped to 買(か)ってきて with the ください simply dropped.

～てくる · The three jobs of ～てくる

Rule of thumb - the て-verb tells you which reading you have: a verb of motion gives approach, a verb of doing gives a round trip, a verb of change or perception gives the time axis.

子(こ)どもが部屋(へや)に入(はい)ってきた。
A child came into the room.

切手(きって)を買(か)ってきます。
I'll go and buy some stamps. (and come back)

最近(さいきん)、日(ひ)が長(なが)くなってきた。
The days have been getting longer lately.

～てくる · The three jobs of ～てくる (cont.)

Buying stamps involves no movement towards the speaker at all - the goods come back with you, and the auxiliary marks the return leg of the errand. That is why てくる on an action verb so often translates as "go and…", which looks backwards until you notice that the sentence is spoken from the place you will return to.

Note - the round-trip reading needs somewhere to come back to. Said on your own doorstep, 走(はし)ってきます means "I'm off for a run (and back)". Said watching someone approach, 走(はし)ってきます is plain motion. Nothing in the grammar distinguishes them; the situation does.

～てくる · ～てくる vs ～ている

Rule of thumb - ている is the state now. てくる is the arrival at that state - it was not so, and now it is.

雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)ってきた。
It's started to snow.

雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)っている。
It's snowing.

The second is what you say when somebody asks about the weather: you look out and describe what is going on. The first is what you say the moment the first flakes appear - the news is not the snow but the change, and you were there for it.

The same split runs through anything gradual:

～てくる · ～てくる vs ～ている (cont.)

おなかがすいてきた。
I'm starting to get hungry.

おなかがすいている。
I'm hungry.

Because てくる reports a change you have witnessed, it does not work for states that have simply always been true. ❌背(せ)が高(たか)くなってきました said about an adult's fixed height is odd; said about a growing child it is perfect.

～てくる · ～てくる vs ～ていく on the time axis

Rule of thumb - draw an arrow at now. てきた runs from the past into the present; ていく runs from the present into the future.

去年(きょねん)から会員(かいいん)が増(ふ)えてきました。
Membership has been growing since last year.

来年(らいねん)からも会員(かいいん)は増(ふ)えていくでしょう。
Membership will probably go on growing next year as well.

The time expressions give it away every time. 昔(むかし)から, 最近(さいきん), これまで and だんだん…てきた pull てくる; これから, 今後(こんご) and a prediction ending pull ていく. A sentence with both is a standard rhetorical move:

～てくる · ～てくる vs ～ていく on the time axis (cont.)

これまで続(つづ)けてきたことを、これからも続(つづ)けていきたい。
I want to keep on doing what we have been doing up to now.

Note that this contrast lives entirely on the time axis. In the physical uses, てくる and ていく are about direction in space, and no amount of thinking about tense will help you choose.

～てくる · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 買(か)うてくる → ✅ 買(か)ってくる
    くる attaches to the て-form, not the dictionary form.

  • ❌（leaving the house）行(い)っていきます。 → ✅ 行(い)ってきます。
    You intend to return, and the whole point of the phrase is the return, so it has to be てくる. This is a fixed expression - learn it whole.

  • ❌ 昔(むかし)から日本人(にほんじん)は魚(さかな)を食(た)べていきました。 → ✅ 昔(むかし)から日本人(にほんじん)は魚(さかな)を食(た)べてきました。
    昔(むかし)から runs from the past up to the present, which is てくる territory. ていく would push the eating off into the future.

  • ❌ さっき昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べていきました。（meaning "I went out and had lunch"）→ ✅ さっき昼(ひる)ごはんを食(た)べてきました。
    You left, ate, and came back to where you are speaking - a round trip, so てくる. ていく would mean you ate and then carried on somewhere else.

～てくる · Practice 1

1. 急(きゅう)に空(そら)が暗(くら)くなって、雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っ____。

雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ってきた。

The sky darkening and then the rain arriving is a change that has just reached the present moment, which is exactly the てくる reading. Note the past tense: てきた announces a state that is true right now.

～てくる · Practice 2

2. 田中(たなか)さんに資料(しりょう)を渡(わた)したいので、席(せき)まで（持(も)っていきます・持(も)ってきます）。

席(せき)まで持(も)っていきます。

You are moving the documents away from where you are towards Tanaka's desk, so the direction is outward and ていく is right. てくる would be what Tanaka says if he asked you to fetch them to his seat.

～てくる · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "I'll go and ask the teacher, so wait here."

先生(せんせい)に聞(き)いてきますので、ここで待(ま)っていてください。

The errand ends with you returning to the person you are speaking to, and that return is what てくる marks. 聞(き)きます alone would leave the listener unsure whether you were coming back at all.

～てくる · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 大学(だいがく)に入(はい)ってから、だんだん数学(すうがく)がおもしろくなっていきました。

✅ 大学(だいがく)に入(はい)ってから、だんだん数学(すうがく)がおもしろくなってきました。

The change runs from a point in the past up to the speaker's present, so the arrow points towards now - てくる. ていく would only fit if the sentence were looking ahead: これからもっとおもしろくなっていくでしょう.

～てくる · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 息子(むすこ)は漢字(かんじ)が書(か)けるようになってきた。 (b) 息子(むすこ)は漢字(かんじ)が書(か)けるようになった。

(b) states the achievement as a completed fact: at some point he crossed the line and now he can write kanji.

(a) presents the same development as a gradual approach to that line, watched from the present. It implies the process took time, that the speaker has been observing it, and often that it is not quite finished - he is getting there.
