Transitive & intransitive verbs

Meaning · paired verbs built on one root - one for "somebody does it to something", one for "it happens"

Japanese keeps two separate verbs where English is happy with one. 開あける is open something; 開あく is (something) opens. English uses the word "open" for both and lets the sentence sort it out. Japanese does not: picking the wrong half of the pair forces the wrong particle, and the sentence stops being Japanese. This is vocabulary, but it behaves like grammar, which is why it gets a page.

The vocabulary word for the transitive half is 他動詞たどうし and for the intransitive half 自動詞じどうし. You will see both in dictionaries.

Rule of thumb - the verb chooses the particle, not the other way round. An intransitive verb can never take a を object, and a transitive verb always wants one. If you can say "somebody did it to something", you need the transitive.

Transitive & intransitive verbs · Nuance & register

Transitivity carries no politeness of its own. Both halves of a pair are equally usable in casual speech, in ます speech and in writing, and nothing about the choice makes you sound formal or rough. What it does carry is whether a person is in the picture, and that has social weight.

Japanese reaches for the intransitive far more often than English does. Where English says "we've decided the date" or "they found the key", ordinary Japanese says 日ひにちが決きまりました and 鍵かぎが見みつかりました. No one is named, no one is credited, and the sentence sounds neutral and adult rather than evasive. English speakers who default to the transitive end up sounding as though they are claiming credit for everything.

Transitive & intransitive verbs · Nuance & register (cont.)

The same choice is how you take or dodge responsibility. お皿さらが 割われました reports an event; お皿さらを割わりました admits you did it. If you broke it and you are apologising, the intransitive will read as squirming. If you are simply telling someone the plate is in pieces, the transitive drags in a culprit nobody asked about.

Each half has its own resulting-state construction. The intransitive takes ～ている (閉しまっている, "is shut"); the transitive takes ～てある (閉しめてある, "has been shut"). That pairing has a page of its own, but it is worth knowing now that it exists - mixing the halves there is the single commonest N4 error.

Transitive & intransitive verbs · Nuance & register (cont.)

A few verbs work both ways with no change of shape. 開ひらく, 増ます and 吹ふく each take が or を depending on the sentence. They are the exception; assume a pair until you find out otherwise.

開く vs 開ける - the particle follows the verb

Rule of thumb - 開あく takes が and no object at all. 開あける takes を and needs somebody doing the opening. You cannot mix and match.

風かぜで窓まどが**開あいた**。
The window blew open in the wind.

暑あつかったので窓まどを**開あけた**。
It was hot, so I opened the window.

開く vs 開ける - the particle follows the verb (cont.)

In the first sentence nothing has an agent - the wind is a cause, marked with で, not a doer. In the second there is a person, and the window is what they acted on. Writing ❌窓まどを開あいた is the most frequent transitivity error there is, and it happens because English lets "opened" cover both.

The same test works on every pair. Ask: does the sentence have somebody doing it to something?

話はなし合あいは十時じゅうじまで**続つづいた**。
The discussion went on until ten.

反対はんたいされても、彼かれは研究けんきゅうを**続つづけた**。
Even when people objected, he carried on with his research.

Transitive & intransitive verbs · The intransitive vs the passive

Rule of thumb - the intransitive says it happened. The passive says somebody did it, and it landed on me.

English translates both with "was broken", which is exactly why the distinction is invisible to learners.

自転車じてんしゃが**壊こわれた**。
My bike broke.

自転車じてんしゃを**壊こわされた**。
Somebody went and broke my bike.

Transitive & intransitive verbs · The intransitive vs the passive (cont.)

The first is bad luck: the bike gave out. The second has a culprit in it, unnamed but definitely there, and the speaker is the injured party - which is why the object keeps を even though the verb is passive. Choosing the passive where the intransitive would do makes you sound aggrieved. Choosing the intransitive when someone really did wreck your things makes you sound oddly forgiving.

財布さいふが見みつかりました。
My wallet turned up.

財布さいふを盗ぬすまれました。
My wallet was stolen.

Where English wants a person and Japanese does not

Rule of thumb - when no one needs naming, Japanese states the event with the intransitive rather than reaching for a passive.

English has no intransitive verb for "decide", so it borrows the passive: "the date has been decided". Japanese has 決きまる sitting there ready, so the passive is unnecessary and would sound heavy.

来月らいげつの予定よていが**決きまりました**。
Next month's schedule has been settled.

なくした鍵かぎが机つくえの下したから**見みつかりました**。
The key I lost turned up under the desk.

Where English wants a person and Japanese does not (cont.)

This is the reason so much everyday Japanese has no visible subject. 連絡れんらくが入はいりました ("word came in"), 準備じゅんびが 整ととのいました ("everything's ready"), 会議かいぎが終おわりました ("the meeting's over") - all intransitive, all agentless, all completely standard in a workplace where naming who did what would be beside the point.

Transitive & intransitive verbs · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 窓まどを開あきました。 → ✅ 窓まどを**開あけました**。／窓まどが**開あきました**。
    開あく is intransitive and cannot take a を object. Either switch the verb or switch the particle - but the two must agree.

  • ❌ 電気でんきが消けしました。 → ✅ 電気でんきを**消けしました**。／電気でんきが**消きえました**。
    Same error in the other direction. Note the readings too: 消けす and 消きえる share a kanji and not a stem.

  • ❌ 授業じゅぎょうを始はじまります。 → ✅ 授業じゅぎょうを**始はじめます**。
    A teacher starting a lesson is doing something to the lesson. 始はじまる would need 授業じゅぎょうが and no actor.

  • ❌ かばんから財布さいふを落おちました。 → ✅ かばんから財布さいふを**落おとしました**。
    Dropping something is transitive. 落おちる is what the wallet does on its own: 財布さいふが落おちました.

Transitive & intransitive verbs · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ ドアが閉しめています。 → ✅ ドアが**閉しまっています**。
    With が you need the intransitive. 閉しめている would mean somebody is in the middle of closing it, and that person would need を.

Transitive & intransitive verbs · Practice 1

1. ボタンを押おすと、ドアが____ます。

ドアが**開あき**ます。

The subject is marked with が and there is no person acting on the door - the button does it - so the intransitive 開あく is required. 開あける would need ドアを and somebody to open it.

The train is the thing that came to a halt, and it is marked with が, so the intransitive is the only fit. 止とめました would describe a driver bringing something to a stop: 車くるまを止とめました.

You are the one who lost hold of it, so the transitive 落おとす with を is right. Saying 財布さいふが落おちました would report that the wallet fell, with nobody at fault - fine as a description, wrong as a confession.

The meeting starts by itself here - nobody is named as starting it - so it takes が plus the intransitive. If you did want to name the chairperson, the other half of the pair works with を: 会議かいぎを始はじめる前まえに.

Transitive & intransitive verbs · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) パスワードを変かえました。 (b) パスワードが変かわりました。

(a) uses the transitive: somebody - normally the speaker - deliberately changed the password. It answers "what did you do?" and it puts a person on record.

(b) uses the intransitive: the password is now different, and the sentence says nothing about who or why. It is what a system message says, and it is also what you say when you want the change reported without anyone being credited or blamed.

～てある

Meaning · something has been done, on purpose, and the result is still there

～てある takes a transitive verb and reports the state it left behind. Somebody did it - that is built into the meaning - but the sentence deliberately declines to say who. What you are describing is the leftover: the note is written, the seats are booked, the beer is in the fridge, and none of that happened by itself.

～てある · Formation

Rule of thumb - ～てある only takes verbs that have an object. If the verb takes が and describes something happening by itself, it wants ～ている instead, and there is no ❌倒たおれてある.

Note - the verb also has to leave a result you can point at, arranged by someone for a reason. 貼はる, 置おく, 書かく, 入いれる, 決きめる and 予約よやくする all qualify. Verbs whose result disappears into the doer - 食たべる, 見みる, 聞きく - sound odd with てある unless the situation genuinely makes them preparation.

The tickets and the hotel had all been booked before we left, so we could take it easy on the day.

～てある · Nuance & register

～てある is register-neutral. てあります is its polite form and there is no casual contraction of the auxiliary itself; what does contract is the negative, where 書かいてありません becomes 書かいてない in everyday speech. 書かいてない is extremely common and entirely normal - do not read it as a different pattern.

～てある · Nuance & register (cont.)

The invisible person is the whole point. ～てある is how Japanese says "this was seen to" without naming an agent, so it turns up constantly in notices, in handovers, and in the answer to "is everything ready?". Because the agent is implied rather than stated, てある also carries a faint sense of for you or for later: エアコンが付つけてあります is not just a fact about the room, it tells you someone thought of you.

It quietly commits you to intention. Saying 窓まどが開あけてあります claims that somebody opened the window on purpose. If the window is simply open and you have no idea why, that claim is wrong and you want 開あいています instead.

～てある · Nuance & register (cont.)

It reads as reporting, not as doing. てある is stative, like ある itself, so it does not take commands, invitations or intentions: there is no ❌置おいてあってください and no ❌置おいてあろう. When you need to ask somebody to arrange something, the pattern you want is ～ておく.

On signs and in formal writing you will also meet the passive - 禁煙きんえんと書かかれています. It says almost the same thing but sounds more official and more distant. ～てある is the everyday version.

～てある · ～てある vs ～ている

Rule of thumb - the transitive verb takes てある; the intransitive verb takes ている. Both describe the same room. てある adds that a person arranged it that way.

This is the pair the whole transitivity system exists for. Take 閉しまる (intransitive) and 閉しめる (transitive):

門もんが**閉しまっている**。
The gate is shut.

門もんが**閉しめてある**。
The gate has been shut. (somebody closed it, deliberately)

～てある · ～てある vs ～ている (cont.)

You are looking at the same gate. The first sentence describes it and stops. The second says a human being is behind the situation - which matters, because it tells you the gate is shut for a reason and possibly that you should not open it.

There is a third member of the set, and forgetting it is where learners come unstuck. The transitive verb with ている is not a state at all - it is the action in progress:

係かかりの人ひとが門もんを**閉しめている**。
The attendant is closing the gate.

～てある · ～てある vs ～ている (cont.)

So: 閉しまっている = shut, nobody in view. 閉しめてある = shut, somebody saw to it. 閉しめている = somebody is closing it right now. Three sentences, one gate, and the verb plus the auxiliary decide which you get.

～てある · ～てある vs ～ておく

Rule of thumb - ～ておく is the doing; ～てある is what you find afterwards. You do the ておく, and later somebody walks in and reports the てある.

明日あした飲のむ分ぶんのコーヒーを**買かっておいた**。
I bought the coffee for tomorrow ahead of time.

明日あした飲のむ分ぶんのコーヒーは**買かってある**。
Tomorrow's coffee is bought and waiting.

The world is identical; the camera angle is not. The first is a report of your action and answers "what did you do?". The second is a report of the state of the kitchen and answers "are we out of coffee?".

～てある · ～てある vs ～ておく (cont.)

The grammatical consequence is sharper than the feel. ておく is a live action verb and behaves like one - 買かっておこう, 買かっておいてください, 買かっておかなきゃ. てある can do none of that, because a state cannot be volunteered for or ordered.

～てある · が vs を with ～てある

Rule of thumb - both occur. が presents the state; を keeps the speaker's own action in view. が is much commoner, and it is the safe answer.

ホワイトボードに予定よていが**書かいてある**。
The schedule is up on the whiteboard.

ホワイトボードに予定よていを**書かいてある**。
I've written the schedule up on the whiteboard.

With が, the object has been promoted to subject and the sentence is pure description - this is what you say when you notice something, or when you tell somebody where to look. With を, the sentence keeps its object and therefore keeps a doer hovering behind it, normally the speaker; the effect shades towards "I've taken care of it", which is ておく territory.

～てある · が vs を with ～てある (cont.)

If you are unsure, use が. The を version is fine but narrow, and it sounds wrong when the speaker clearly did not do the deed: ❌誰だれかが窓まどを開あけてある.

～てある · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 木きが倒たおれてある。 → ✅ 木きが**倒たおれている**。
    倒たおれる is intransitive, and nobody knocks a tree over on purpose. Events without an arranger take ている.

  • ❌ 窓まどが開あいてある。 → ✅ 窓まどが**開あけてある**。
    てある needs the transitive half of the pair. 開あく + てある mixes the two systems and is simply not a form.

  • ❌ ドアを閉しめてあってください。 → ✅ ドアを**閉しめておいて*ください。
    てある is a state and cannot be requested. Asking somebody to arrange something is ～ておく.*

～てある · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ この本ほんはもう読よんであります。（meaning "I've already read it"）→ ✅ この本ほんはもう**読よみました**。
    てある reports a prepared state that someone can now make use of, not your personal experience of having done something. Reading a book leaves no arranged result for anyone to find.

～てある · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) かぎが掛かかっています。 (b) かぎが掛かけてあります。

(a) uses the intransitive 掛かかる: the door is locked, and that is all the sentence claims. It might have locked itself; you are just describing what you found.

(b) uses the transitive 掛かける with てある: somebody locked it, on purpose, and the lock is still on. It implies a decision - the room was secured, and probably for a reason you should respect.

～てみる

Meaning · do something and see what happens - try it out

～てみる does not mean "attempt". The action really is carried out; what is uncertain is the result. You eat the thing to find out what it tastes like, you press the button to find out what it does, you ask the question to find out what the answer is. English "try" covers this and a second meaning that ～てみる cannot express, which is where most of the trouble comes from.

Can I try this on?

駅前えきまえの新あたらしいラーメン屋やに**行いってみた**。
I gave the new ramen place by the station a go.

一度いちど富士山ふじさんに**登のぼってみたい**です。
I'd like to climb Mt Fuji once.

むずかしそうだけど、とりあえずやってみよう。
It looks hard, but let's give it a shot anyway.

～てみる · Examples (cont.)

田中たなかさんに**聞きいてみます**ね。
I'll ask Tanaka and see what he says.

このアプリ、ためしに**使つかってみて**ください。びっくりするほど便利べんりですよ。
Give this app a go - it's astonishingly handy.

自分じぶんで**作つくってみた**けれど、店みせの味あじにはぜんぜんかなわなかった。
I had a go at making it myself, but it was nowhere near as good as the shop's.

どこにもないと思おもっていたが、机つくえの引ひき出だしを**開あけてみたら**、なくした鍵かぎはそこにあった。
I thought it was nowhere to be found, but when I opened the desk drawer to check, the lost key was sitting right there.

～てみる · Nuance & register

～てみる is register-neutral in itself, but it is one of the most useful politeness tools in the language, because it converts an instruction into an invitation. 読よんでください tells somebody to read; 読よんでみてください offers them the chance and leaves the verdict to them. Upward, the same idea takes a longer shape: ～てみていただけますか, ～てみてはいかがでしょうか.

The past form invites a follow-up. ～てみた almost always wants a result after it, because the whole point was to find something out: 食たべてみたけど、思おもったより甘あまかった. A bare 食たべてみました with nothing after it sounds unfinished, like telling half a story.

～てみる · Nuance & register (cont.)

It needs an action you control. Weather, feelings and involuntary events cannot be sampled: ❌雨あめが降ふってみる, ❌疲つかれてみる, ❌わかってみる. If the subject cannot decide to do it, てみる is out.

It can sound presumptuous in the wrong direction. Suggesting that a superior "give something a try" implies you know better and that they are the one experimenting. To a customer or a boss, prefer 一度いちどご覧らんください or a plain polite request over ～てみてください for anything they are obliged to do.

Do not confuse it with ～てみせる. That is a separate auxiliary meaning "do it and show you" or, with a volitional, "I'll manage it come what may": 今度こんどこそ合格ごうかくしてみせる.

～てみる · ～てみる vs ～ようとする

Rule of thumb - てみる means the action happened and you are waiting on the outcome. ようとする means you were making the effort, and it may never have come off at all.

その薬くすりを**飲のんでみた**が、ぜんぜん効きかなかった。
I tried the medicine, but it didn't do a thing.

その薬くすりを**飲のもうとした**が、苦にがくて飲のめなかった。
I tried to take the medicine, but it was too bitter to get down.

In the first sentence the medicine went down; only its effect disappointed. In the second it never got past the speaker's lips. English uses "tried" for both, so learners reach for てみる in situations where the action failed to occur - and that is a contradiction, because てみる presupposes it occurred.

～てみる · ～てみる vs ～ようとする (cont.)

A quick test: if you can add "…and here is what I found out", you want てみる. If you have to add "…but I couldn't", you want ようとする.

～てみる · ～てみる vs ～たことがある

Rule of thumb - てみる is the occasion when you did it to find out. たことがある is the bare fact that at some point in your life you have done it.

なっとうを**食たべてみました**。
I gave natto a try.

なっとうを食たべたことがあります。
I've eaten natto before.

English "I've tried natto" is the second of these, not the first, and that mismatch sends learners the wrong way constantly. The first sentence is an event with a time and a purpose behind it; the second is a line on your CV of experiences.

～てみる · ～てみる vs ～たことがある (cont.)

The two stack happily when you mean both at once:

一度いちどだけ馬うまに乗のってみたことがあります。
I've had a go at riding a horse exactly once.

～てみる · ～てみてください vs ～てください

Rule of thumb - てください tells. てみてください offers. Same request, completely different pressure on the listener.

このボタンを**押おしてください**。
Press this button.

うまく動うごかないときは、このボタンを**押おしてみてください**。
If it isn't working properly, try pressing this button.

～てみる · ～てみてください vs ～てください (cont.)

The second version admits that the speaker is not certain it will help, which is exactly what makes it feel considerate: the listener is being given an option, not an order. That is also its limit. For something the listener genuinely has to do - a form to sign, a passport to hand over - てみて is wrong, because it suggests the outcome is up for discussion: ❌パスポートを見みせてみてください.

～てみる · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 食たべるてみる → ✅ 食たべてみる
    みる attaches to the て-form, never to the dictionary form.

  • ❌ 漢字かんじを書かいて見みました。 → ✅ 漢字かんじを**書かいてみました**。
    The auxiliary is written in kana. Using the kanji turns it back into "looked at", which is not what you mean.

  • ❌ 明日あしたは雨あめが降ふってみます。 → ✅ 明日あしたは雨あめが降ふるかもしれません。
    You can only sample something you choose to do. Weather does not experiment with itself.

  • ❌ 昨日きのう、新あたらしいカメラを買かってみました。（as a plain report）→ ✅ 昨日きのう、新あたらしいカメラを**買かいました**。
    てみる adds "to see how it would go", so on an ordinary purchase it sounds as though you bought the camera as an experiment and are still deciding.

～てみる · Practice 1

1. おいしいかどうかわからないので、少すこし食たべ____ます。

少すこし食たべてみます。

The reason given is that the outcome is unknown, which is precisely the job of てみる: you carry out the action in order to find out. Plain 食たべます would lose the "and then I'll know" that the first clause sets up.

～てみる · Practice 2

2. 窓まどを（開あけてみた・開あけようとした）が、固かたくて動うごかなかった。

窓まどを**開あけようとした**が、固かたくて動うごかなかった。

The window never moved, so the action did not take place - and てみる requires that it did. ようとする is the pattern for an effort that came to nothing.

～てみる · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "I'd like to try living abroad someday."

いつか外国がいこくに**住すんでみたい**です。

てみたい is てみる plus the desire ending, and it is the natural way to say you want to sample an experience rather than simply wanting a thing. 住すみたい alone would state a settled wish to live there; 住すんでみたい keeps the sense of finding out what it is like.

～てみる · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: すみません、この書類しょるいにサインしてみてください。

✅ すみません、この書類しょるいにサインしてください。

Signing the document is not optional and there is nothing to find out, so the invitation flavour of てみて is out of place - it makes a required step sound like a suggestion the listener could decline.

～てみる · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 京都きょうとに行いったことがあります。 (b) 京都きょうとに行いってみました。

(a) is a statement about your past experience: at some point you have been to Kyoto. It carries no information about why you went or how it turned out, and it is the right answer to "have you ever been?".

(b) is about one particular occasion, and it frames the trip as something you did to see what Kyoto was like. It practically demands a follow-up - どうでしたか - because the whole point of てみる is the verdict that comes after.

～ておく / ～とく

Meaning · do it now so that later is taken care of - or leave it as it stands

～ておく frames an action as setup. The speaker is not merely doing the thing; they are doing it now so that some later moment goes smoothly. Out of that comes a second, quieter use: if something is already in a useful state, ～ておく means deliberately leaving it there instead of undoing it.

～ておく / ～とく · Formation

おく is a う-verb and conjugates normally after the て-form.

～ておく / ～とく · Examples

ビールを**冷ひやしておきます**。
I'll put the beer in to chill, ready for later.

出発しゅっぱつ前まえにホテルを**調しらべておいた**。
I looked up hotels before we set off.

この書類しょるい、コピーしといてくれる？
Could you get this photocopied?

そのままでいいですよ。エアコンはつけておいてください。
Leave it as it is - please keep the air conditioning on.

～ておく / ～とく · Examples (cont.)

今いまのうちに宿題しゅくだいをやっとこう。
Let's get the homework out of the way while we've got the chance.

明日あしたの会議かいぎで配くばるので、資料しりょうを三十さんじゅう部ぶ印刷いんさつしておいてください。
We're handing these out at tomorrow's meeting, so please print thirty copies in advance.

話はなしがややこしくなるから、そのことは彼女かのじょには**言いわないでおこう**。
It'll only complicate things, so let's keep that from her.

冷さめてもおいしいので、お弁当べんとうは朝あさのうちに**作つくっておきました**。
It tastes fine cold, so I made the lunchbox first thing this morning.

～ておく / ～とく · Nuance & register

とく is not slang; it is how people talk. やっとく, 見みとく, 言いっとくけど, 覚おぼえとけ - the contraction runs across every age and both genders, and refusing to use it will make your casual speech sound stiff. It stays out of writing, out of ます speech and out of exams, but it belongs in your ear from day one.

As a request, ておいて carries an edge. It hands somebody a task and closes the topic - 「じゃ、よろしく。予約よやくしといて」 is what a manager says to a junior. Aimed upward it is too brisk; use 予約よやくしておいていただけますか or 予約よやくをお願ねがいできますでしょうか instead.

～ておく / ～とく · Nuance & register (cont.)

There is a self-interested tinge to it. ておく says the action clears your own path, so it can sound slightly calculating when the beneficiary is somebody else: 「言いっておきますが…」 is a common preface meaning "just so we're clear", and it is a mild warning shot, not a favour.

The negative splits two ways. 買かっておかない is "I'm not going to buy it in advance"; 買かわないでおく is "I'm going to deliberately not buy it (and leave things that way)". The second is the one people mean when they choose to hold back - 聞きかないでおく, "I'll not ask".

～ておく / ～とく · Nuance & register (cont.)

In polite speech it is completely ordinary, and it appears constantly in service Japanese: お預あずかりしておきます, 確認かくにんしておきます. Nothing about ておく is casual by itself - only the contraction is.

The two jobs of ～ておく - get it done ahead, or leave it as it is

Rule of thumb - if the て-verb changes something, ておく means do it now for later. If the て-verb has already produced the state you want, ておく means don't undo it.

会議かいぎの前まえに席せきを**決きめておく**。
We'll settle the seating before the meeting.

暑あついので、窓まどは**開あけておいて**ください。
It's hot, so please leave the window open.

The two jobs of ～ておく - get it done ahead, or leave it as it is (cont.)

There is only one grammar here. The "leave it" reading appears when the state is already in place and closing it again would take an action - so telling somebody 開あけておいて is telling them to keep choosing the open window. English needs two different verbs, "set up" and "leave", where Japanese needs one.

The clearest case of the second reading is the fixed phrase そのままにしておく:

散ちらかっているけど、今日きょうはそのままにしておこう。
It's a mess, but let's just leave it for today.

～ておく / ～とく · ～ておく vs ～てある

Rule of thumb - ておく is what you do; てある is what somebody finds afterwards. ておく is a live action verb, てある is a state.

飲のみ物ものを**用意よういしておきました**。
I got the drinks ready.

飲のみ物ものは**用意よういしてあります**。
The drinks are ready - somebody's seen to it.

～ておく / ～とく · ～ておく vs ～てある (cont.)

The first answers "what did you do?"; the second answers "is everything sorted?". The grammatical fallout is the sharper half of the distinction. Because ておく is an action, it takes everything an action takes - 買かっておこう, 買かっておいてください, 買かっておかなければ. てある takes none of them, so the moment you need a command, an invitation or an intention, ておく is your only option.

Note also the particles. ておく keeps the original verb's frame, so the object stays を: 窓まどを開あけておく. てある normally promotes that object to が: 窓まどが開あけてある.

～ておく / ～とく · ～ておく vs ～てしまう

Rule of thumb - ておく looks forward: it's done so the future is easier. てしまう looks at the finish line: it's done, over, off the list.

夜よるのうちにレポートを**書かいておく**。
I'll write the report tonight so it's ready.

夜よるのうちにレポートを**書かいてしまう**。
I'll get the report finished off tonight.

In practice the same evening produces both sentences, but the reason differs. ておく is chosen because something comes next - the report is needed tomorrow. てしまう is chosen because you want the thing behind you, complete, with nothing left over.

～ておく / ～とく · ～ておく vs ～てしまう (cont.)

Two further separations. てしまう can carry regret, and ておく never can: there is no way to say ておく about something you wish had not happened. And てしまう insists on completion - you finish the whole report - whereas ておく is satisfied with whatever preparation is enough: 少すこしだけ書かいておく is fine, while ❌少すこしだけ書かいてしまう fights itself.

～ておく / ～とく · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 買かうておく → ✅ 買かっておく
    おく attaches to the て-form, not the dictionary form.

  • ❌ まだホテルを予約よやくしておきません。 → ✅ まだホテルを**予約よやくしていません**。
    "I haven't booked yet" is the plain ていない. The negative of ておく means you have decided against preparing, which is a different claim.

  • ❌ 窓まどが開あけておく。 → ✅ 窓まどを**開あけておく**。
    ておく does not change the verb's particles. The object of a transitive verb stays on を; promotion to が belongs to てある.

～ておく / ～とく · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 先生せんせい、これを読よんどいてください。 → ✅ 先生せんせい、こちらを**読よんでおいて**いただけますか。
    Two problems at once: どく is a casual contraction, and ておいてください hands out tasks. Neither goes upward.

～ておく / ～とく · Practice 1

1. 友ともだちが来くる前まえに、部屋へやを片かたづけて____。

部屋へやを片かたづけておきます（casual: 片かたづけとく）。

The tidying is being done because something is coming afterwards, which is exactly the preparation reading of ておく. Plain 片かたづけます would report the action with no forward-looking purpose attached.

～ておく / ～とく · Practice 2

2. ケーキは冷蔵庫れいぞうこに（入いれておきました・入いれてあります）。自分じぶんの行動こうどうを報告ほうこくする場合ばあいは、どちらですか。

入いれておきました。

Reporting your own action calls for ておく, which is a normal action verb in the past tense. 入いれてあります would describe the state of the fridge without saying who did it - the right choice if you were answering "is the cake somewhere safe?".

～ておく / ～とく · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "I'll charge my phone before I go out."

出でかける前まえに携帯けいたいを**充電じゅうでんしておきます**。

The charging exists only to serve the trip that follows, which is what ておく marks. In speech this would very often come out as 充電じゅうでんしとく, and the meaning is identical.

～ておく / ～とく · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 旅行りょこうのことはまだ家族かぞくに話はなしておきません。

✅ 旅行りょこうのことはまだ家族かぞくに**話はなしていません**。／旅行りょこうのことは家族かぞくには**話はなさないでおきます**。

If you simply have not got round to it, the plain negative ていません is what you want. If you are choosing not to tell them and leaving it that way on purpose, the form is ない-form + でおく - and those are genuinely different situations.

～ておく / ～とく · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 電気でんきをつけておきます。 (b) 電気でんきをつけます。

(b) simply reports switching the light on. It says nothing about why, and it could be about the next second.

(a) says the light is being switched on for what comes later - so that the person arriving home tonight is not fumbling in the dark, or so that the room is ready for the guests. Depending on the situation it can also mean the light is already on and you are leaving it that way rather than turning it off.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう

Meaning · finish something off completely - or do something you wish you hadn't

～てしまう carries two loads at once, and learners usually notice only one of them. The first is completion: the action is taken all the way to the end, with nothing left over. The second is regret: it happened, it cannot be undone, and you are not pleased about it. The same three syllables do both jobs, and the situation tells you which.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Formation

しまう is a う-verb and conjugates normally. The contracted forms are far more common in speech than the full ones.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Examples

ケーキを全部ぜんぶ食たべてしまった。
I ate the whole cake.

かさを電車でんしゃに**忘わすれちゃった**。
I've gone and left my umbrella on the train.

宿題しゅくだいを先さきにやってしまいましょう。
Let's get the homework done and out of the way first.

バスが**行いってしまいました**。
The bus has gone.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Examples (cont.)

その本ほん、おもしろくて一日いちにちで**読よんじゃった**。
That book was so good I got through it in a day.

急いそいでいて、まだ送おくっていない大事だいじなメールを**消けしてしまいました**。
I was in a rush and deleted an important email I hadn't even sent yet.

練習れんしゅうを休やすんでいるうちに、弾ひき方かたを**忘わすれてしまった**。
I took a break from practising and ended up forgetting how to play.

昼ひるまでにこの仕事しごとを**終おわらせてしまいたい**んですが、手伝てつだっていただけませんか。
I'd like to get this job finished off before lunch - could you give me a hand?

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Nuance & register

ちゃった is one of the most-heard endings in casual Japanese. It is neutral between men and women and across ages, and it is the default in conversation: 寝坊ねぼうしちゃった, 壊こわしちゃった, 買かっちゃった. Polite speech uses the full てしまいました, and business apologies stretch it further: 遅おくれてしまい、申もうし訳わけございません.

ちまう / じまう is a different register again - rough, blunt, distinctly masculine and slightly old-fashioned. You will meet it in films and manga and in older men's speech. Recognise it; do not adopt it.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Nuance & register (cont.)

Regret here is softer than "regret" sounds in English. てしまう does not mean you are wracked with guilt; it means this is not how I wanted it. That is why it is the standard way to own up to something. 「壊こわしちゃいました」 sounds honest and human, while a flat 「壊こわしました」 can come across as oddly cold, as though you broke it and do not much mind.

The completion reading is the one that survives into plans. With the volitional, with たい, and in invitations, only the "get it finished" sense is available: やってしまおう, 片かたづけてしまいたい. Nobody volunteers to regret something.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Nuance & register (cont.)

Write しまう in kana. The kanji 仕舞しまう belongs to the independent verb "put away, stow" - which is the image the auxiliary comes from: you finish with the thing and shut it away.

In neutral writing it is an intrusion. A news report or a lab record uses the plain past, because てしまう editorialises. Seeing it in an otherwise flat text tells you the writer has an opinion.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Completion vs regret - telling them apart

Rule of thumb - look at the verb and at whether you chose it. A verb you control, plus a quantity or a deadline, gives completion. An outcome that happened to you gives regret.

三日みっかでこのドラマを全部ぜんぶ見みてしまった。
I got through the whole drama in three days.

母ははにもらった大切たいせつなお皿さらを**割わってしまった**。
I broke the plate my mother gave me.

The first sentence has a time span and a total, and watching a drama is something you decide to do - so てしまう measures the finish. The second has a valued object and a bad outcome, so it measures the damage.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Completion vs regret - telling them apart (cont.)

Certain verbs point one way almost automatically. 忘わすれる, 転ころぶ, 落おとす, 遅おくれる, 寝坊ねぼうする, 死しぬ - nobody plans these, so てしまう on them is regret. Meanwhile 全部ぜんぶ, 最後さいごまで, 一日いちにちで and a volitional ending pull hard towards completion.

And sometimes both readings sit in one sentence. 全部ぜんぶ食たべちゃった can be a satisfied "I polished off the lot" or a sheepish "oh no, I've eaten all of it". Japanese speakers hear no ambiguity because the tone of voice and the situation settle it - which is the honest answer to "how do I tell?".

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · ～てしまう vs ～終わる

Rule of thumb - ～終おわる is the neutral fact that the action reached its end. ～てしまう adds an attitude: relief, thoroughness, or dismay.

この本ほんを**読よみ終おわった**ら、貸かしてください。
Once you've finished reading this book, lend it to me.

貸かした本ほんを一晩ひとばんで**読よんでしまった**そうだ。
Apparently he polished off the book I lent him in a single night.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · ～てしまう vs ～終わる (cont.)

Three practical differences. 終おわる attaches to the ます-stem (読よみ終おわる), not the て-form. It is purely aspectual and can never mean regret. And it needs a verb with a natural finishing point, so ❌忘わすれ終おわる and ❌死しに終おわる are impossible, while てしまう takes both quite happily.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · ～てしまった vs the plain past

Rule of thumb - the plain past reports. てしまった comments.

さいふをなくした。
I lost my wallet.

さいふをなくしてしまった。
I've gone and lost my wallet.

Both are true and both are grammatical; the second tells you how the speaker feels about it. In apologies the auxiliary is close to obligatory, because leaving it out makes the event sound like weather:

待またせてしまって、すみませんでした。
I'm sorry to have kept you waiting.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · ～てしまった vs the plain past (cont.)

The reverse holds too. Where nothing is regrettable and nothing is being finished off, adding てしまう is a mistake of tone rather than of grammar: 昨日きのう映画えいがを見みてしまいました would leave a listener wondering what went wrong at the cinema.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 食たべるてしまう → ✅ 食たべてしまう
    しまう attaches to the て-form, not the dictionary form.

  • ❌ 読よんでちゃった → ✅ 読よんじゃった
    A で て-form contracts to じゃう, not ちゃう. The rule follows the same voicing split you already learned for the て-form itself.

  • ❌ 部長ぶちょう、遅おくれちゃってすみません。 → ✅ 部長ぶちょう、遅おくれてしまい、申もうし訳わけありません。
    The contraction is casual. An apology to a superior needs the full form, and ideally the stiffer 申もうし訳わけありません as well.

  • ❌ 宿題しゅくだいを忘わすれ終おわった。 → ✅ 宿題しゅくだいを**忘わすれてしまった**。
    終おわる needs an action with a finish line, and forgetting has none. This is also exactly the meaning てしまう exists for.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Practice 1

1. 急いそいでいたので、駅えきに傘かさを置おいて____。

傘かさを置おいてしまいました（casual: 置おいちゃった）。

Leaving the umbrella behind was an accident with a result the speaker regrets, so てしまう is what carries that. The plain 置おいてきました would be a flat report with no sense that anything went wrong.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Practice 2

2. 夕方ゆうがたまでにこの仕事しごとを（やってしまおう・やり終おわろう）。

夕方ゆうがたまでにこの仕事しごとをやってしまおう。

終おわる describes an action coming to its end as a fact and is odd in the volitional - you do not decide to reach an endpoint, you decide to get something finished. てしまおう is the standard way to say "let's knock this out".

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "I overslept and missed the first train."

寝坊ねぼうして、始発しはつに**乗のり遅おくれてしまいました**。

Missing a train is not something anyone plans, so the regret reading is the one that fires. In casual speech this becomes 乗のり遅おくれちゃった, which is what you would actually say to a friend.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 新あたらしいゲームをやったら、夜中よなかまで遊あそんでちゃった。

✅ 新あたらしいゲームをやったら、夜中よなかまで**遊あそんじゃった**。

遊あそぶ is a ぶ verb, so its て-form is 遊あそんで - a で form, which contracts to じゃう. Only verbs whose て-form ends in て take ちゃう.

～てしまう / ～ちゃう · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) この漫画まんがを全部ぜんぶ読よみました。 (b) この漫画まんがを全部ぜんぶ読よんでしまいました。

(a) is a plain statement of fact: you have read the whole series, and the sentence takes no position on it.

(b) adds the speaker's attitude. Depending on the situation it reads either as "I got through the lot" - thoroughness, maybe a little pride at the speed - or as "I've gone and read them all", the tone you would use if you had meant to ration them or should have been studying instead.

～ていく

Meaning · do it and go - and, on the time axis, keep changing from now on

One idea sits behind everything ～ていく does: movement away from where the speaker is standing. Physically, that means the subject does the て-verb and then leaves, or takes something along. On the time axis, "away from here" becomes "on into the future", so a change that starts now and carries forward also takes ～ていく.

～ていく · Formation

いく is the ordinary motion verb and conjugates as it always does, including the irregular て-form 行いって.

～ていく · Examples

かさを**持もっていきます**。
I'll take an umbrella with me.

鳥とりが山やまのほうへ**飛とんでいった**。

You'd be better off eating breakfast before you head out.

これから物価ぶっかはもっと**上あがっていく**だろう。
Prices will probably keep going up from here.

～ていく · Examples (cont.)

三月さんがつを過すぎると、だんだん暖あたたかくなっていきます。
Once March is behind us, it gradually gets warmer and warmer.

荷物にもつが重おもいから、車くるまで**運はこんでいこう**。
The bags are heavy, so let's take them in the car.

うちは駅えきから遠とおいので、昼ひるごはんは途中とちゅうで**買かっていく**ことにしている。
My place is a long way from the station, so I make a point of buying lunch on the way.

日本語にほんごは時間じかんがかかるが、毎日まいにち少すこしずつ**続つづけていけば**、いつか話はなせるようになる。
Japanese takes time, but if you keep at it a little each day, one day you'll be able to speak it.

～ていく · Nuance & register

～ていく is register-neutral. ていきます is its polite form and nothing about either sounds stiff or rough.

The casual contraction is てく. 持もってく, 連つれてく, やってく - the い simply drops. The past goes further in relaxed speech, where 持もっていった becomes 持もってった. Both belong to conversation only.

～ていく · Nuance & register (cont.)

Everything depends on where the speaker imagines standing. If you are at home and the party is elsewhere, you 持もっていく the wine. If you are already at the party, the same bottle 持もってくる - and the mistake of choosing by the map rather than by the viewpoint is the classic one. The speaker's position can also be a place they merely identify with: a receptionist says お持もちします about their own building.

In speeches and public commitments ～ていきます is close to a fixed formula. 「これからも努力どりょくしていきます」, 「安全あんぜんを守まもって いきます」 - the auxiliary projects the promise forward, and leaving it out makes the sentence sound like a one-off rather than a commitment.

～ていく · Nuance & register (cont.)

A past ていった in the change sense is perfectly normal. It simply places the viewpoint at a past moment and lets the change run forward from there: 人ひとが少すこしずつ減へっていった. The auxiliary is about direction, not about tense.

～ていく · ～ていく vs ～てくる - which way from the speaker

Rule of thumb - いく = away from me. くる = towards me. The choice is not about the map; it is about where the speaker is standing when they say it.

おみやげを**持もっていく**。
I'll take a present with me. (to where they are)

おみやげを**持もってくる**。
I'll bring a present back. (to where we are)

～ていく · ～ていく vs ～てくる - which way from the speaker (cont.)

The same split runs through every pair built this way: 連つれていく / 連つれてくる for people, 送おくっていく / 送おくってくる for seeing somebody off or home, 走はしっていく / 走はしってくる for a figure receding or approaching.

犬いぬが門もんのほうへ**走はしっていった**。
The dog ran off towards the gate.

If you are on the phone to somebody in another city and you say you will visit, Japanese takes their position as the destination, so the verb is 行いく where English says "I'll come" - the same instinct that makes ていく the right auxiliary there.

～ていく · ～ていく vs ～に行く

Rule of thumb - ～に行いく: you go in order to do it, and the doing happens at the far end. ～ていく: you do it first, then go.

パンを**買かいに行いく**。
I'm going out to buy bread.

パンを**買かっていく**。
I'll pick up bread on the way.

The order of events is the whole difference. In the first, the going comes first and the shop is the destination. In the second, the buying comes first - at a shop you pass - and then you continue to wherever you were actually headed, bread in hand.

～ていく · ～ていく vs ～に行く (cont.)

Note the forms as well: ～に行いく attaches to the ます-stem (買かい + に), while ～ていく attaches to the て-form. Mixing them up produces ❌買かってに行いく, which is not a form at all.

～ていく · "Do and go" vs "keep on" - telling the two ～ていく apart

Rule of thumb - a concrete action somebody performs gives the physical reading. A verb of change (なる, 増ふえる, 減へる, 変かわる) or of continued activity (続つづける, がんばる) gives the time-axis reading.

子こどもを保育園ほいくえんに**送おくっていった**。
I dropped the kids off at nursery on my way.

町まちの様子ようすは少すこしずつ**変かわっていった**。
The look of the town changed little by little.

～ていく · "Do and go" vs "keep on" - telling the two ～ていく apart (cont.)

There is no journey anywhere in the second sentence. Nothing physically goes anywhere; what moves is time, and the change is being followed forward from the moment the speaker has in mind.

The adverbs are a reliable clue. これから, 今後こんご, だんだん and 少すこしずつ point forward and pull the time reading; a destination marked with に or へ pulls the physical one.

人口じんこうはこれからも**減へっていく**と言いわれている。
The population is said to be set on continuing to fall.

～ていく · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 持もつていく → ✅ 持もっていく
    いく attaches to the て-form. 持もつ is a つ verb, so the て-form is 持もって.

  • ❌（arriving at the host's house）ケーキを持もっていきました。 → ✅ ケーキを**持もってきました**。
    You are already at the destination, so the cake has come to where you both are. Deixis is judged from where you stand as you speak, not from where you set off.

  • ❌ 来週らいしゅうから寒さむくなってきます。 → ✅ 来週らいしゅうから寒さむくなっていきます。
    A change running into the future takes ていく. てくる looks back at a change that has already reached the present.

～ていく · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 雨あめが降ふっていきました。（meaning "it started raining"）→ ✅ 雨あめが**降ふってきました**。
    Something newly arriving at you is てくる. ていく would suggest the rain was moving off away from the speaker.

～ていく · Practice 1

1. 寒さむいから、コートを____いきます。

コートを**着きて**いきます。

The て-form of 着きる plus いく gives "put it on and then go" - you dress first and leave afterwards. This is the everyday physical reading of the pattern, and it is why the coat never has to be mentioned again.

～ていく · Practice 2

2. 会社かいしゃの近ちかくのパン屋やで昼ひるごはんを（買かっていきます・買かいに行いきます）。通勤つうきんの途中とちゅうで買かう場合ばあいは？

買かっていきます。

Buying on the way means the purchase happens first and the journey continues afterwards, which is ていく. 買かいに行いきます would mean you make a separate trip to the bakery with buying lunch as its purpose.

～ていく · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "From now on, working conditions will slowly improve."

これから、働はたらく環境かんきょうは少すこしずつよくなっていくでしょう。

これから puts the viewpoint at the present and looks forward, and the verb is a change rather than a journey - the time-axis reading of ていく. Using よくなってくる would describe an improvement that had already reached us.

～ていく · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 友ともだちの家いえに着ついて、「おみやげを持もっていきました」と言いった。

✅ 友ともだちの家いえに着ついて、「おみやげを**持もってきました**」と言いった。

By the time you speak you are standing in your friend's house, so the souvenir has travelled towards the place you now are. ていく would only work said before you left, while you were still at home.

～ていく · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 子こどもの数かずが減へってきた。 (b) 子こどもの数かずが減へっていく。

(a) looks backwards from now: the number has been falling over some past stretch of time and has arrived at today's figure. It is a report on what has already happened.

(b) looks forwards from now: the fall is expected to continue past the present moment into the future. It is a prediction rather than a report, which is why it so often turns up with これから or でしょう.

～てくる

Meaning · do it and come back - and, on the time axis, a change that has reached now

～てくる is the mirror image of ～ていく: movement towards where the speaker is. Three everyday uses fall out of that one idea. Someone does the て-verb while approaching; or they go off, do it, and return; or - because "towards here" on the time axis means "up to now" - a change has been building and has just arrived at the present.

くる is the irregular motion verb, so watch the readings: 来きて, 来こない, 来こよう.

～てくる · Examples

I'm just popping to the convenience store.

雨あめが**降ふってきた**。

牛乳ぎゅうにゅうを**買かってきて**くれない？
Could you pick up some milk while you're out?

夜よるになって、風かぜが強つよくなってきました。
The wind has picked up now that it's evening.

～てくる · Examples (cont.)

むこうから大おおきな犬いぬが**走はしってきた**。
A big dog came running towards us.

三年さんねん勉強べんきょうして、やっと新聞しんぶんが読よめるようになってきた。
After three years of study I'm finally starting to be able to read the newspaper.

この地方ちほうでは昔むかしから米こめを**作つくってきました**。
This region has grown rice since long ago.

話はなしているうちに、彼かれが何なにを言いいたいのかだんだんわかってきた。
As we talked, it gradually became clear what he was getting at.

～てくる · Nuance & register

～てくる is register-neutral, and てきます is its ordinary polite form. The humble equivalent てまいります belongs to business and announcements - 行いってまいります, 確認かくにんしてまいります - and sounds noticeably formal.

The round trip is why 「行いってきます」 is the phrase you say leaving the house. Literally "I'll go and come back", it promises a return, which is exactly what the household reply 「行いってらっしゃい」 answers. Any errand can take the same shape: 見みてきます ("I'll go and have a look"), 聞きいてきます ("I'll go and ask").

～てくる · Nuance & register (cont.)

The change reading normally surfaces in the past. てきた is far commoner than てくる here, because you notice a change only once it has arrived: 眠ねむくなってきた, 見みえてきた, 慣なれてきた. The state it announces is present even though the verb is past - this catches learners out regularly.

With verbs of feeling and perception it means a feeling emerging. 思おもえてくる, 気きがしてくる, 腹はらが立たってくる - the sensation was not there, and now it is welling up. This is one of the most natural-sounding uses and one learners almost never reach for.

～てくる · Nuance & register (cont.)

There is no tidy contraction to match ていく → てく, because てくる is already short. What you will hear instead is てくる stacked with other auxiliaries in casual speech - 買かってきちゃった, 買かってきといて - and the request form clipped to 買かってきて with the ください simply dropped.

～てくる · The three jobs of ～てくる

Rule of thumb - the て-verb tells you which reading you have: a verb of motion gives approach, a verb of doing gives a round trip, a verb of change or perception gives the time axis.

子こどもが部屋へやに**入はいってきた**。
A child came into the room.

切手きってを**買かってきます**。
I'll go and buy some stamps. (and come back)

最近さいきん、日ひが長ながくなってきた。
The days have been getting longer lately.

～てくる · The three jobs of ～てくる (cont.)

Buying stamps involves no movement towards the speaker at all - the goods come back with you, and the auxiliary marks the return leg of the errand. That is why てくる on an action verb so often translates as "go and…", which looks backwards until you notice that the sentence is spoken from the place you will return to.

Note - the round-trip reading needs somewhere to come back to. Said on your own doorstep, 走はしってきます means "I'm off for a run (and back)". Said watching someone approach, 走はしってきます is plain motion. Nothing in the grammar distinguishes them; the situation does.

～てくる · ～てくる vs ～ている

Rule of thumb - ている is the state now. てくる is the arrival at that state - it was not so, and now it is.

雪ゆきが**降ふってきた**。
It's started to snow.

雪ゆきが**降ふっている**。
It's snowing.

The second is what you say when somebody asks about the weather: you look out and describe what is going on. The first is what you say the moment the first flakes appear - the news is not the snow but the change, and you were there for it.

～てくる · ～てくる vs ～ている (cont.)

The same split runs through anything gradual:

おなかがすいてきた。
I'm starting to get hungry.

おなかがすいている。
I'm hungry.

Because てくる reports a change you have witnessed, it does not work for states that have simply always been true. ❌背せが高たかくなってきました said about an adult's fixed height is odd; said about a growing child it is perfect.

～てくる · ～てくる vs ～ていく on the time axis

Rule of thumb - draw an arrow at now. てきた runs from the past into the present; ていく runs from the present into the future.

去年きょねんから会員かいいんが**増ふえてきました**。
Membership has been growing since last year.

来年らいねんからも会員かいいんは**増ふえていく**でしょう。
Membership will probably go on growing next year as well.

～てくる · ～てくる vs ～ていく on the time axis (cont.)

The time expressions give it away every time. 昔むかしから, 最近さいきん, これまで and だんだん…てきた pull てくる; これから, 今後こんご and a prediction ending pull ていく. A sentence with both is a standard rhetorical move:

これまで続つづけてきたことを、これからも続つづけていきたい。
I want to keep on doing what we have been doing up to now.

Note that this contrast lives entirely on the time axis. In the physical uses, てくる and ていく are about direction in space, and no amount of thinking about tense will help you choose.

～てくる · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 買かうてくる → ✅ 買かってくる
    くる attaches to the て-form, not the dictionary form.

  • ❌（leaving the house）行いっていきます。 → ✅ 行いってきます。
    You intend to return, and the whole point of the phrase is the return, so it has to be てくる. This is a fixed expression - learn it whole.

  • ❌ 昔むかしから日本人にほんじんは魚さかなを食たべていきました。 → ✅ 昔むかしから日本人にほんじんは魚さかなを**食たべてきました**。
    昔むかしから runs from the past up to the present, which is てくる territory. ていく would push the eating off into the future.

～てくる · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ さっき昼ひるごはんを食たべていきました。（meaning "I went out and had lunch"）→ ✅ さっき昼ひるごはんを**食たべてきました**。
    You left, ate, and came back to where you are speaking - a round trip, so てくる. ていく would mean you ate and then carried on somewhere else.

～てくる · Practice 1

1. 急きゅうに空そらが暗くらくなって、雨あめが降ふっ____。

雨あめが降ふってきた。

The sky darkening and then the rain arriving is a change that has just reached the present moment, which is exactly the てくる reading. Note the past tense: てきた announces a state that is true right now.

～てくる · Practice 2

2. 田中たなかさんに資料しりょうを渡わたしたいので、席せきまで（持もっていきます・持もってきます）。

席せきまで**持もっていきます**。

You are moving the documents away from where you are towards Tanaka's desk, so the direction is outward and ていく is right. てくる would be what Tanaka says if he asked you to fetch them to his seat.

～てくる · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "I'll go and ask the teacher, so wait here."

先生せんせいに**聞きいてきます**ので、ここで待まっていてください。

The errand ends with you returning to the person you are speaking to, and that return is what てくる marks. 聞ききます alone would leave the listener unsure whether you were coming back at all.

～てくる · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 大学だいがくに入はいってから、だんだん数学すうがくがおもしろくなっていきました。

✅ 大学だいがくに入はいってから、だんだん数学すうがくがおもしろくなってきました。

The change runs from a point in the past up to the speaker's present, so the arrow points towards now - てくる. ていく would only fit if the sentence were looking ahead: これからもっとおもしろくなっていくでしょう.

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 息子むすこは漢字かんじが書かけるようになってきた。 (b) 息子むすこは漢字かんじが書かけるようになった。

(b) states the achievement as a completed fact: at some point he crossed the line and now he can write kanji.

(a) presents the same development as a gradual approach to that line, watched from the present. It implies the process took time, that the speaker has been observing it, and often that it is not quite finished - he is getting there.
