より

Meaning · than - it marks the side of a comparison that comes off worse

より is a postposition: it goes after the noun that loses. Nothing else in the sentence changes, because Japanese adjectives have no comparative ending - 安(やす)い covers both "cheap" and "cheaper", and より is what tells you which one is meant. It also has a second, unrelated life as a stiff written stand-in for から, "from".

より · Formation

より attaches to whatever names the standard you are measuring against. That standard does not have to be another object: it can be a moment in time, an expectation, or a number.

Attaches to Form Example
Noun Noun + より 兄(あに) → 兄(あに)より背(せ)が高(たか)い
Verb (plain form) V(plain) + より 待(ま)つ → 待(ま)つより歩(ある)く方(ほう)が早(はや)い
An expectation 思(おも)った／予想(よそう)した + より 思(おも)ったより安(やす)い
A habitual standard いつも／前(まえ)／去年(きょねん) + より いつもより早(はや)く起(お)きた
A number Number + より + 多(おお)い／少(すく)ない 百人(ひゃくにん)より多(おお)い
With emphasis ～よりも 去年(きょねん)よりも寒(さむ)い
Written "from" (= から) Time / place + より 九時(くじ)より開始(かいし)します

より · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - より compares exactly two things. The moment there are three or more, Japanese switches to the superlative frame with 中(なか)で and いちばん; the two constructions never combine in one clause.

The winner half of the comparison is optional. 去年(きょねん)より寒(さむ)いです is a complete sentence - the thing being measured is simply the topic, stated or understood.

より · Examples

この辞書(じしょ)はあの辞書(じしょ)より重(おも)いです。
This dictionary is heavier than that one.

思(おも)ったより人(ひと)が少(すく)なかったです。
There were fewer people than I expected.

今年(ことし)の冬(ふゆ)はいつもより雪(ゆき)が多(おお)いです。
There's more snow than usual this winter.

電話(でんわ)するより、メールを送(おく)る方(ほう)が楽(らく)です。
Sending an email is easier than phoning.

弟(おとうと)は去年(きょねん)より五(ご)センチも背(せ)が高(たか)くなりました。
My little brother has grown a whole five centimetres since last year.

より · Examples (cont.)

会議(かいぎ)は三時(さんじ)より大(おお)ホールで行(おこな)います。
The meeting will be held from three o'clock in the main hall.

この町(まち)の冬(ふゆ)は北海道(ほっかいどう)より寒(さむ)いと言(い)われています。
They say the winters in this town are colder than in Hokkaido.

新(あたら)しいアパートは駅(えき)から遠(とお)いですが、前(まえ)のところより家賃(やちん)が安(やす)くて静(しず)かです。
The new flat is far from the station, but the rent is lower than my old place and it's quiet.

より · Nuance & register

The comparative より is register-neutral. It is the same word in 安(やす)いです and 安(やす)い, in conversation and in a report, and nothing about it is marked for age or gender.

よりも is the emphatic form - "than that, definitely". It is common in both speech and writing and costs nothing to use: 今日(きょう)は昨日(きのう)よりも風(かぜ)が強(つよ)い. There is also a casual よりか heard in relaxed speech; recognise it, but keep it out of writing and out of anything you are graded on.

Two expressions are worth learning whole, because native speakers reach for them constantly:

  • 思(おも)ったより - "more/less … than I thought". Always the past
    form 思(おも)った, even when you are talking about right now.
  • 何(なに)より - "above all else".
    お元気(げんき)そうで何(なに)よりです is a set greeting meaning "I'm glad above all to see you well".

より · Nuance & register (cont.)

The "from" より is a different animal. It is written, formal and slightly old-fashioned in feel: timetables, invitations, event notices, price tags （千円(せんえん)より, "from ¥1,000"）, and the sign-off of a letter （田中(たなか)より, "from Tanaka"）. Nobody says it to a friend. If you are speaking, use から.

より · より vs ～の方(ほう)が

Rule of thumb - these are not rivals; they are the two ends of one comparison. より goes on the loser, の方(ほう)が on the winner. Either half can stand alone, but より can never mark the winner.

自転車(じてんしゃ)より地下鉄(ちかてつ)の方(ほう)が速(はや)いです。
The subway is faster than a bicycle.

Drop either half and the sentence still works, because context supplies the rest:

地下鉄(ちかてつ)の方(ほう)が速(はや)いです。
The subway is faster.

自転車(じてんしゃ)より速(はや)いです。
It's faster than a bicycle.

より · より vs ～の方(ほう)が (cont.)

What you cannot do is put より on the thing that wins. ❌地下鉄(ちかてつ)より速(はや)いのは自転車(じてんしゃ)です reverses the meaning: it now says the bicycle is the faster one.

より · より（＝から）vs から

Rule of thumb - where they overlap, より is the formal written form and から the spoken one. But から covers ground より never touches.

受付(うけつけ)は午前(ごぜん)十時(じゅうじ)より始(はじ)まります。
Reception opens at 10 a.m. (printed on a notice)

受付(うけつけ)、十時(じゅうじ)からだって。
Apparently reception starts at ten. (said to a friend)

から also means "because" and marks the material something is made of; 寒(さむ)いから帰(かえ)ります cannot become ❌寒(さむ)いより帰(かえ)ります. The overlap is only the starting-point sense.

より · より vs もっと

Rule of thumb - より names what you are comparing against; もっと refuses to.

もっと大(おお)きい声(こえ)で話(はな)してください。
Please speak louder.

さっきより大(おお)きい声(こえ)で話(はな)してください。
Please speak louder than you were a moment ago.

もっと is a bare "more" - one step further up the scale from wherever we are now. より demands a stated benchmark. That is why the two do not stack: with より already in the sentence, もっと is redundant.

より · Common mistakes

  • ❌ より去年(きょねん)寒(さむ)いです。 → ✅ 去年(きょねん)より寒(さむ)いです。
    English puts "than" in front of its noun; Japanese puts より behind it. Word order is the whole grammar here.

  • ❌ 思(おも)うより難(むずか)しかったです。 → ✅ 思(おも)ったより難(むずか)しかったです。
    The fixed expression takes the plain past 思(おも)った. The thought came first, then reality contradicted it.

  • ❌ 犬(いぬ)より猫(ねこ)がもっと好(す)きです。 → ✅ 犬(いぬ)より猫(ねこ)が好(す)きです。
    より already supplies "more". Adding もっと says it twice.

  • ❌ 三時(さんじ)より飲(の)みに行(い)こうよ。 → ✅ 三時(さんじ)から飲(の)みに行(い)こうよ。
    The "from" より belongs on printed notices. In casual speech it sounds like you are reading an announcement aloud.

より · Practice 1

1. 去年(きょねん)____給料(きゅうりょう)が少(すこ)し上(あ)がりました。

去年(きょねん)より給料(きゅうりょう)が少(すこ)し上(あ)がりました。

The standard being measured against is last year, and it is the side that comes off lower, so it takes より. Note that the benchmark here is a point in time, not another object - that is completely normal for より.

より · Practice 2

2. Choose the natural particle, said to a friend over lunch: コンサートは七時(しちじ)（より・から）始(はじ)まるよ。

コンサートは七時(しちじ)から始(はじ)まるよ。

Both words can mark a starting time, but the より version belongs to a printed programme. Saying it to a friend, with the casual ending よ, mixes two registers that do not go together.

より · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "This bag is lighter than it looks."

このかばんは見(み)た目(め)より軽(かる)いです。

見(み)た目(め) ("appearance") is the benchmark, so it takes より, and 軽(かる)い stays in its ordinary form - there is no separate comparative to conjugate into. 思(おも)ったより軽(かる)いです is also fine and shifts the benchmark from how it looks to what you had assumed.

より · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: ぼくは姉(あね)よりもっと背(せ)が高(たか)いです。

✅ ぼくは姉(あね)より背(せ)が高(たか)いです。

Delete もっと. The comparison is already carried by より; もっと is a free-standing "more" for when no benchmark is named, so putting both in one clause doubles the same idea. If you want extra force, use よりも instead.

より · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) もっと静(しず)かな部屋(へや)がいいです。 (b) この部屋(へや)より静(しず)かな部屋(へや)がいいです。

(a) asks for a quieter room without saying quieter than what - it works anywhere, including before you have seen a single room, and it is the softer request.

(b) pins the comparison to the room you are standing in. It is a complaint with a measurement attached, and the desk clerk now knows exactly which room failed. The benchmark is what より adds.

～の中で

Meaning · in; among - it fences off the set you are choosing or ranking within

～の中(なか)で names the field. Everything that follows it - a superlative, a second place, an exception, a question word - is understood to apply inside that fence and nowhere else. Literally "in the middle of ～", but the set does not have to be a physical container: friends, seasons, films you have watched all work.

～の中で · Formation

The pattern is a noun (中(なか)) plus the particle で, so anything that can modify a noun can sit in front of it.

Attaches to Form Example
Noun (a set) Noun + の中(なか)で クラス → クラスの中(なか)で
A spelled-out set AとBとCの中(なか)で 米(こめ)とパンと麺(めん)の中(なか)で
Demonstrative この／その／あの + 中(なか)で（no の） この中(なか)で
Noun-modifying clause Clause + Noun + の中(なか)で 読(よ)んだ本(ほん)の中(なか)で
Topicalised ～の中(なか)では スポーツの中(なか)では
Place or time span Place／span + で（no 中(なか)） 日本(にほん)で／一年(いちねん)で

～の中で · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - の中(なか)で is only half a sentence. It has to be followed by something that picks a member out: いちばん, 二番目(にばんめ)に, だけ, 特(とく)に, or a question word like どれ / だれ / どこ.

Note - a demonstrative already contains the "this set" idea, so it takes 中(なか) directly: この中(なか)で, never ❌このの中(なか)で.

～の中で · Examples

この四(よっ)つの中(なか)で、どれがいいですか。
Which of these four would you like?

野菜(やさい)の中(なか)で、トマトだけ苦手(にがて)です。
Tomatoes are the one vegetable I can't handle.

日本(にほん)の都市(とし)の中(なか)で、京都(きょうと)が二番目(にばんめ)に好(す)きです。
Of Japan's cities, Kyoto is my second favourite.

友(とも)だちの中(なか)で、彼女(かのじょ)だけが本当(ほんとう)のことを知(し)っています。
She's the only one of my friends who knows the truth.

スポーツの中(なか)では、特(とく)に水泳(すいえい)が得意(とくい)です。
Among sports, swimming is what I'm especially good at.

～の中で · Examples (cont.)

世界(せかい)の国(くに)の中(なか)で、一度(いちど)行(い)ってみたいのはアイスランドです。
Of all the countries in the world, the one I'd like to visit once is Iceland.

今学期(こんがっき)習(なら)った文法(ぶんぽう)の中(なか)で、いちばん難(むずか)しかったのは敬語(けいご)でした。
Of the grammar we studied this term, the hardest was the honorific language.

同(おな)じチームのメンバーの中(なか)で、朝(あさ)いちばん早(はや)く来(く)るのは部長(ぶちょう)です。
Out of everyone on the team, the one who gets in earliest is the department head.

～の中で · Nuance & register

Register-neutral and equally at home in speech and writing. 中(なか) is often written in kanji and often in kana; neither spelling changes the reading or the formality.

Two things do vary. First, with a group of members the 中(なか) is frequently dropped in speech: クラスでいちばん足(あし)が速(はや)い says the same thing as クラスの中(なか)でいちばん足(あし)が速(はや)い, a little more briskly. Keeping 中(なか) makes the boundary of the set explicit, which is why it survives in careful speech and in writing.

Second, with a place or a time span the 中(なか) is not merely optional - it is out of place. 日本(にほん)で, 世界(せかい)で, 一年(いちねん)で, クラスで are locations and stretches of time, not bags of items, so there is no "inside the set" to point at. 日本(にほん)の中(なか)で is not ungrammatical, but it reads as though you meant physically within Japan's borders, and most speakers would just say 日本(にほん)で.

～の中で · Nuance & register (cont.)

Adding は - の中(なか)では - topicalises the field and quietly implies a contrast: "within this set it's true; elsewhere, who knows." 和食(わしょく)の中(なか)ではおすしがいちばん好(す)きです leaves room for some non-Japanese dish to be the overall winner.

～の中で · ～の中(なか)で vs bare で

Rule of thumb - a set of members takes の中(なか)で; a place or a period takes plain で.

家族(かぞく)の中(なか)で父(ちち)がいちばんよく笑(わら)います。
My father laughs the most of anyone in the family.

この町(まち)で一番(いちばん)古(ふる)いお寺(てら)はどこですか。
Which is the oldest temple in this town?

A family is a collection of individuals you can line up and compare - the fence is real, so 中(なか) earns its place. A town is where the comparing happens, not what is being compared, and inserting 中(なか) there points at geography instead of at a set.

～の中で · ～の中(なか)で vs ～の中(なか)から

Rule of thumb - で compares within the set; から takes something out of it.

この中(なか)で、いちばん軽(かる)いのはどれですか。
Which of these is the lightest?

この中(なか)から、好(す)きなものを一(ひと)つ選(えら)んでください。
Please choose one thing you like from these.

Verbs of choosing and extracting - 選(えら)ぶ, 取(と)り出(だ)す, 決(き)める - pull the item out, so they want から. Adjectival judgements leave everything where it is, so they want で. Using 中(なか)で選(えら)ぶ is not impossible, but it shifts the meaning to "do your choosing inside this room".

～の中で · ～の中(なか)で vs より

Rule of thumb - three or more members: の中(なか)で plus a superlative. Exactly two: より.

この三(さん)種類(しゅるい)の中(なか)で、これがいちばん甘(あま)いです。
Of these three varieties, this one is the sweetest.

こちらのほうがあちらより甘(あま)いです。
This one is sweeter than that one.

The mistake to avoid is running a two-item set through the superlative frame. 紅茶(こうちゃ)と緑茶(りょくちゃ)の中(なか)でどちらがいいですか is overbuilt - with two candidates on the table, drop the fence and just ask 紅茶(こうちゃ)と緑茶(りょくちゃ)と、どちらがいいですか.

～の中で · Common mistakes

  • ❌ このの中(なか)で → ✅ この中(なか)で
    この, その, あの already attach to the following noun. Doubling the の is the single most frequent slip on this pattern.

  • ❌ 果物(くだもの)の中(なか)にみかんがいちばん好(す)きです。 → ✅ 果物(くだもの)の中(なか)でみかんがいちばん好(す)きです。
    に would say the tangerines are physically located inside the fruit. で is the particle that turns a set into an arena for comparison.

  • ❌ 犬(いぬ)と猫(ねこ)の中(なか)で、猫(ねこ)がいちばん好(す)きです。 → ✅ 犬(いぬ)と猫(ねこ)と、猫(ねこ)のほうが好(す)きです。
    Two candidates cannot have a "number one". A superlative needs a field of at least three.

  • ❌ 世界(せかい)の中(なか)でこの湖(みずうみ)がいちばん深(ふか)いです。 → ✅ 世界(せかい)でこの湖(みずうみ)がいちばん深(ふか)いです。
    世界(せかい), 日本(にほん), 町(まち) and the like are places. Attaching 中(なか) to them sounds like you are drawing a line on a map.

～の中で · Practice 1

1. 一年(いちねん)____二月(にがつ)がいちばん寒(さむ)いです。

一年(いちねん)で二月(にがつ)がいちばん寒(さむ)いです。

A year is a stretch of time, not a bag of members, so it takes plain で. 一年(いちねん)の中(なか)で is heard, but it makes the year sound like a container you are rummaging in; the bare で is what a native speaker says.

～の中で · Practice 2

2. Choose the natural option: 三(さん)つのプラン（の中(なか)で・の中(なか)から）一(ひと)つ選(えら)んでください。

三(さん)つのプランの中(なか)から一(ひと)つ選(えら)んでください。

選(えら)ぶ physically removes one item from the group, and the source of a movement is から. Use の中(なか)で when nothing is being taken out - when you are only judging which member wins.

～の中で · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "Of the films I saw last year, this one was the most interesting."

去年(きょねん)見(み)た映画(えいが)の中(なか)で、これがいちばんおもしろかったです。

去年(きょねん)見(み)た is a clause modifying 映画(えいが), and the whole noun phrase then takes の中(なか)で. Note that 映画(えいが) is plural here without any marking - Japanese leaves number to context, and the fence does the work of "of the films".

～の中で · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: あのの中(なか)にどれが一番(いちばん)便利(べんり)ですか。

✅ あの中(なか)でどれが一番(いちばん)便利(べんり)ですか。

Two fixes. First, あの attaches straight to 中(なか) - there is no の in between, because あの is already the modifier. Second, the particle on 中(なか) must be で: the set is the arena in which the comparison is carried out, and に would only say where something sits.

～の中で · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 日本料理(にほんりょうり)の中(なか)で、てんぷらがいちばん好(す)きです。 (b) 日本料理(にほんりょうり)の中(なか)では、てんぷらがいちばん好(す)きです。

(a) simply states the winner inside a stated field: among Japanese dishes, tempura tops the list.

(b) adds は, which topicalises the field and hints at a boundary - "as far as Japanese food goes, tempura." It leaves open that something outside that set, maybe a curry or a steak, would beat it overall. The は is doing its usual contrastive work.

AとBとどちらが

Meaning · which is more ～, A or B? - the question form of a two-way comparison

You already know how to state a comparison. This is how to ask for one. Put the two candidates side by side with と, then let どちら stand in for the answer. The reply comes back with の方(ほう)が, which is why the two patterns are really one conversation split in half.

AとBとどちらが · Formation

The frame is fixed: A と B と、どちらが + adjective + か. The second と is usually there and usually followed by a comma when written.

AとBとどちらが · Formation (cont.)

Attaches to Form Example
Two nouns AとBと、どちらが 春(はる)と秋(あき)と、どちらが好(す)きですか
Two verbs Vるのと Vるのと、どちらが 歩(ある)くのと走(はし)るのと、どちらが
Two い-adjective options ～いのと～いのと 安(やす)いのと軽(かる)いのと
With 方(ほう) spelled out どちらの方(ほう)が どちらの方(ほう)が近(ちか)いですか
Casual どっち（の方(ほう)）が どっちが速(はや)い?
The answer Bの方(ほう)が + adjective + です 秋(あき)の方(ほう)が好(す)きです
Answering "both" どちらも + affirmative どちらも好(す)きです
Answering "neither" どちらも + negative どちらも好(す)きではありません

AとBとどちらが · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - with exactly two candidates the question word is どちら, whatever they are. Not どれ for things, not だれ for people, not どこ for places. Two means どちら.

Note - どちら takes が, never は. It is the unknown in the sentence, and unknowns sit on the が side. The same applies to the answer: 秋(あき)の方(ほう)が.

AとBとどちらが · Examples

コーヒーと紅茶(こうちゃ)と、どちらがいいですか。
Which would you like, coffee or tea?

朝(あさ)と夜(よる)と、どちらが忙(いそが)しいですか。
Which is busier for you, mornings or evenings?

新幹線(しんかんせん)と飛行機(ひこうき)と、どちらが安(やす)いですか。
Which is cheaper, the bullet train or flying?

この二(ふた)つ、どっちが似合(にあ)うと思(おも)う?
Which of these two do you think suits me better?

自分(じぶん)で作(つく)るのと店(みせ)で買(か)うのと、どちらが早(はや)いでしょうか。
Which would be quicker, making it myself or buying it in a shop?

AとBとどちらが · Examples (cont.)

田中(たなか)さんと山田(やまだ)さんと、どちらが先(さき)に着(つ)きましたか。
Which of them arrived first, Tanaka or Yamada?

会議(かいぎ)は水曜日(すいようび)と木曜日(もくようび)と、どちらがご都合(つごう)がよろしいですか。
For the meeting, which suits you better - Wednesday or Thursday?

引(ひ)っ越(こ)すなら、駅(えき)の近(ちか)くと公園(こうえん)の近(ちか)くと、どちらがいいと思(おも)いますか。
If you were moving, which do you think would be better - near the station or near the park?

AとBとどちらが · Nuance & register

どちら is the neutral-to-polite form and works everywhere: with strangers, with customers, in writing. どっち is its casual contraction and is extremely common in relaxed speech among friends and family. There is nothing rude about どっち between equals, but it lands badly in a shop or an office.

Adding の方(ほう) - どちらの方(ほう)が - is slightly more deliberate and a shade more formal. It is never wrong, and it is what you hear when someone is choosing their words carefully.

どちら has a second job worth knowing: it is the polite word for "where" and "which one" in general. どちらからいらっしゃいましたか means "where have you come from", and どちらさまですか is a formal "who are you?". So when you hear どちら, check whether two options were actually put on the table.

AとBとどちらが · Nuance & register (cont.)

Politeness at the top end: いかがですか or よろしいですか replace いいですか, and でしょうか softens the question further. To a customer, a shop assistant is more likely to say どちらになさいますか than どちらがいいですか.

One thing English speakers forget: this is not a yes/no question. Answering はい to どちらがいいですか leaves the other person waiting.

AとBとどちらが · どちらが vs どれが

Rule of thumb - two candidates: どちら. Three or more: どれ.

赤(あか)と青(あお)と、どちらが好(す)きですか。
Which do you prefer, red or blue?

この五(いつ)つの色(いろ)の中(なか)で、どれが好(す)きですか。
Of these five colours, which do you like?

The split is about how many options exist, not about what kind of thing they are. This is also why the two patterns come with different frames: どれ normally needs a stated field with の中(なか)で, while どちら carries its own field in the AとBと part.

AとBとどちらが · どちらが vs どっちが

Rule of thumb - same grammar, different clothes. どっち is the casual form; どちら is the one to default to when you are unsure.

ねえ、どっちがおいしそう?
Hey, which one looks better?

お飲(の)み物(もの)は、どちらがよろしいでしょうか。
Which would you prefer to drink?

Notice that the register is carried by the whole sentence, not just the question word. どっち sits with plain forms and a rising intonation; どちら sits with です・ます, よろしい and でしょうか. Mixing them - ❌どっちがよろしいでしょうか - sounds like two people talking at once.

AとBとどちらが · どちらが vs どちらか・どちらも

Rule of thumb - どちらが asks which one. どちらか means "one or the other, I don't mind which". どちらも means "both" with an affirmative verb and "neither" with a negative one.

傘(かさ)は、どちらが軽(かる)いですか。
Which umbrella is lighter?

傘(かさ)はどちらか一(ひと)つ持(も)っていってください。
Take one umbrella or the other with you.

傘(かさ)はどちらも古(ふる)いです。
Both umbrellas are old.

AとBとどちらが · どちらが vs どちらか・どちらも (cont.)

か and も are doing exactly what they do after だれ and なに: か makes the identity vague, も sweeps in everything. The negative flip on どちらも is automatic - どちらも新(あたら)しくないです is "neither is new".

AとBとどちらが · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 紅茶(こうちゃ)と緑茶(りょくちゃ)と、どれが好(す)きですか。 → ✅ 紅茶(こうちゃ)と緑茶(りょくちゃ)と、どちらが好(す)きですか。
    Only two things were named. どれ needs a field of three or more.

  • ❌ 姉(あね)と妹(いもうと)と、だれが背(せ)が高(たか)いですか。 → ✅ 姉(あね)と妹(いもうと)と、どちらが背(せ)が高(たか)いですか。
    Even for people, two options means どちら. だれ opens the field to everybody.

  • ❌ どちらは安(やす)いですか。 → ✅ どちらが安(やす)いですか。
    A question word is the unknown, and it cannot be a topic. どちらが, always.

  • ❌ 「どちらが好(す)きですか。」「はい、コーヒーです。」 → ✅ 「コーヒーの方(ほう)が好(す)きです。」
    This is a choice question, not a yes/no one. The answer names a winner, normally with の方(ほう)が.

AとBとどちらが · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 和食(わしょく)と洋食(ようしょく)と、どちらが好(す)きですか。 (b) 和食(わしょく)と洋食(ようしょく)と、どちらも好(す)きですか。

など

Meaning · such as; things like - it declares a list unfinished

など is a full stop that refuses to be one. You name one or two items, add など, and the listener understands that more of the same kind exist but are not worth spelling out. Its second use grows straight out of the first: if you present something as a mere example, you are also saying it is nothing special - which is how など becomes modest about yourself and dismissive about everything else.

など · Formation

など · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - や joins items; など closes the list. They are not alternatives, and the two together - AやBなど - is the most common shape of all.

There's a convenience store, a post office and the like around here, so it's handy.

など · Nuance & register

In its plain listing sense など is neutral, and if it leans anywhere it leans written and careful. It is the form you want in a report, an announcement or a polite explanation to a customer.

Its two informal twins are worth keeping straight:

  • なんか is the everyday spoken form. Same meaning, no formality.
    漫画(まんが)なんか読(よ)まないよ. Very frequent, completely ordinary in conversation, out of place in writing.
  • なんて adds feeling - surprise, contempt, or dismissal.
    日本語(にほんご)なんて簡単(かんたん)だ is boastful; 私(わたし)なんて is self-deprecating. It is not a neutral option.

The softening use is one you will hear constantly and rarely see taught: offering something as a mere example makes the offer less pushy. コーヒーなどいかがですか is warmer and less presumptuous than コーヒーはいかがですか, because it pretends not to have decided anything for you.

など · Nuance & register (cont.)

The dismissive use cuts both ways, and the direction depends on who the noun refers to. Pointed at yourself it is humble and perfectly polite: 私(わたし)などが申(もう)し上(あ)げることではありませんが. Pointed at someone else it belittles them, so ❌田中(たなか)さんなどに聞(き)いてみます, said in front of Tanaka, is an insult you did not intend.

など · など vs や

Rule of thumb - や sits between two nouns and links them. など sits after the last noun and stops the list without finishing it.

机(つくえ)の上(うえ)にノートやはさみがあります。
There's a notebook, scissors and so on on the desk.

机(つくえ)の上(うえ)にノートやはさみなどがあります。
There's a notebook, scissors and that sort of thing on the desk.

や already implies the list is partial, so など is not strictly required - it just makes the implication explicit and a little more polite. What you cannot do is swap their positions: ❌ノートなどはさみ joins nothing.

など · など vs なんか・なんて

Rule of thumb - one meaning, three registers: など written and neutral, なんか casual and spoken, なんて emotional.

報告書(ほうこくしょ)には人数(にんずう)や費用(ひよう)などを書(か)きます。
In the report we write the number of people, the cost and so on.

そんなこと、私(わたし)なんかに聞(き)かないでよ。
Don't ask me about that, of all people.

一人(ひとり)で行(い)くなんて、あぶないよ。
Going alone - that's dangerous.

Note the third one: なんて will attach to a whole verb phrase, which など resists in speech. なんて also tends to run straight into the predicate without a case particle behind it.

など · など vs とか

Rule of thumb - とか is repeated after each item and is casual; など appears once at the end and is the neutral or written choice.

週末(しゅうまつ)は買(か)い物(もの)とか散歩(さんぽ)とかしています。
At weekends I go shopping, take walks, that sort of thing.

週末(しゅうまつ)は買(か)い物(もの)や散歩(さんぽ)などをしています。
At weekends I do things like shopping and going for walks.

Both say the same thing about the world. The difference is who is speaking: the first is a friend over coffee, the second could go in a self-introduction. とか also attaches happily to verbs and whole clauses, where など has to be helped along by a noun.

など · Common mistakes

  • ❌ パンなど卵(たまご)を買(か)いました。 → ✅ パンや卵(たまご)などを買(か)いました。
    など is not a conjunction. It cannot stand between two nouns; it can only close the list from behind.

  • ❌ 野菜(やさい)をなど買(か)います。 → ✅ 野菜(やさい)などを買(か)います。
    The case particle goes outside など, because など belongs to the noun phrase and を belongs to the sentence.

  • ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)などにご相談(そうだん)します。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)にご相談(そうだん)します。
    Attaching など to a person you are showing respect to reduces them to a random example. Keep the dismissive など for yourself.

など · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ この資料(しりょう)には住所(じゅうしょ)や電話番号(でんわばんごう)なんかが書(か)いてあります。 → ✅ ～電話番号(でんわばんごう)などが書(か)いてあります。
    Not ungrammatical, but なんか is spoken register and jars against a written document. Match the word to the medium.

～とか～とか

Meaning · things like A and B - a casual, deliberately incomplete list

とか tags each item as one example among others. Two or three go on the record, the rest stay implied, and the whole thing sounds relaxed rather than official. Unlike the tidier や, it will happily list verbs, adjectives and whole complaints, which is why spoken Japanese leans on it so heavily.

～とか～とか · Formation

とか follows each item, including - usually - the last one.

Attaches to Form Example
Noun Nとか Nとか すし → すしとかラーメンとか
Verb (plain form) Vるとか Vるとか 歩(ある)くとか走(はし)るとか

Rule of thumb - the final とか is optional but normal. Leaving it on is what makes the list feel open; cutting it off after the last item makes the sentence sound as though you had planned it.

Note - with な-adjectives and nouns the standard written form inserts だ: 静(しず)かだとか. In fast speech you will hear the bare 静(しず)かとか as well; treat だとか as the safe form.

～とか～とか · Examples

朝(あさ)はパンとかヨーグルトとかを食(た)べます。
For breakfast I have bread, yoghurt, that sort of thing.

音楽(おんがく)はジャズとかクラシックとかが好(す)きです。
Music-wise I like jazz, classical and so on.

明日(あした)とか、時間(じかん)ある?
Are you free tomorrow or something?

休(やす)みの日(ひ)は部屋(へや)を片付(かたづ)けるとか、買(か)い物(もの)に行(い)くとかしています。
On my days off I tidy the flat, go shopping, things like that.

彼(かれ)は忙(いそが)しいとか疲(つか)れたとか言(い)って、集(あつ)まりに来(こ)ません。
He says he's busy, he's tired and so on, and never turns up to the get-togethers.

～とか～とか · Examples (cont.)

沖縄(おきなわ)とか北海道(ほっかいどう)とか、遠(とお)いところへ行(い)ってみたいなあ。
Okinawa, Hokkaido - I'd love to go somewhere far away like that.

アルバイトを探(さが)すなら、カフェとか本屋(ほんや)とかがいいんじゃない?
If you're looking for part-time work, wouldn't a cafe or a bookshop or somewhere be good?

面接(めんせつ)では志望(しぼう)の理由(りゆう)とか将来(しょうらい)の計画(けいかく)とかを聞(き)かれました。
In the interview they asked me about my reasons for applying, my future plans, that kind of thing.

～とか～とか · Nuance & register

とか is casual and spoken. It is not slang and no one will think less of you for it in conversation, but it does not belong in a report, a formal email or an exam essay - the neutral equivalents there are や and など.

Piled on too thickly it makes a speaker sound vague and non-committal, which is exactly why it is useful when you want to be non-committal:

  • Softening a proposal. 来週(らいしゅう)の火曜日(かようび)とか、どうですか
    floats a date instead of demanding one. This is one of the most common modern uses and it involves no listing at all.
  • Distancing yourself from what you are reporting.
    中止(ちゅうし)になるとか聞(き)きました - "I heard something about it being cancelled." You pass on the information without vouching for it.
  • Quoting a complaint you find tiresome. The
    ～とか～とか言(い)って frame in the fifth example above carries an audible eye-roll.

～とか～とか · Nuance & register (cont.)

とかいう deserves its own line: 田中(たなか)とかいう人(ひと)が来(き)ました means "some person called Tanaka, or something like that, came". It signals that you barely registered the name - polite in a self-effacing way when the name is yours to remember, faintly rude when it is a person the listener cares about.

Written forms of the same word exist（だとか, ～とか～とかいう）, but if you are choosing between registers, the rule is simple: speech takes とか, paper takes や and など.

～とか～とか · とか vs や

Rule of thumb - both leave the list open. や goes between nouns only and is register-neutral; とか goes after each item, is casual, and takes verbs and adjectives as well.

棚(たな)に辞書(じしょ)や地図(ちず)が置(お)いてあります。
There are dictionaries, maps and so on on the shelf.

棚(たな)に辞書(じしょ)とか地図(ちず)とか置(お)いてあるよ。
There are dictionaries and maps and stuff on the shelf.

The first could be a museum label; the second could not. And where や simply stops working, とか carries on:

泳(およ)ぐとか走(はし)るとか、体(からだ)を動(うご)かすことが好(す)きです。
I like moving my body - swimming, running, that sort of thing.

～とか～とか · とか vs や (cont.)

❌泳(およ)ぐや走(はし)る is impossible: や joins nouns and nothing else.

～とか～とか · とか vs ～たり～たり

Rule of thumb - ～たり～たり lists actions and has to be closed with する. とか just lists, and needs no closing verb.

夜(よる)は音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)いたり、本(ほん)を読(よ)んだりします。
In the evening I listen to music, read, and so on.

夜(よる)は音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)くとか、本(ほん)を読(よ)むとかかな。
Evenings are, like, listening to music or reading, I suppose.

～たり is neutral in register and grammatically tighter - the する at the end is not optional. とか is looser in both senses. Note also that とか can list nouns, which ～たり cannot.

～とか～とか · とか vs か

Rule of thumb - か offers a genuine either/or. とか offers examples and asks you to choose nothing.

コーヒーか紅茶(こうちゃ)を持(も)ってきてください。
Please bring coffee or tea.

コーヒーとか紅茶(こうちゃ)とかを持(も)ってきてください。
Please bring coffee, tea, something along those lines.

With か the listener picks one of exactly two. With とか the two named drinks are only illustrations, and green tea would satisfy the request perfectly well. This is also why a question offering a real choice cannot use とか.

～とか～とか · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 泳(およ)ぐや走(はし)るが好(す)きです。 → ✅ 泳(およ)ぐとか走(はし)るとかが好(す)きです。
    や links nouns and only nouns. When the items are actions, とか is the listing particle that works.

  • ❌ この書類(しょるい)には名前(なまえ)とか住所(じゅうしょ)とかを書(か)いてください。 → ✅ ～名前(なまえ)や住所(じゅうしょ)などを書(か)いてください。
    Grammatically fine, socially wrong. A written instruction takes や and など; とか makes an official form sound like a chat.

  • ❌ 肉(にく)とか魚(さかな)とか、どちらが好(す)きですか。 → ✅ 肉(にく)と魚(さかな)と、どちらが好(す)きですか。
    どちら asks for a choice between two fixed options, but とか says the two named items are merely examples. The two ideas cancel each other out.

  • ❌ とかコーヒーとか紅茶(こうちゃ)が飲(の)みたい。 → ✅ コーヒーとか紅茶(こうちゃ)とか飲(の)みたい。
    とか is a postposition: it follows the item it tags. It never leads.

いたします is humble, formal Japanese, and とか is casual speech; the two cannot share a sentence. Swap in や and など and the register lines up from beginning to end.

～とか～とか · Practice 5

でも

Meaning · ～ or something; ～ or the like - names one thing as a token example

Attach でも to a noun in a suggestion and the noun stops being a demand. You put one option on the table as a representative of a whole class of acceptable options, and the listener is left free to counter with something else. It is the single most useful politeness trick in casual invitations.

でも · Formation

でも follows the noun. It replaces が and を, and stacks on top of に, へ and と.

Attaches to Form Example
Noun Noun + でも お茶(ちゃ) → お茶(ちゃ)でも
Noun taking を を is dropped ケーキでも食(た)べよう
Noun taking が が is dropped 音楽(おんがく)でもあるといいね
Noun taking に／へ／と に／へ／と + でも 海(うみ)にでも行(い)こう

でも · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - this でも lives in sentences about what might happen: invitations, suggestions, offers, plans, volitional forms. It cannot report a completed fact. ❌昨日(きのう)コーヒーでも飲(の)みました is not a sentence you can rescue with context.

Note - three different things share these two kana. This page is about the one that follows a noun in a suggestion. There is also the concessive ～ても／でも that follows a verb or an adjective （雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っても, "even if it rains"）, and the sentence-initial でも meaning "but". Same shape, unrelated jobs.

でも · Examples

少(すこ)し休(やす)んで、お茶(ちゃ)でも飲(の)みませんか。
Let's take a break - shall we have some tea or something?

映画(えいが)でも見(み)に行(い)こうか。
Shall we go and see a film or something?

週末(しゅうまつ)は公園(こうえん)にでも行(い)きましょうか。
Shall we go to the park or somewhere at the weekend?

暇(ひま)なら、本(ほん)でも読(よ)んだらどう?
If you're free, why not read a book or something?

ケーキでも買(か)って帰(かえ)ろうかな。
Maybe I'll pick up a cake or something on the way home.

でも · Examples (cont.)

一人(ひとり)で悩(なや)まないで、先生(せんせい)にでも相談(そうだん)してみたらどうですか。
Don't sit with it on your own - why not talk to your teacher or somebody?

疲(つか)れたでしょう。冷(つめ)たいものでも飲(の)んで、少(すこ)し休(やす)んでください。
You must be tired. Have something cold to drink and take a rest.

来週(らいしゅう)の土曜日(どようび)、時間(じかん)があったら食事(しょくじ)にでも行(い)きませんか。
If you have time next Saturday, would you like to go out for a meal or something?

でも · Nuance & register

The whole point of this でも is not committing the listener. Compare the two invitations:

  • お茶(ちゃ)を飲(の)みませんか - "would you like to drink tea?" The listener
    has to accept tea or refuse the invitation.
  • お茶(ちゃ)でも飲(の)みませんか - "shall we have tea or something?" The
    listener can say "sure, but I'd rather have juice" without refusing anything.

That softness makes it standard in casual and semi-polite invitations at every level of formality: ～でも食(た)べようか between friends, ～でもいかがですか to a guest. It is register-neutral in itself; the politeness is carried by the ending.

でも · Nuance & register (cont.)

There is one situation where it backfires. If the other person has just told you exactly what they want, offering it back with でも treats their request as arbitrary. 「コーヒーが飲(の)みたい」「じゃあコーヒーでも買(か)ってくるね」 reads as mildly dismissive - they asked for coffee, not for coffee-or-whatever. Drop the でも and use を.

The related question word + でも pattern - いつでも, どこでも, だれでも, 何(なん)でも - means "any at all", and it is a different construction with the same particle: だれでもできる仕事(しごと)です, "it's a job anyone can do". Worth knowing here because learners meet both in the same week and mix them.

Finally, でも is not the only softener of its kind. とか does a similar job （明日(あした)とかどう?）but is more casual and attaches happily to times and dates; でも sits more comfortably with the thing being consumed or done.

でも · ～でも vs ～を

Rule of thumb - を names the thing you mean. でも names one thing you would accept.

昼(ひる)ごはんにそばを食(た)べましょう。
Let's have soba for lunch.

昼(ひる)ごはんにそばでも食(た)べましょう。
Let's have soba or something for lunch.

The first has decided; the second is thinking aloud. Note that を disappears entirely when でも moves in - ❌そばをでも is impossible, because both are fighting for the same slot.

でも · Noun + でも vs question word + でも

Rule of thumb - noun + でも offers an example. Question word + でも sweeps in the whole category: "any at all".

飲(の)み物(もの)でも買(か)ってきます。
I'll go and buy some drinks or something.

飲(の)み物(もの)なら何(なん)でもいいです。
As for drinks, anything at all is fine.

The first suggests one purchase among several possible ones; the second refuses to narrow anything down. The same contrast runs through the family: どこでも "anywhere", いつでも "any time", だれでも "anyone".

でも · ～でも vs ～ても・でも（even if）

Rule of thumb - check what comes before it. A noun means "or something". A verb or adjective stem means "even if".

散歩(さんぽ)にでも行(い)きませんか。
Shall we go for a walk or something?

雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っても散歩(さんぽ)に行(い)きます。
I'm going for a walk even if it rains.

They are not related in meaning at all, and the second one is not really でも - it is the て-form plus も, which turns into でも only after verbs whose て-form ends in で（読(よ)んでも）. And a でも at the very front of a sentence is the conjunction "but": でも、今日(きょう)は忙(いそが)しいです。

でも · Common mistakes

  • ❌ ケーキをでも食(た)べましょう。 → ✅ ケーキでも食(た)べましょう。
    でも takes the place of を and が. Only に, へ and と survive alongside it.

  • ❌ 昨日(きのう)コーヒーでも飲(の)みました。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)コーヒーを飲(の)みました。
    You cannot be vague about what you already did. This でも only works where the action is still open - suggestions, offers, plans.

  • ❌ 「紅茶(こうちゃ)がほしいです。」「じゃ、紅茶(こうちゃ)でも入(い)れますね。」 → ✅ 「じゃ、紅茶(こうちゃ)を入(い)れますね。」
    When the other person has named exactly what they want, でも says you were not really listening.

  • ❌ でも、音楽(おんがく)聞(き)きませんか。（meaning "music or something"） → ✅ 音楽(おんがく)でも聞(き)きませんか。
    At the head of a sentence でも is the conjunction "but". The softening でも has to sit on the noun.

1. 雨(あめ)でどこにも行(い)けませんね。うちでDVD____見(み)ましょうか。

The speaker is proposing a way to spend the afternoon, not insisting on DVDs. With でも the other person can suggest a board game instead without turning anything down, which is exactly what makes the invitation comfortable.

でも · Practice 2

でも · Practice 4

でも · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 山田(やまだ)さんに聞(き)いてみます。 (b) 山田(やまだ)さんにでも聞(き)いてみます。

ばかり - only; nothing but

Meaning · nothing but ～; ～ and nothing else - usually with the feeling that there is too much of it

ばかり draws the same line だけ does, then adds an opinion. It says one thing fills the whole picture and, nine times out of ten, that this is too much of it. It is also not a mathematician: someone who 肉(にく)ばかり食(た)べている probably eats a salad occasionally. ばかり is about proportion and attitude, not arithmetic.

Attaches to Form Example

Verb (habitual excess) て-form + ばかりいる 遊(あそ)ぶ → 遊(あそ)んでばかりいる

Rule of thumb - if you can add "…and I'm getting tired of it" to the English, ばかり is the right word. If you are just stating a boundary, you want だけ.

弟(おとうと)はゲームばかりしています。
My little brother does nothing but play games.

You mustn't live on nothing but sweets.

Stop complaining all the time and give me a hand.

My son does nothing but sleep - he won't even try to study.

Lately it's been rain, rain, rain, and the washing won't dry.

ばかり - only; nothing but · Examples (cont.)

He only ever talks about himself and never listens to anyone else.

In the meeting they went over the same ground again and again, and the time just drained away.

Last year was nothing but good news - a genuinely happy year.

ばかり - only; nothing but · Nuance & register

ばかり itself is register-neutral: 遊(あそ)んでばかりいます and 遊(あそ)んでばかりいる differ only in politeness of ending. The contraction ばっかり（and the clipped ばっか）is casual and adds emphasis and feeling - ゲームばっかりして! is what a parent shouts, not what a report says.

The attitude is the heart of the word. In the great majority of sentences it is criticism, either of somebody else（文句(もんく)ばかり）or, turned inward, an apology（ご迷惑(めいわく)ばかりかけてすみません, "I do nothing but cause you trouble"）. That inward use is genuinely humble and very common in polite speech.

But the underlying idea is excess, not badness, and excess is occasionally welcome. いいことばかりでした and 好(す)きな人(ひと)ばかり集(あつ)まったパーティー are both positive. What you cannot do is use ばかり neutrally: it always comments.

Two smaller points:

ばかり - only; nothing but · Nuance & register (cont.)

  • The counting ばかり - 一週間(いっしゅうかん)ばかり休(やす)みます, "I'll
    take about a week off" - is formal and slightly old-fashioned. Recognise it; in speech use ぐらい.
  • ～たばかり is a different pattern meaning "just did", and it is
    built on the past form: 着(つ)いたばかりです, "I've only just arrived". The contrast section below separates them.

ばかり - only; nothing but · ばかり vs だけ

Rule of thumb - だけ reports a boundary and says nothing about how it feels. ばかり says the boundary is too tight, and does not claim to be exact.

昼(ひる)ごはんはパンだけでした。
Lunch was just bread.

昼(ひる)ごはんはパンばかりです。
It's bread for lunch every single time.

The first is a fact about one lunch: bread, nothing else, no comment. The second complains about a pattern across many lunches - and would still be a fair thing to say on a day when you also had an apple. That inexactness is why ばかり and a precise number sit badly together: 三人(さんにん)だけ来(き)ました is a count, 三人(さんにん)ばかり来(き)ました means "about three people came" and belongs to the other, formal ばかり.

ばかり - only; nothing but · ばかり vs だけ (cont.)

テレビを見(み)ている人(ひと)ばかりで、手伝(てつだ)ってくれる人(ひと)がいません。
Everyone's just sitting watching television and nobody will help.

Swap だけ in there and the annoyance evaporates.

ばかり - only; nothing but · ばかり vs ～しか～ない

Rule of thumb - ばかり complains that there is too much of one thing. しか～ない complains that there is not enough of everything else.

冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)にビールばかり入(はい)っています。
The fridge is full of nothing but beer.

冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)にビールしか入(はい)っていません。
There's nothing in the fridge but beer.

Both describe the same fridge and both are unhappy, but they are unhappy about different things. The first is disgusted by the quantity of beer; the second is disappointed that there is no food. Note the grammar too: ばかり takes a positive verb, しか demands a negative one.

ばかり - only; nothing but · Noun + ばかり vs ～たばかり

Rule of thumb - look at what is in front. A noun or a て-form gives you "nothing but". A past-tense verb gives you "just did".

日本(にほん)に来(き)たばかりで、まだ漢字(かんじ)が読(よ)めません。
I've only just come to Japan, so I still can't read kanji.

日本(にほん)へ来(き)てから、コンビニのごはんばかり食(た)べています。
Since coming to Japan I've eaten nothing but convenience store food.

The first is about time - the arrival was recent. The second is about proportion - the diet is monotonous. Learners produce ❌来(き)るばかりです for "I just arrived"; the pattern needs the plain past 来(き)た.

ばかり - only; nothing but · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 水(みず)をばかり飲(の)んでいます。 → ✅ 水(みず)ばかり飲(の)んでいます。
    が and を disappear in front of ばかり. If you want the object marked, it goes behind instead: 水(みず)ばかりを飲(の)む.

  • ❌ 遊(あそ)ぶばかりいます。 → ✅ 遊(あそ)んでばかりいます。
    With いる, the verb has to be in て-form. The plain form works only in the V(plain) + ばかりだ frame, which has no いる.

  • ❌ きのうは一人(ひとり)ばかり来(き)ました。（meaning "only one person came"） → ✅ きのうは一人(ひとり)しか来(き)ませんでした。
    ばかり cannot mean "a mere N". With a number it either means "approximately" or nothing at all; the "disappointingly few" job belongs to しか～ない.

  • ❌ 野菜(やさい)ばかり食(た)べて、健康(けんこう)にいいですね。 → ✅ 野菜(やさい)をたくさん食(た)べていて、健康(けんこう)にいいですね。
    ばかり on somebody's habits reads as a complaint, so pairing it with praise sounds sarcastic. Use it when you mean the criticism.

～だけで

Meaning · just by doing ～; with only ～ - that small amount is all it takes

だけ draws the line; the で behind it says "and that line is where the cause sits". So ～だけで is not simply "only" - it links a minimal amount of effort, time, money or evidence to a result that follows from it. Sometimes the tone is admiring ("that's all it takes!") and sometimes dismissive ("that's all it is").

～だけで · Formation

だけ attaches as it always does, and で follows it.

Attaches to Form Example
Verb (plain) Vる + だけで 見(み)る → 見(み)るだけで
Verb (past) Vた + だけで 聞(き)いた → 聞(き)いただけで
Noun Noun + だけで 水(みず) → 水(みず)だけで

Rule of thumb - if the sentence goes on to say what results from the small amount, you want だけで. If it simply reports the amount and stops, plain だけ is enough.

～だけで · Examples

顔(かお)を見(み)るだけで元気(げんき)になります。
Just seeing your face cheers me up.

ボタンを押(お)すだけでお湯(ゆ)が出(で)ます。
Hot water comes out just by pressing the button.

考(かんが)えるだけで怖(こわ)くなります。
It frightens me just to think about it.

一人(ひとり)だけで全部(ぜんぶ)運(はこ)ぶのは無理(むり)ですよ。
There's no way you can carry all of it with just one person.

スマホだけで切符(きっぷ)が買(か)えます。
You can buy a ticket with nothing but your phone.

～だけで · Examples (cont.)

話(はなし)を聞(き)いただけで、何(なに)が起(お)きたかすぐに分(わ)かりました。
Just from hearing about it, I knew straight away what had happened.

この仕事(しごと)は給料(きゅうりょう)がいいだけで、やりがいがありません。
This job has nothing going for it but the pay - there's no satisfaction in it.

毎朝(まいあさ)十分(じゅっぷん)歩(ある)くだけで、体(からだ)の調子(ちょうし)がずいぶんよくなりました。
Just walking for ten minutes every morning has made me feel a great deal better.

～だけで · Nuance & register

Register-neutral in itself; it takes its politeness from the ending. Two very different tones live inside the same three kana, and which one you hear depends on what the result is:

  • "That's all it takes." The result is good and the effort is tiny.
    Instruction manuals, advertising and recipes are built out of this: 混(ま)ぜるだけでできます. In speech it is warm and encouraging.
  • "That's all it is." Here だけで follows an adjective and the
    second half of the sentence takes something away: 新(あたら)しいだけで、使(つか)いにくい. This is faint praise turning into a complaint, and it is quite cutting.

The frame ～だけでいい - "that's all you need to do" - is worth memorising whole. It is how permission and reassurance are given in Japanese, and it is much softer than an imperative: ここにサインするだけでいいです is far friendlier than ここにサインしてください.

～だけで · Nuance & register (cont.)

Adding は - だけでは - flips the sentence to "that alone is not enough", and it almost always precedes a negative: 練習(れんしゅう)だけでは上手(じょうず)になりません. The は is the ordinary contrastive は, and leaving it out sounds unfinished.

～だけで · ～だけで vs ～だけ

Rule of thumb - plain だけ reports an amount. だけで makes that amount the cause of what comes next.

昨日(きのう)は水(みず)だけ飲(の)みました。
Yesterday I drank only water.

水(みず)だけで三日間(みっかかん)生(い)きられますか。
Can you survive for three days on water alone?

In the first, water is what got drunk. In the second, water is the means by which surviving happens. Slipping a で into the first sentence - ❌水(みず)だけで飲(の)みました - would say you drank something using only water, which is nonsense.

～だけで · ～だけで vs ～だけでは

Rule of thumb - だけで says the small amount is enough. だけでは says it is not.

この説明(せつめい)を読(よ)むだけで分(わ)かります。
You can understand it just by reading this explanation.

この説明(せつめい)を読(よ)むだけでは分(わ)かりません。
Just reading this explanation isn't enough to understand it.

The は is contrastive: it sets "reading the explanation" against everything else you would also have to do. Because of that, the predicate after だけでは is nearly always negative - 足(た)りない, できない, いけない, 分(わ)からない.

～だけで · ～だけで vs ～ば・～と

Rule of thumb - a conditional says if X, then Y. だけで says X and nothing more is required for Y.

説明書(せつめいしょ)を読(よ)めば使(つか)い方(かた)が分(わ)かります。
If you read the manual you'll understand how to use it.

説明書(せつめいしょ)を読(よ)むだけで使(つか)い方(かた)が分(わ)かります。
Just reading the manual is enough to understand how to use it.

The conditional states a route to the result; だけで also insists the route is short. That extra claim is what makes it the natural choice for anything selling ease - and what makes it sound sarcastic if the task is in fact difficult.

～だけで · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 見(み)てだけで分(わ)かります。 → ✅ 見(み)るだけで分(わ)かります。
    だけ attaches to the plain form or the past form, never to the て-form. Both 見(み)るだけで and 見(み)ただけで are fine; the past one presents the looking as already done.

  • ❌ 静(しず)かだだけで、不便(ふべん)です。 → ✅ 静(しず)かなだけで、不便(ふべん)です。
    な-adjectives take な before だけ, exactly as they do before a noun. だ only appears at the end of a sentence.

  • ❌ これだけで足(た)りません。 → ✅ これだけでは足(た)りません。
    When the point is that the amount is insufficient, the contrastive は is required. Without it the sentence sounds like it was cut off halfway.

～だけで · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ サラダだけで食(た)べました。（meaning "I only ate salad"） → ✅ サラダだけ食(た)べました。
    The で adds "by means of", which the sentence does not want. Reserve だけで for when a result follows.

～だけで · Practice 1

1. このアプリは写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)る____、花(はな)の名前(なまえ)を教(おし)えてくれます。

このアプリは写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)るだけで、花(はな)の名前(なまえ)を教(おし)えてくれます。

Taking a photo is the whole of the effort required, and the app naming the flower is the result. That "minimum input, full result" shape is exactly what だけで exists for, which is why product descriptions are full of it.

～だけで · Practice 2

2. Choose the natural option: 辞書(じしょ)を買(か)う（だけで・だけでは）上手(じょうず)になりません。

The predicate is negative - you will not improve - so the sentence is saying the one action is insufficient, and that requires the contrastive は. With plain だけで the sentence would be claiming the opposite and then contradicting itself.

～だけで · Practice 3

～だけで · Practice 4

親切(しんせつ) is a な-adjective, so it takes な in front of だけ. Notice what the sentence does with the pattern: it grants one merit and then withdraws everything else - the dismissive "that's all he is" flavour of だけで.

～だけで · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 話(はなし)を聞(き)くだけでした。 (b) 話(はなし)を聞(き)くだけで涙(なみだ)が出(で)ました。

(a) is a closed report: listening was the whole of what you did, and nothing is said about any consequence. It often carries a hint of "I didn't get involved".

(b) makes the listening a trigger. The point is not the amount of listening but how little of it was needed before you cried. The で is doing all of that work - it turns a boundary into a cause.

～しか～ない

Meaning · only ～; nothing but ～ - and it is less than you wanted

しか is a limiter that comes with a mood attached. It excludes everything outside its noun, exactly as だけ does, but it also tells you the speaker finds the remainder thin. The grammar is the strange part: the verb at the end must be negative, even though the sentence is reporting something that does exist. 千円(せんえん)しかありません means "I have a thousand yen" - said by somebody who wishes they had more.

～しか～ない · Formation

しか goes on the noun; the negative goes on the predicate. Anything at all can sit between them.

Attaches to Form Example
Noun Noun + しか + negative 水(みず)しかありません
Number / counter Counter + しか + negative 二(ふた)つしか残(のこ)っていない
After が／を／は those particles are dropped ❌お金(かね)をしか → ✅お金(かね)しか
After に／で／と／から particle + しか 駅(えき)でしか買(か)えない
Polite ～しか + ません／ないです 一人(ひとり)しか来(き)ませんでした
With material in between ～しか … + negative 少(すこ)ししか食(た)べませんでした

～しか～ない · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - しか and the negative are one unit with a gap in the middle. If you write しか you have committed yourself to a negative ending, however many words later it arrives.

Note - the sentence is negative in form only. To answer 「お金(かね)、ありますか」 with 「百円(ひゃくえん)しかありません」 is to say that you do have a hundred yen. English speakers hear the negative and conclude the opposite.

～しか～ない · Examples

冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)に卵(たまご)が一(ひと)つしか残(のこ)っていません。
There's only one egg left in the fridge.

今日(きょう)は水(みず)しか飲(の)んでいません。
All I've had to drink today is water.

その話(はなし)は田中(たなか)さんにしか話(はな)していません。
Tanaka is the only person I've told about it.

この雑誌(ざっし)は大(おお)きい書店(しょてん)でしか売(う)っていません。
This magazine is only sold in the big bookshops.

会場(かいじょう)には十人(じゅうにん)しか来(き)ませんでした。
Only ten people turned up at the venue.

～しか～ない · Examples (cont.)

残(のこ)り時間(じかん)が五分(ごふん)しか****ありません。
There are only five minutes left.

去年(きょねん)買(か)ったコートは高(たか)かったのに、まだ二回(にかい)しか着(き)ていません。
The coat I bought last year was expensive, and I've still only worn it twice.

仕事(しごと)が終(お)わらなくて、昨日(きのう)は三時間(さんじかん)しか寝(ね)られませんでした。
I couldn't finish work, so I only got three hours' sleep last night.

～しか～ない · Nuance & register

Register-neutral: 一人(ひとり)しかいない and 一人(ひとり)しかいません are the same sentence at different politeness levels, and nothing about しか marks age or gender. It is extremely common in ordinary speech.

The feeling it carries is shortfall. That can be regret （三時間(さんじかん)しか寝(ね)ていない）, an apology （少(すこ)ししかご用意(ようい)できませんでした, said while serving a generous meal - a standard piece of Japanese modesty）, or a warning （時間(じかん)が少(すこ)ししかありません）. What it cannot be is neutral. If you have no feelings about the quantity, use だけ.

You will hear だけしか doubled up - これだけしかない. Strictly it says the same thing twice, but it is standard colloquial Japanese and intensifies the disappointment.

～しか～ない · Nuance & register (cont.)

One more thing worth flagging early: V(plain) + しかない, as in 行(い)くしかない, is a different pattern meaning "there's no choice but to go". It looks identical and it is not the same construction; the contrast section separates them.

～しか～ない · ～しか～ない vs ～だけ

Rule of thumb - same fact, opposite mood. だけ states a limit flatly; しか～ない states it with regret. And only しか forces the verb negative.

土曜日(どようび)だけ営業(えいぎょう)しています。
We're open on Saturdays.

土曜日(どようび)しか営業(えいぎょう)していません。
We're only open on Saturdays, I'm afraid.

A shop advertising its hours writes the first. A shop apologising for them says the second. Advertisers do sometimes use しか on purpose - 当店(とうてん)でしか買(か)えません, "you can't get it anywhere else" - but notice what they are doing: they are turning the shopper's inability to buy it elsewhere into the shortfall, not the shop's own range.

～しか～ない · ～しか～ない vs ～だけ (cont.)

The mechanical difference matters too. だけ leaves the verb alone and can take が/を on either side; しか swallows が, を and は entirely and rewrites the ending.

～しか～ない · ～しか～ない vs a plain negative

Rule of thumb - a plain negative denies. しか～ない denies everything except the thing it marks - so it quietly asserts that thing.

一人(ひとり)来(き)ませんでした。
One person didn't come.

一人(ひとり)しか来(き)ませんでした。
Only one person came.

The first sentence has one absentee and an unknown number of attendees. The second has one attendee and everybody else absent. The single word しか reverses which side of the count the number is on, and this is the mistake that costs learners marks in reading comprehension.

～しか～ない · Noun + しかない vs verb + しかない

Rule of thumb - after a noun, しかない means "there is only ～". After a plain-form verb, it means "there is nothing to do but ～".

バスは一日(いちにち)に三本(さんぼん)しかありません。
There are only three buses a day.

バスがないので、歩(ある)くしかありません。
There's no bus, so we'll just have to walk.

The second is a decision made under pressure, not a count of anything, and it is formally an N3 pattern. Keeping them apart is a matter of looking left: a noun or a counter means quantity, a dictionary-form verb means resignation.

～しか～ない · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 千円(せんえん)しかあります。 → ✅ 千円(せんえん)しかありません。
    しか cannot live with a positive predicate. The negative ending is part of the pattern, not a separate decision.

  • ❌ お金(かね)をしか持(も)っていません。 → ✅ お金(かね)しか持(も)っていません。
    を is dropped in front of しか, as are が and は. Only に, で, と and から survive, and they come first: 駅(えき)でしか.

  • ❌ 私(わたし)はしか知(し)りません。 → ✅ 私(わたし)しか知(し)りません。
    Same rule, and this one is easy to miss because は feels like it belongs to the topic rather than to the noun. しか takes the slot.

  • ❌ 二人(ふたり)しか来(こ)なくて、うれしかったです。 → ✅ 二人(ふたり)だけ来(き)て、うれしかったです。
    しか says the number disappointed you, so it fights with うれしい. When the small number is good news, だけ is the word.

～しか～ない · Practice 1

1. この町(まち)には病院(びょういん)が一(ひと)つ____あり____。

この町(まち)には病院(びょういん)が一(ひと)つしかありません。

しか needs the negative ending to complete it. Note that 一(ひと)つ, the counter, is where しか attaches - the pattern is very often built on a number, because that is where shortfall is easiest to feel.

～しか～ない · Practice 2

2. Choose the natural option: 小(ちい)さいパーティーにしたかったので、友(とも)だちを五人(ごにん)（だけ・しか）呼(よ)び（ました・ませんでした）。

友(とも)だちを五人(ごにん)だけ呼(よ)びました。

The first half says the small guest list was deliberate and wanted, so there is no shortfall to complain about. しか would say five friends was disappointingly few, which contradicts the reason given - and it would also force the ending negative: 五人(ごにん)しか呼(よ)びませんでした.

～しか～ない · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "I've only been to Japan once."

しか sits on 一度(いちど) and the ending goes negative - ありません, not あります. The English "only once" already contains the regret, which is why しか fits better here than 一度(いちど)だけ行(い)ったことがあります, a flatter statement of fact.

～しか～ない · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)は三十分(さんじゅっぷん)をしか勉強(べんきょう)しました。

Two fixes. The を in front of しか is deleted - しか occupies that slot itself. And the ending has to go negative: しか is only half a pattern without it.

～しか～ない · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 土曜日(どようび)だけ休(やす)めます。 (b) 土曜日(どようび)しか休(やす)めません。

Both mean that Saturday is your one free day.

(a) reports it. It could be an answer to "which day are you free?", and it carries no complaint - you might even be pleased to have the day.

(b) complains about it. The negative ending and しか together say one day is not enough, and the sentence naturally continues with something like 疲(つか)れます. Choosing between them is choosing whether to sound content or hard done by.

あまり～ない

Meaning · not very; not much; not particularly

あまり sits in front of a negative and softens it. It converts a flat denial into a measured one: not "I don't like it" but "I'm not all that keen". Because Japanese conversation avoids blunt refusals, this is one of the most-used adverbs in the language - and one learners under-use, because English rarely needs it.

あまり～ない · Formation

あまり is an adverb, but it comes with a condition attached, which is why it is treated as a pattern: the predicate at the end of its clause must be negative. The two halves belong together the way しか～ない does.

あまり～ない · Formation (cont.)

Attaches to Form Example
い-adjective あまり + ～くない 高(たか)い → あまり高(たか)くない
な-adjective あまり + ～じゃない 静(しず)かだ → あまり静(しず)かじゃない
Noun predicate あまり + ～じゃない あまり上手(じょうず)じゃない
Verb あまり + ない-form 見(み)る → あまり見(み)ない
Polite あまり + ～ません／～ないです あまり飲(の)みません
Past あまり + ～なかった／～ませんでした あまり寒(さむ)くなかった
Casual あんまり／あんま + negative あんまりおいしくない

あまり～ない · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - あまり goes in front of whatever it is measuring, and the negative comes at the end of that same clause. Anything may sit between them: あまり日本(にほん)の映画(えいが)を見(み)ません.

Note - あまり with a positive predicate is a different, more literary construction meaning "excessively": あまりの寒(さむ)さに驚(おどろ)いた, "I was startled by how bitterly cold it was". At N4 the safe rule is the one above - あまり takes a negative.

あまり～ない · Examples

この料理(りょうり)はあまり辛(から)くありません。
This dish isn't very spicy.

私(わたし)はあまりテレビを見(み)ません。
I don't watch much television.

きのうの夜(よる)はあまり眠(ねむ)れませんでした。
I didn't sleep very well last night.

その映画(えいが)はあまりおもしろくなかったですね。
That film wasn't all that interesting, was it.

漢字(かんじ)はあんまり覚(おぼ)えられないんです。
I'm not very good at getting kanji to stick, to be honest.

あまり～ない · Examples (cont.)

駅(えき)から遠(とお)いので、この店(みせ)はあまり込(こ)みません。
It's a long way from the station, so this place doesn't get very crowded.

新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)は給料(きゅうりょう)はいいのですが、自分(じぶん)の時間(じかん)があまり取(と)れません。
The new job pays well, but I don't get much time to myself.

甘(あま)いものはあまり好(す)きではないので、飲(の)み物(もの)だけいただきます。
I'm not that fond of sweet things, so I'll just have a drink.

あまり～ない · Nuance & register

あまり itself is register-neutral. What varies is the contraction:

  • あまり - the standard form. Safe everywhere, including writing.
  • あんまり - very common in ordinary speech, slightly warmer and more
    emphatic. Fine in conversation, out of place in a report or an exam essay.
  • あんま - clipped and quite casual. Recognise it; think twice before using it.

The reason to reach for あまり is almost always politeness. Japanese treats a bare negative judgement as blunt, so the softened version is the default in company:

あまり～ない · Nuance & register (cont.)

  • 嫌(きら)いです - "I dislike it." Strong, and can sound aggressive about
    someone's cooking.
  • 好(す)きではありません - "I don't like it." Still flat.
  • あまり好(す)きではありません - "I'm not particularly keen." This is the one
    people actually say.

The same softening lets あまり function as a polite refusal. Asked whether you can come on Saturday, 土曜日(どようび)はあまり… - trailing off, with no negative at all - is understood as no. The listener supplies the rest. Learning to hear this saves a lot of confusion.

Do not confuse the degree with zero. あまり飲(の)みません means you drink a little; 飲(の)みません means you do not drink; ぜんぜん飲(の)みません means never, not a drop.

あまり～ない · あまり～ない vs ぜんぜん～ない

Rule of thumb - あまり means a small amount. ぜんぜん means none at all.

肉(にく)はあまり食(た)べません。
I don't eat much meat.

肉(にく)はぜんぜん食(た)べません。
I don't eat meat at all.

The first speaker will accept a slice of ham; the second will not. Because ぜんぜん is absolute, it is also more forceful, and using it about someone's work or cooking can sound harsh where あまり would be diplomatic. If you are unsure how strong to be, あまり is the safer word.

あまり～ない · あまり～ない vs a plain negative

Rule of thumb - the plain negative states a fact. あまり states the same fact politely, and leaves the door slightly open.

この辺(へん)のことは知(し)りません。
I don't know this area.

この辺(へん)のことはあまり知(し)りません。
I don't know this area very well.

The second is both more modest and more accurate - you know a little. In conversation the softened version also invites the other person to keep talking, where the flat one closes the topic. This is why Japanese speakers add あまり to statements that are, strictly speaking, absolute.

あまり～ない · あまり～ない vs そんなに～ない

Rule of thumb - あまり measures against a general expectation. そんなに measures against something the other person just said or did.

この店(みせ)はあまり高(たか)くないですよ。
This place isn't very expensive.

この店(みせ)はそんなに高(たか)くないですよ。
It isn't that expensive here - not as much as you're imagining.

そんなに points backwards at a standard someone else raised: they winced at the menu, or said they had heard the place was pricey. あまり needs no such antecedent and works as an opening remark. In many contexts either is fine, and そんなに simply sounds more conversational.

あまり～ない · Common mistakes

  • ❌ この問題(もんだい)はあまり簡単(かんたん)です。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)はあまり簡単(かんたん)じゃありません。
    あまり cannot sit with a positive predicate. Without the negative the sentence has no meaning to land on.

  • ❌ 昨日(きのう)はあまり暑(あつ)かったです。（meaning "it was very hot"） → ✅ 昨日(きのう)はとても暑(あつ)かったです。
    あまり is not a synonym for とても. It looks like an intensifier and behaves like the opposite of one.

  • ❌ この報告書(ほうこくしょ)の数字(すうじ)はあんまり正(ただ)しくありません。 → ✅ ～はあまり正(ただ)しくありません。
    Grammatically fine, but あんまり is spoken register and clashes with a written document.

あまり～ない · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 「納豆(なっとう)は好(す)きですか。」「ぜんぜん好(す)きじゃありません。」（meaning "not all that keen"） → ✅ 「あまり好(す)きじゃありません。」
    ぜんぜん is a total rejection and can sting. If you only mean "not especially", あまり is the word - and the polite one.

あまり～ない · Practice 1

1. 今朝(けさ)は忙(いそが)しくて、食(た)べ。

あまり and the negative ending are one unit: you cannot supply one without the other. The meaning is that you ate a little, not that you skipped breakfast - for that you would need ぜんぜん食(た)べませんでした.

あまり～ない · Practice 2

The reply ends in a negative, and only あまり can pair with one. とても needs a positive predicate - とても使(つか)いやすいです - so it has nothing to attach to here.

あまり～ない · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese, politely: "I'm not very good at singing."

上手(じょうず) is a な-adjective, so its negative is じゃありません, and あまり sits in front of the whole thing. This is the standard modest reply when someone praises your singing; 下手(へた)です would be true but unnecessarily harsh about yourself.

あまり～ない · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: この町(まち)は静(しず)かあまりじゃないです。

あまり is an adverb and has to come before the word it modifies, not after it. Word order is what tells the listener how much of the sentence あまり is measuring.

あまり～ない · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) その話(はなし)は知(し)りません。 (b) その話(はなし)はあまり知(し)りません。

(a) is a clean denial: the topic is new to you, and the sentence closes the subject.

(b) admits partial knowledge and hedges it. You have heard something, but not enough to be useful - and by declining to sound certain, you invite the other person to fill you in. In a workplace, (b) is also the safer answer, because it does not commit you to knowing nothing.

ぜんぜん

Meaning · (not) at all; not in the slightest

ぜんぜん is the zero end of the scale. Where あまり says "not much", ぜんぜん says "none, nothing, no part of it". It normally pairs with a negative, and in casual modern speech it has picked up a second, affirmative life that textbooks tend not to mention and learners hear on their first day in Japan.

ぜんぜん · Usage

ぜんぜん is an adverb. It sits in front of the predicate it is emptying out, and anything may stand between it and the end of the sentence. In kanji it is 全然(ぜんぜん); both spellings are ordinary.

There are three patterns to recognise:

ぜんぜん · Usage (cont.)

  • With a negative - the core use.
    全然(ぜんぜん)分(わ)かりません, 全然(ぜんぜん)寒(さむ)くなかった, 全然(ぜんぜん)好(す)きじゃない.
  • With a positive verb or adjective that already means "not" -
    全然(ぜんぜん)違(ちが)います ("completely wrong"), 全然(ぜんぜん)だめです ("no good at all"). These are positive in form and negative in sense, and they are fully standard.
  • With an ordinary positive, in casual speech - 全然(ぜんぜん)大丈夫(だいじょうぶ),
    全然(ぜんぜん)いいよ, meaning "totally fine, no problem at all". Extremely common in conversation; see the register note below before you write it down.

It also works as a one-word answer: 「疲(つか)れた?」「ぜんぜん。」 - "Tired?" "Not a bit."

ぜんぜん · Examples

その話(はなし)はぜんぜん知(し)りませんでした。
I had absolutely no idea about that.

この問題(もんだい)の意味(いみ)がぜんぜん分(わ)かりません。
I don't understand what this question means at all.

昨日(きのう)の説明(せつめい)とぜんぜん違(ちが)うじゃないですか。
That's completely different from what you told us yesterday.

「待(ま)たせてごめん。」「ううん、ぜんぜん大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)だよ。」
"Sorry to keep you waiting." "No, no, it's totally fine."

ぜんぜん · Nuance & register

In its negative use ぜんぜん is register-neutral and works in speech and writing alike. What it is not is soft. It is an absolute, and pointed at another person's work it can be blunt: 全然(ぜんぜん)おもしろくなかったです about a friend's film recommendation is a flat verdict. When you want to be diplomatic, あまり is the word.

The affirmative use - 全然(ぜんぜん)平気(へいき), 全然(ぜんぜん)いける - needs a clear-eyed description, because you will meet it constantly and be told it is wrong. It is casual spoken Japanese, especially common among younger speakers, and in conversation nobody blinks. Style guides and many older speakers treat it as incorrect, and it does not belong in an essay, a business email or a JLPT answer. Interestingly the affirmative pattern is not new: it appears in Meiji-era literature, and the "negative only" rule solidified later - so what is often called a modern corruption is closer to a revival. Use it in speech if you like; keep it off paper.

ぜんぜん · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two more points of feel:

  • The intensity ladder runs 全然(ぜんぜん) → あまり → ちょっと. Only the first
    is a true zero.
  • Because it is absolute, ぜんぜん cannot be qualified. There is no "almost not
    at all"; if the amount is small but real, you have moved to あまり.

ぜんぜん · Common mistakes

  • ❌ その映画(えいが)はぜんぜん楽(たの)しかったです。（meaning "not fun at all"） → ✅ その映画(えいが)はぜんぜん楽(たの)しくなかったです。
    With an ordinary adjective the predicate has to go negative. Leaving it positive produces the casual "totally fun", which is the opposite of what you meant.

  • ❌ ぜんぜん少(すこ)し分(わ)かります。 → ✅ 少(すこ)し分(わ)かります。／あまり分(わ)かりません。
    ぜんぜん is zero, so it cannot be attached to a small quantity. Choose one or the other.

  • ❌ この作品(さくひん)は全然(ぜんぜん)すばらしいと思(おも)います。（in an essay） → ✅ この作品(さくひん)は非常(ひじょう)にすばらしいと思(おも)います。
    The affirmative ぜんぜん is spoken register. In writing it reads as an error even to readers who use it themselves every day.

ぜんぜん · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 時間(じかん)が全然(ぜんぜん)ありましたから、ゆっくりできました。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)がたくさんありましたから、ゆっくりできました。
    全然(ぜんぜん)ある is not the affirmative pattern; the casual use attaches to evaluations like 大丈夫(だいじょうぶ) and いい, not to plain statements of quantity.

ぜんぜん · Practice 1

1. 三年(さんねん)習(なら)いましたが、まだ____話(はな)せ____。

三年(さんねん)習(なら)いましたが、まだぜんぜん話(はな)せません。

ぜんぜん demands the negative ending, and the contrast with 三年(さんねん)習(なら)いました is what makes it the right adverb here - three years of study and nothing to show for it. あまり話(はな)せません would mean you can manage a little, which is a different complaint.

ぜんぜん · Practice 2

2. Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)のテストはぜんぜん難(むずか)しいでした。

✅ 昨日(きのう)のテストはぜんぜん難(むずか)しくありませんでした。

Two things. い-adjectives never take でした - the past goes on the adjective itself. And ぜんぜん needs the negative, so the correct past negative is 難(むずか)しくありませんでした（or 難(むずか)しくなかったです）.

ぜんぜん · Practice 3

3. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) この料理(りょうり)はあまり辛(から)くないです。 (b) この料理(りょうり)はぜんぜん辛(から)くないです。

(a) says there is a little heat in it, but not much. Someone who cannot take spice at all should still be careful.

(b) says there is no heat whatsoever - it is safe for anyone. ぜんぜん is an absolute zero, あまり a small but real amount, and choosing the wrong one is a genuinely useful thing to get right at a dinner table.

そんなに

Meaning · that much; so much; to that extent

そんなに measures against something the other person has just supplied - their words, their reaction, the amount they poured, the price they winced at. It is not a free-standing "very". Point it at a standard nobody has raised and it sounds as though you are answering a question that was never asked.

そんなに · Usage

そんなに is an adverb built from そんな ("that kind of") plus に. It goes directly in front of the adjective, adverb or verb whose degree it is measuring.

Most of the time it appears with a negative, playing down an amount the other person has overestimated:

  • そんなに高(たか)くないですよ - "it isn't that expensive."
  • そんなに遠(とお)くありません - "it isn't that far."
  • そんなに時間(じかん)はかかりません - "it won't take that long."

With a positive predicate it usually carries surprise or a mild protest: そんなに食(た)べたの? ("you ate that much?"), そんなに急(いそ)がないで ("don't rush like that").

そんなに · Usage (cont.)

It belongs to a set of four. こんなに measures against something in the speaker's own hands, そんなに against the listener's, あんなに against something remembered or far from both, and どんなに asks "how much?".

The crucial spelling point: そんな goes in front of a noun （そんな人(ひと), そんなこと）and そんなに in front of an adjective or verb （そんなに大(おお)きい, そんなに笑(わら)う）.

そんなに · Examples

そんなに急(いそ)がなくてもいいですよ。
There's no need to rush like that.

この辞書(じしょ)はそんなに重(おも)くありません。
This dictionary isn't as heavy as all that.

そんなに大(おお)きい声(こえ)を出(だ)さないでください。赤(あか)ちゃんが起(お)きてしまいます。
Please don't talk that loudly - you'll wake the baby.

「三時間(さんじかん)も並(なら)んだんだ。」「えっ、そんなに待(ま)ったの?」
"I queued for three whole hours." "What - you waited that long?"

そんなに · Nuance & register

Register-neutral and very frequent in conversation. In formal writing the same job is done by それほど, which is the stiffer equivalent: それほど難(むずか)しくはありません in a manual, そんなに難(むずか)しくないよ to a friend.

The reason it turns up so often is that it is a polite way to disagree. Rather than contradicting someone flatly, you scale their estimate down: 「大変(たいへん)でしょう」「いえ、そんなに大変(たいへん)じゃありません」. The trailing 「そんなに…」 on its own - with the rest left unsaid - is a very soft way of saying "not really".

Two feelings to keep apart. With a negative, そんなに is reassuring: it tells the listener to relax. With a positive it is often the opposite - surprise （そんなにかかったの?）or restraint （そんなに怒(おこ)らないで, "don't be so angry"）, and in that second use it can sound like a mild telling-off.

そんなに · Nuance & register (cont.)

Compared with あまり, そんなに is more conversational and more tied to context. あまり measures against the world in general and can open a conversation; そんなに needs something to point back at.

そんなに · Common mistakes

  • ❌ そんなに人(ひと)は信用(しんよう)できません。 → ✅ そんな人(ひと)は信用(しんよう)できません。
    Before a noun the form is そんな. そんなに is the adverb and needs an adjective or a verb to modify.

  • ❌ そんな高(たか)くないですよ。 → ✅ そんなに高(たか)くないですよ。
    The mirror image of the mistake above. You will hear the reduced そんな高(たか)い in fast casual speech, but そんなに is the standard form and the only safe one in writing or on a test.

  • ❌ （opening a conversation with no context）そんなに忙(いそが)しくないです。 → ✅ あまり忙(いそが)しくないです。
    そんなに answers an estimate someone else has made. With nothing to point back at, it leaves the listener hunting for what "that much" refers to.

そんなに · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ （holding up your own enormous suitcase）そんなに重(おも)いんです。 → ✅ こんなに重(おも)いんです。
    The thing is in your hands, so it belongs to the speaker's territory: こんなに. そんなに would put it on the listener's side of the conversation.

そんなに · Practice 1

1. 「駅(えき)まで歩(ある)くと一時間(いちじかん)ぐらいかかりますか。」「いいえ、____かかりません。十五分(じゅうごふん)ぐらいです。」

「いいえ、そんなにかかりません。十五分(じゅうごふん)ぐらいです。」

The other person has just put an hour on the table, and そんなに scales that specific estimate down without calling them wrong. This is the pattern's home ground: a polite correction that points back at what was just said.

そんなに · Practice 2

2. Find and fix the error: そんなに話(はなし)は信(しん)じられません。

✅ そんな話(はなし)は信(しん)じられません。 "I can't believe a story like that."

話(はなし) is a noun, so it takes そんな. If you wanted the adverb you would need something for it to modify: そんなに簡単(かんたん)には信(しん)じられません, "I can't believe it that easily."

そんなに · Practice 3

3. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) こんなに降(ふ)るとは思(おも)いませんでした。 (b) あんなに降(ふ)るとは思(おも)いませんでした。

(a) is said while the rain is coming down in front of you - the amount is right here, in the speaker's own space, so こんなに is the form.

(b) is said afterwards, about a downpour that is over or elsewhere. あんなに points at something distant from both speakers, in space or in memory, which is why it so often shows up in stories about the past.
