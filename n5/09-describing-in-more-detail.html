の - of; possessive

Meaning · links two nouns, with the first one describing or owning the second

の is the glue between nouns. 私(わたし)の本(ほん) is "my book", 日本語(にほんご)の本(ほん) is "a Japanese-language book", 木(き)の机(つくえ) is "a wooden desk" - ownership is only the most obvious of a much wider set of relationships.

の - of; possessive · Formation

The rule is one-directional and never varies: the modifier comes first, the head noun comes last. Whatever の points at, the thing being described is always on the right.

の - of; possessive · Formation (cont.)

Attaches to Form Example
Noun + Noun N1 の N2 私(わたし) + かばん → 私(わたし)のかばん
Chained N1 の N2 の N3 友(とも)だちの車(くるま)のかぎ
Apposition ("N1, who is N2") N1 の N2 友(とも)だちのミラーさん
Material or origin N1 の N2 木(き)の机(つくえ)／イタリアのくつ
Standing in for the noun N1 の (nothing after) この傘(かさ)は姉(あね)のです
After another particle N + particle + の + N 友人(ゆうじん)からの手紙(てがみ)
❌ な-adjective takes な, not の ❌きれいの花(はな) → ✅きれいな花(はな)
❌ い-adjective attaches bare ❌大(おお)きいの家(いえ) → ✅大(おお)きい家(いえ)

の - of; possessive · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - の joins a noun to a noun. If the word in front of it is an adjective, の is wrong: い-adjectives attach directly, な-adjectives take な.

の - of; possessive · Examples

これは私(わたし)の自転車(じてんしゃ)です。
This is my bicycle.

田中(たなか)さんの会社(かいしゃ)は東京(とうきょう)にあります。
Mr Tanaka's company is in Tokyo.

この傘(かさ)は姉(あね)のです。
This umbrella is my sister's.

イタリア語(ご)の本(ほん)を三(さん)冊(さつ)借(か)りました。
I borrowed three Italian-language books.

友(とも)だちのミラーさんが来週(らいしゅう)遊(あそ)びに来(き)ます。
My friend Miller is coming to visit next week.

の - of; possessive · Examples (cont.)

会社(かいしゃ)の近(ちか)くにおいしいラーメン屋(や)があります。
There's a good ramen place near the office.

これは母(はは)が焼(や)いたパンで、あちらは店(みせ)のです。
This is bread my mother baked; that one over there is from the shop.

大学(だいがく)時代(じだい)の友人(ゆうじん)からの手紙(てがみ)が、久(ひさ)しぶりに届(とど)きました。
A letter arrived from a university friend for the first time in ages.

の - of; possessive · Nuance & register

の is completely register-neutral - the single most common word in Japanese text, and invisible in the way that English "of" and "'s" are invisible.

Three things worth knowing beyond the basic rule:

の is vague on purpose. 山田(やまだ)さんの本(ほん) can mean the book Yamada owns, the book Yamada wrote, the book Yamada is holding, or the book about Yamada. Japanese does not distinguish these, and context does all the work. Do not go looking for a more precise particle; there isn't one.

の can stand in for a noun already understood. これは私(わたし)のです - "this is mine." The noun has been deleted, not the の. This is why 赤(あか)いの ("the red one") looks similar but is a different の: there, の is standing in for a whole noun after an adjective rather than marking a possessor.

の - of; possessive · Nuance & register (cont.)

Chains get heavy fast. 会社(かいしゃ)の近(ちか)くの駅(えき)の前(まえ)の店(みせ) is grammatical and no Japanese speaker would say it. Two の in a row is ordinary; three starts to strain; four means you should rebuild the sentence.

Contracted ん in speech. In fast casual speech の sometimes reduces: 俺(おれ)んち ("my place", from 俺(おれ)の家(うち)), 君(きみ)んとこ. These are fixed, informal, and mostly limited to places and homes; do not generalise the pattern.

の - of; possessive · の vs な

Rule of thumb - の after a noun, な after a な-adjective. The words look alike in the dictionary, which is the whole problem.

Many な-adjectives - きれい, 有名(ゆうめい), 元気(げんき), 便利(べんり) - feel like nouns to a learner, and some of them genuinely can be nouns in other contexts. When they are describing the following noun, they take な.

有名(ゆうめい)な店(みせ)に行(い)きました。
I went to a famous shop.

東京(とうきょう)の店(みせ)に行(い)きました。
I went to a shop in Tokyo.

の - of; possessive · の vs な (cont.)

東京(とうきょう) is a noun, so it takes の; 有名(ゆうめい) is a な-adjective, so it takes な. ❌有名(ゆうめい)の店(みせ) and ❌東京(とうきょう)な店(みせ) are both wrong, and the second one is instantly noticeable to a native ear.

The awkward middle case is 病気(びょうき) and 元気(げんき), which go both ways depending on what you mean: 元気(げんき)な子(こ) = "a lively child"; 病気(びょうき)の子(こ) = "a child who is ill" (the noun "illness" describing them).

の - of; possessive · の (linking nouns) vs の (the nominaliser)

Rule of thumb - if the word in front of の is a noun, it is this の. If it is a verb or a clause, it is the nominaliser - a different job entirely.

私(わたし)の趣味(しゅみ)は写真(しゃしん)です。
My hobby is photography. (noun + の - possession)

写真(しゃしん)をとるのが好(す)きです。
I like taking photographs. (verb + の - the verb has been made into a noun)

The two never compete for the same slot, so once you can see whether the word in front is a noun or a verb, the ambiguity disappears. Both are extremely common and you will use them in the same conversation.

の - of; possessive · AのB vs BのA

Rule of thumb - the head noun goes last, always. English puts it first ("a map of Tokyo") and second ("Tokyo*'s* map"), which is exactly why learners get the order backwards.

東京(とうきょう)の地図(ちず)を買(か)いました。
I bought a map of Tokyo.

地図(ちず)の東京(とうきょう)
the Tokyo that belongs to the map - not a thing anyone would say

The test that always works: what am I actually talking about? In "a map of Tokyo" you are talking about a map, so 地図(ちず) goes at the end. In "the teacher's car" you are talking about a car, so 車(くるま) goes at the end: 先生(せんせい)の車(くるま).

の - of; possessive · Common mistakes

  • ❌ きれいの花(はな)をもらいました。 → ✅ きれいな花(はな)をもらいました。
    きれい is a な-adjective despite ending in い. It takes な, never の.

  • ❌ 大(おお)きいの家(いえ)に住(す)んでいます。 → ✅ 大(おお)きい家(いえ)に住(す)んでいます。
    い-adjectives attach straight to the noun. Nothing goes in between.

  • ❌ 車(くるま)の私(わたし)です。 → ✅ 私(わたし)の車(くるま)です。
    The owner comes first and the thing owned comes last. Reversing them reverses the meaning, or produces nonsense.

  • ❌ 日本語(にほんご)先生(せんせい)に聞(き)きました。 → ✅ 日本語(にほんご)の先生(せんせい)に聞(き)きました。
    Two nouns in a row need の to link them. (Some pairs have fused into single compound words over time, but you cannot invent new ones.)

  • ❌ これは私(わたし)のかばんのです。 → ✅ これは私(わたし)のかばんです。
    の stands in for a missing noun. When the noun is right there, a second の has nothing to do.

の - of; possessive · Practice 1

1. これは田中(たなか)さん____傘(かさ)ですか。

これは田中(たなか)さんの傘(かさ)ですか。

Two nouns side by side always need の between them. Note that the answer could also drop the second noun entirely - これは田中(たなか)さんのですか - because の can stand in for a noun both speakers already have in mind.

の - of; possessive · Practice 2

2. 静(しず)か（の・な）部屋(へや)をお願(ねが)いします。

静(しず)かな部屋(へや)をお願(ねが)いします。

静(しず)か is a な-adjective, so it links to the following noun with な. の is for nouns only, and the difference is audible - ❌静(しず)かの部屋(へや) marks a speaker as a beginner faster than almost any other error.

の - of; possessive · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "That's my mother's friend's dog."

あれは母(はは)の友(とも)だちの犬(いぬ)です。

Chained の runs in the same order as the English possessives - mother, then friend, then dog - with the thing you are actually talking about at the very end. Two の in a row like this is perfectly ordinary.

の - of; possessive · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 有名(ゆうめい)の人(ひと)に会(あ)いました。

✅ 有名(ゆうめい)な人(ひと)に会(あ)いました。

有名(ゆうめい) is a な-adjective. The の here comes from thinking of it as a noun meaning "fame", which it is not in this sentence - it is describing the person, so it needs な.

の - of; possessive · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 日本(にほん)の車(くるま)を買(か)いました。 (b) 車(くるま)の日本(にほん)を買(か)いました。

(a) is a normal sentence: 日本(にほん) describes 車(くるま), so this is "I bought a Japanese car." The head noun - the thing that was bought - sits at the end.

(b) reverses the roles, making 車(くるま) the description and 日本(にほん) the thing bought: "I bought the Japan that belongs to a car." It is not ambiguous or poetic, just broken. The lesson is that in Japanese the order carries all the meaning; there is no second particle to fall back on.

の - nominaliser

Meaning · turns a verb or a whole clause into a noun, so it can be a subject or an object

Japanese has no infinitive and no "-ing" noun. To say "swimming is fun" or "I forgot to lock the door", you take the clause you want and stick の on the end of it. What comes out behaves like an ordinary noun and can take が, を or は.

の - nominaliser · Formation

の attaches to the plain form of whatever is in front of it. Nouns and な-adjectives need な first - the same shape as ので and のに.

の - nominaliser · Formation (cont.)

Attaches to Form Example
Verb (plain) dictionary form + の 泳(およ)ぐ → 泳(およ)ぐの
Verb (past) た-form + の 会(あ)った → 会(あ)ったの
Verb (negative) ない-form + の 行(い)かない → 行(い)かないの
い-adjective plain form + の 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いの
な-adjective ～な + の 静(しず)かだ → 静(しず)かなの
Noun Noun + な + の 学生(がくせい)だ → 学生(がくせい)なの
A whole clause clause + の 彼(かれ)が来(こ)なかったの
Before です／だ の → ん 行(い)くのです → 行(い)くんです

の - nominaliser · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - never ❌だの. A noun or な-adjective takes な before the nominalising の, exactly as it does before ので.

Once nominalised, the phrase takes a case particle like any noun: 泳(およ)ぐのが好(す)きです、閉(し)めるのを忘(わす)れた、買(か)うのはやめた。

の - nominaliser · Examples

泳(およ)ぐのが好(す)きです。
I like swimming.

漢字(かんじ)を覚(おぼ)えるのは難(むずか)しい。
Memorising kanji is hard.

電気(でんき)を消(け)すのを忘(わす)れました。
I forgot to turn off the light.

子(こ)どもが公園(こうえん)で遊(あそ)んでいるのを見(み)ました。
I saw children playing in the park.

朝(あさ)早(はや)く起(お)きるのは苦手(にがて)です。
I'm no good at getting up early.

の - nominaliser · Examples (cont.)

隣(となり)の部屋(へや)で誰(だれ)かが歌(うた)っているのが聞(き)こえた。
I could hear someone singing in the next room.

彼女(かのじょ)が会社(かいしゃ)をやめたのを知(し)っていますか。
Did you know she'd quit the company?

雨(あめ)の日(ひ)に自転車(じてんしゃ)で通勤(つうきん)するのは、思(おも)っていたより大変(たいへん)でした。
Commuting by bike on rainy days turned out to be harder than I'd expected.

の - nominaliser · Nuance & register

The nominaliser の is register-neutral, but it leans spoken and concrete. Its rival こと leans written and abstract, and the two are treated in the Contrast section below.

The thing worth real attention here is the ん contraction, because it is everywhere and it changes the feel of a sentence completely.

の becomes ん before です and だ. 行(い)くのです → 行(い)くんです. The のです form is the written or careful-speech version; んです is what you actually hear.

んです is not decoration. It frames the sentence as an explanation - the speaker is accounting for something, or asking the listener to account for something.

の - nominaliser · Nuance & register (cont.)

  • 頭(あたま)が痛(いた)いです。 - "I have a headache." A fact, offered flat.
  • 頭(あたま)が痛(いた)いんです。 - "The thing is, I have a headache." Now it
    explains why you are leaving early.
  • どうしたんですか。 - "What's the matter?" Asking for an explanation. The bare
    ❌どうしましたか is possible but noticeably colder.

A learner who never uses んです sounds like they are reading a list; one who uses it in every sentence sounds like they are constantly justifying themselves. It belongs where there is a reason in the air.

The plain-form version is んだ: 明日(あした)、試験(しけん)なんだ。 Note the な survives the contraction - noun + な + んだ.

の - nominaliser · Nuance & register (cont.)

Finally, a sentence-final の with rising intonation is a soft question, and in Tokyo speech it is marked as feminine or as child-directed: どうしたの? 食(た)べないの? Men use it too, but usually with a following か or in a gentler register.

の - nominaliser · の vs こと

Rule of thumb - の for things you directly see, hear and experience; こと for abstractions, facts and reported information. Where either works, の is the spoken choice.

Three cases where the choice is not free:

Verbs of direct perception require の. 見(み)る、聞(き)く、感(かん)じる and friends are watching an event happen, not contemplating a fact.

猫(ねこ)が走(はし)っているのを見(み)ました。
I saw the cat running. (I watched it happen - の)

Fixed expressions require こと. ことができる, ことがある, ことにする, ことになる are set phrases and admit no substitution: ❌泳(およ)ぐのができる.

の - nominaliser · の vs こと (cont.)

X は Y です equations require こと. 趣味(しゅみ)は本(ほん)を読(よ)むことです。 With の this sounds like the hobby is the act happening in front of you.

Everywhere else - 泳(およ)ぐの／ことが好(す)きです - both are correct, and の is what you will hear.

の - nominaliser · の (nominaliser) vs の ("the one")

Rule of thumb - after a verb or clause, の makes an action into a noun. After an adjective, の usually stands in for a concrete thing: "the ~ one".

走(はし)るのが速(はや)いですね。
You're fast at running. (の = the act of running)

赤(あか)いのをください。
I'll take the red one. (の = the object itself)

In practice the difference is decided by what makes sense, and a clause can go either way: 昨日(きのう)買(か)ったの can mean "the one I bought yesterday" or "the fact that I bought it yesterday". Context settles it, and native speakers do not experience this as ambiguous.

の - nominaliser · ～のです／んです vs the plain statement

Rule of thumb - んです says "here is the explanation". A plain です sentence just states the fact.

明日(あした)、東京(とうきょう)へ行(い)きます。
I'm going to Tokyo tomorrow.

明日(あした)、東京(とうきょう)へ行(い)くんです。
I'm going to Tokyo tomorrow, you see. (…which is why I can't come / why I'm packing)

Get this wrong in either direction and you sound odd. Answering 何(なに)をしていますか with a bare fact is fine; answering どうして来(こ)なかったんですか without んです in your own reply sounds evasive, because the question explicitly asked for a reason.

の - nominaliser · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 日本語(にほんご)を話(はな)すのができます。 → ✅ 日本語(にほんご)を話(はな)すことができます。
    ことができる is a fixed expression. の cannot be substituted into it, however natural the rest of the sentence sounds.

  • ❌ 静(しず)かのが好(す)きです。 → ✅ 静(しず)かなのが好(す)きです。
    な-adjectives take な before the nominaliser, exactly as they do before ので and のに.

  • ❌ 猫(ねこ)が鳴(な)いていることを聞(き)きました。 → ✅ 猫(ねこ)が鳴(な)いているのを聞(き)きました。
    With こと this becomes "I heard that the cat was crying" - secondhand news. Direct perception needs の.

  • ❌ 学生(がくせい)のんです。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)なんです。
    A noun keeps its な when の contracts to ん. ❌学生(がくせい)のんです and ❌学生(がくせい)だんです are both wrong.

の - nominaliser · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 窓(まど)を閉(し)めるの忘(わす)れた。 → ✅ 窓(まど)を閉(し)めるのを忘(わす)れた。
    Once you have made a noun, it needs a particle like any other noun. (を does drop in fast speech, but learn the full form first.)

の - nominaliser · Practice 1

1. 毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)る____は気持(きも)ちがいいです。

毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)るのは気持(きも)ちがいいです。

走(はし)る is a verb, and the sentence needs a subject for 気持(きも)ちがいい - so the verb has to become a noun first. こと would also be grammatical here; の is the more spoken choice.

の - nominaliser · Practice 2

2. 私(わたし)はギターを弾(ひ)く（の・こと）ができます。

私(わたし)はギターを弾(ひ)くことができます。

ことができる is a set phrase for ability, and its こと is frozen in place. This is one of the small number of contexts where の and こと are not interchangeable at all.

の - nominaliser · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "I saw him get on the train."

彼(かれ)が電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)るのを見(み)ました。

Direct perception - you watched it with your own eyes - so this must be の, not こと. Note that the subject inside the nominalised clause takes が, never は.

の - nominaliser · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 来週(らいしゅう)、試験(しけん)だのです。

✅ 来週(らいしゅう)、試験(しけん)なのです。 (spoken: 試験(しけん)なんです)

A noun takes な before the nominaliser, never だ. The same な is what survives into the contracted んです, which is why you hear 試験(しけん)なんです and never ❌試験(しけん)だんです.

の - nominaliser · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 田中(たなか)さんは来(き)ません。 (b) 田中(たなか)さんは来(こ)ないんです。

(a) simply reports that Tanaka is not coming. It is the right sentence if someone asked you for the guest list.

(b) presents the same fact as the explanation for something - perhaps why there is a spare chair, or why the meeting is being moved. んです always points at a surrounding situation the fact is accounting for, and using it with no such situation in view makes the listener hunt for one.

Noun-modifying clauses

Meaning · put a whole clause in front of a noun to describe it - no relative pronoun, no word order change

English says "the book that I bought yesterday", moving the noun to the front and inserting a relative pronoun. Japanese says 昨日(きのう)買(か)った本(ほん) - clause first, noun last, nothing inserted. This one structure is the key to every sentence above N5.

Noun-modifying clauses · Formation

The clause goes in front of the noun, in the plain form. That is the whole rule.

Attaches to Form Example
Verb plain form + Noun 買(か)った本(ほん)／買(か)う本(ほん)
Verb (negative) ない-form + Noun 読(よ)まない人(ひと)
Verb (continuous) ～ている + Noun 走(はし)っている人(ひと)
い-adjective plain form + Noun 高(たか)い店(みせ)／高(たか)かった店(みせ)
な-adjective ～な + Noun 有名(ゆうめい)な店(みせ)
Noun (describing) Noun + の + Noun 木(き)の机(つくえ)
Noun (as a predicate inside the clause) Noun + だった + Noun 先生(せんせい)だった人(ひと)

Noun-modifying clauses · Formation (cont.)

Attaches to Form Example
Subject inside the clause が - or の 父(ちち)が作(つく)った／父(ちち)の作(つく)った
❌ Polite form inside never ❌買(か)いました本(ほん)
❌ Topic は inside never ❌私(わたし)は買(か)った本(ほん)

Rule of thumb - three things Japanese does not do: it does not use a relative pronoun, it does not move the noun, and it does not use polite forms inside the clause. Everything you would express with "who / which / that" is expressed by position alone.

Noun-modifying clauses · Formation (cont.)

Note - the clause can be any length at all, and it keeps its own particles inside. 去年(きょねん)京都(きょうと)で買(か)った小(ちい)さい茶(ちゃ)わん - every word before 茶(ちゃ)わん is describing it. When reading, look for the noun at the end first, then work backwards.

Noun-modifying clauses · Examples

これは母(はは)が作(つく)ったケーキです。
This is a cake my mother made.

眼鏡(めがね)をかけている人(ひと)が中村(なかむら)さんです。
The person wearing glasses is Nakamura.

昨日(きのう)見(み)た映画(えいが)はとてもおもしろかったです。
The film I saw yesterday was really good.

去年(きょねん)泊(と)まったホテルは駅(えき)から遠(とお)かった。
The hotel we stayed at last year was a long way from the station.

父(ちち)の撮(と)った写真(しゃしん)が新聞(しんぶん)に載(の)りました。
A photograph my father took appeared in the newspaper.

Noun-modifying clauses · Examples (cont.)

甘(あま)い物(もの)が好(す)きな人(ひと)にこの店(みせ)をおすすめします。
I'd recommend this shop to anyone who likes sweet things.

家族(かぞく)みんなで旅行(りょこう)に行(い)った思(おも)い出(で)を今(いま)でもよく覚(おぼ)えています。
I still remember clearly the trip we all took as a family.

駅前(えきまえ)に新(あたら)しくできた図書館(としょかん)は、夜(よる)九時(くじ)まで開(あ)いています。
The library that's just opened by the station stays open until nine at night.

Noun-modifying clauses · Nuance & register

The construction itself carries no register - it is simply how Japanese describes things, and it appears in every sentence type at every politeness level. What is register-marked is the fact that the clause is always plain: 買(か)った本(ほん)をお送(おく)りします is entirely polite, because politeness lives on the final verb of the sentence and nowhere else.

Three habits that separate fluent-sounding Japanese from translated English:

Long modifiers are normal. English resists front-loading and reaches for "which…" clauses instead. Japanese does not: a modifier of fifteen syllables sitting in front of a two-syllable noun is unremarkable in a newspaper. Learn to read to the end of the phrase before deciding what it means.

Noun-modifying clauses · Nuance & register (cont.)

が inside the clause often becomes の. 父(ちち)が撮(と)った写真(しゃしん) and 父(ちち)の撮(と)った写真(しゃしん) mean the same thing. The の version sounds slightly more compact and slightly more written; it is very common in headlines and titles. This substitution works only inside a modifying clause - never in a main sentence.

Ambiguity is tolerated. 私(わたし)が好(す)きな人(ひと) is "the person I like" in most contexts, but it can also be read as "the person who likes me". Japanese does not disambiguate this, and speakers rely on context rather than rebuilding the sentence.

Noun-modifying clauses · が vs の inside the clause

Rule of thumb - inside a noun-modifying clause, the subject may be marked が or の, with no change of meaning. Outside such a clause, only が works.

友(とも)だちが貸(か)してくれた本(ほん)を読(よ)んでいます。
I'm reading a book a friend lent me.

友(とも)だちの貸(か)してくれた本(ほん)を読(よ)んでいます。
I'm reading a book a friend lent me. (same meaning, slightly tighter)

Two guard rails. The の substitution fails as soon as the clause contains another の, because the string becomes unreadable. And it never applies to a main clause: ❌友(とも)だちの来(き)ました is simply wrong, where 友(とも)だちが来(き)ました is right.

Noun-modifying clauses · clause + Noun vs clause + の

Rule of thumb - a clause in front of a noun describes that noun. A clause in front of の turns the clause itself into a noun.

The two look identical until you notice what is at the end.

毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)っている人(ひと)を見(み)ました。
I saw a person who runs every morning.

毎朝(まいあさ)走(はし)っているのを見(み)ました。
I saw them running every morning.

In the first, the thing seen is a person. In the second, the thing seen is the event of running. Learners often produce the second when they meant the first, because の feels like a safe filler - it is not; it changes what the sentence is about.

Noun-modifying clauses · modifying clause vs ～という + Noun

Rule of thumb - if the noun could fill a slot inside the clause, modify it directly. If the clause is the content of the noun, you need という.

田中(たなか)さんが書(か)いた手紙(てがみ)を読(よ)みました。
I read a letter Tanaka wrote. (the letter is what he wrote - direct modification)

田中(たなか)さんが来(こ)ないという話(はなし)を聞(き)きました。
I heard that Tanaka isn't coming. (the news is the clause - という)

The test: can you put the noun back into the clause? 田中(たなか)さんが手紙(てがみ)を書(か)いた works, so 手紙(てがみ) modifies directly. ❌田中(たなか)さんが話(はなし)を来(こ)ない does not work, so 話(はなし) needs という. Content nouns like 話(はなし)、ニュース、約束(やくそく) and 予定(よてい) behave this way; you will meet the rule properly at N4.

Noun-modifying clauses · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 昨日(きのう)買(か)いました本(ほん)はこれです。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)買(か)った本(ほん)はこれです。
    The clause is always plain. Politeness goes on the last verb of the sentence, which here is です.

  • ❌ 昨日(きのう)見(み)たの映画(えいが)はよかった。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)見(み)た映画(えいが)はよかった。
    Nothing goes between the clause and the noun. The の is imported from 私(わたし)の本(ほん), where it links two nouns - a verb needs no linker.

  • ❌ 人(ひと)が走(はし)っている、田中(たなか)さんです。 → ✅ 走(はし)っている人(ひと)が田中(たなか)さんです。
    The description goes before the noun. Japanese has no way to hang a clause off the back of a noun.

Noun-modifying clauses · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 私(わたし)は買(か)った本(ほん)をなくしました。 → ✅ 私(わたし)が買(か)った本(ほん)をなくしました。
    は marks the topic of a whole sentence and cannot appear inside a modifying clause. Inside, the subject takes が (or の).

  • ❌ 有名(ゆうめい)人(ひと)に会(あ)いました。 → ✅ 有名(ゆうめい)な人(ひと)に会(あ)いました。
    な-adjectives keep their な in front of the noun, just as they do outside a clause.

Noun-modifying clauses · Practice 1

1. ____人(ひと)は山田(やまだ)さんです。（あそこで新聞(しんぶん)を読(よ)んでいます）

あそこで新聞(しんぶん)を読(よ)んでいる人(ひと)は山田(やまだ)さんです。

Two changes: the clause moves in front of 人(ひと), and the polite 読(よ)んでいます becomes plain 読(よ)んでいる. Nothing is inserted between the clause and the noun.

Noun-modifying clauses · Practice 2

2. 母(はは)（が・は）作(つく)った料理(りょうり)はおいしいです。

母(はは)が作(つく)った料理(りょうり)はおいしいです。

は belongs to the main sentence - here it is already marking 料理(りょうり) as the topic. Inside a modifying clause the subject takes が, and in this position の would also be acceptable: 母(はは)の作(つく)った料理(りょうり).

Noun-modifying clauses · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "The city where I was born is small."

私(わたし)が生(う)まれた町(まち)は小(ちい)さいです。

English needs "where"; Japanese needs nothing - the relationship between 町(まち) and the clause is carried by position alone. Note again that the clause verb is plain (生(う)まれた) while the sentence ends politely.

Noun-modifying clauses · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: きのう会(あ)いましたの人(ひと)は先生(せんせい)でした。

✅ きのう会(あ)った人(ひと)は先生(せんせい)でした。

Two errors in one phrase: the clause must be plain (会(あ)った, not 会(あ)いました), and there is no の between a clause and the noun it describes. The の is only for linking one noun to another.

Noun-modifying clauses · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 昨日(きのう)買(か)った本(ほん)を読(よ)みました。 (b) 昨日(きのう)買(か)ったのを読(よ)みました。

(a) names the thing explicitly: a book, bought yesterday, was read. It works whether or not the listener already knows about any book.

(b) leaves the noun out and lets の stand in for it, so it only works when both speakers already know what is being talked about - "I read the one I bought yesterday." Grammatically the difference is whether there is a noun at the end of the clause at all; conversationally, (b) assumes shared context and (a) does not.

Counters

Meaning · you cannot count a Japanese noun with a bare number - every noun needs a counter word

English says "three apples". Japanese says りんごを三(みっ)つ - number plus a counter chosen to fit the kind of thing being counted. The counters themselves are easy; the readings are not, because the number and the counter fuse and change sound. Those changes are the whole content of this page.

Counters · Nuance & register

Counters are not optional and not stylistic - they are part of the grammar, and a bare number in front of a noun is simply ungrammatical.

The ～つ series is your safety net. It is native Japanese vocabulary, it runs only from one to ten, and it counts almost any concrete object whose proper counter you have forgotten. Nobody will misunderstand りんごを四(よっ)つ. Above ten, ～つ stops existing and you fall back on 個(こ) or the specific counter.

People are the one place you cannot improvise. ❌いちにん and ❌ににん do not exist. 一人(ひとり) and 二人(ふたり) are irregular native forms, and from three on the counter switches to the Sino-Japanese にん. This is worth over- learning: 一人(ひとり)で ("alone") and 二人(ふたり)で ("the two of us") are everyday words.

Counters · Nuance & register (cont.)

Register. In shops and restaurants you will hear the deferential 名(めい)様(さま) for people - 二名様(にめいさま) - and お一人(ひとり)様(さま). You are not expected to produce these at N5, only to recognise them. In casual speech the counter can drop when context is obvious: コーヒー、二(ふた)つ。

Where the phrase sits. The neutral position is after the noun-plus-particle, as an adverb. Putting it in front with の (三(みっ)つのりんご) is also correct but feels more written and slightly more emphatic - it is what you use when the quantity is the point.

Counters · ～つ vs ～個(こ)

Rule of thumb - both count generic objects. ～つ is native, softer, and stops at ten; 個(こ) is Sino-Japanese, has no upper limit, and leans towards small, discrete, roundish things.

たまごを六(むっ)つ使(つか)います。
I'll use six eggs.

たまごを**十二個(じゅうにこ)**買(か)いました。
I bought twelve eggs.

The second sentence cannot use ～つ, because the series has no word for twelve. Below ten the two are largely interchangeable for objects like eggs, apples and boxes, with ～つ sounding a shade more everyday.

Counters · ～つ vs ～個(こ) (cont.)

個(こ) is also what a shop assistant will say, because it works for any quantity and does not need a decision about shape.

Counters · ～回(かい) vs ～階(かい)

Rule of thumb - 回(かい) counts times; 階(かい) counts floors. They sound almost identical, and the one place they diverge is at three: 三回(さんかい) but 三階(さんがい).

エレベーターで**三階(さんがい)**まで上(あ)がってください。
Please take the lift up to the third floor.

このボタンを**三回(さんかい)**押(お)してください。
Please press this button three times.

Both 何回(なんかい) ("how many times") and 何階(なんがい) ("which floor") exist, and the 階(かい) one is voiced. Getting 三階(さんがい) right is a small thing that immediately marks a careful learner.

Counters · りんごを三(みっ)つ vs 三(みっ)つのりんご

Rule of thumb - the counter phrase normally goes after the noun and its particle. Moving it in front with の is grammatical but shifts the emphasis onto the quantity.

テーブルにりんごを三(みっ)つ置(お)きました。
I put three apples on the table.

テーブルに三(みっ)つのりんごを置(お)きました。
I put three apples on the table. (with the "three" foregrounded)

The first is what people say. The second reads like a description in a catalogue or a maths problem, and a learner who defaults to it - because it matches English word order - will sound stilted without ever making an outright error.

Counters · Common mistakes

  • ❌ りんごを三(さん)買(か)いました。 → ✅ りんごを三(みっ)つ買(か)いました。
    A bare number cannot count a noun. You must choose a counter, and ～つ is the safe default for objects up to ten.

  • ❌ いちにん、ににん → ✅ 一人(ひとり)、二人(ふたり)
    The first two people are native readings and do not follow the pattern. From 三人(さんにん) onwards the Sino-Japanese にん takes over.

  • ❌ よんにん → ✅ よにん
    四人(よにん) drops the ん. (よんにん is widely heard, but よにん is what you should say and what the JLPT expects.)

  • ❌ さんほん、いちほん → ✅ さんぼん、いっぽん
    本(ほん) voices to ぼん after 三(さん) and geminates to ぽん after 1, 6, 8 and 10. The same shift hits 杯(はい)、匹(ひき) and 分(ふん).

  • ❌ 二(に)つ、三(さん)つ → ✅ 二(ふた)つ、三(みっ)つ
    The ～つ series takes native numerals (ひと、ふた、みっ…), never the Sino-Japanese ones. Mixing the two systems is the most audible counter error there is.

Counters · Practice 1

1. ノートを____買(か)いました。（3）

ノートを三冊(さんさつ)買(か)いました。（also acceptable: 三(みっ)つ）

Notebooks are bound volumes, so the proper counter is 冊(さつ) - read さんさつ, with no sound change at three. If the counter escapes you, 三(みっ)つ is always understood.

Counters · Practice 2

2. 事務所(じむしょ)は（三回(さんかい)・三階(さんがい)）にあります。

事務所(じむしょ)は三階(さんがい)にあります。

A floor of a building is 階(かい), and after 三(さん) it voices to がい. 回(かい) would be counting occurrences, which makes no sense with にあります.

Counters · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "There were two cats and one dog in the garden."

庭(にわ)に猫(ねこ)が二匹(にひき)と犬(いぬ)が一匹(いっぴき)いました。

Small animals take 匹(ひき), which geminates to ぴき after one: いっぴき, not ❌いちひき. Note also that animals are counted with いる, not ある.

Counters · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 部屋(へや)に人(ひと)が三(みっ)ついます。

✅ 部屋(へや)に人(ひと)が三人(さんにん)います。

The ～つ series counts objects and never people. People always take 人(にん), which is irregular at one and two (一人(ひとり), 二人(ふたり)) but perfectly regular from three: さんにん.

Counters · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) みかんを四(よっ)つ食(た)べました。 (b) 四(よっ)つのみかんを食(た)べました。

(a) is the ordinary sentence. The counter phrase sits after the object and its particle and works adverbially - this is how quantities are normally expressed, and it is what you should default to.

(b) attaches the quantity to the noun with の, which makes "the four mandarins" a single described thing. It is correct, but it foregrounds the number in a way that suits a written description or a context where the count itself is the news. Learners produce (b) too often because it mirrors English word order.

ずつ

Meaning · ~ each; ~ at a time - the same amount, distributed or repeated

ずつ takes a quantity and spreads it evenly. Either across people (一(ひと)つずつ = one each) or across time (少(すこ)しずつ = a little at a time). Both readings come from the same idea: the same portion, again and again.

ずつ · Formation

ずつ attaches to the end of a quantity expression - a number with its counter, or a word of amount. It never attaches to a bare noun.

ずつ · Formation (cont.)

Attaches to Form Example
Number + counter Number+Counter + ずつ 一(ひと)つ → 一(ひと)つずつ
Number + counter (people) 二人(ふたり) + ずつ 二人(ふたり)ずつ

Amount word 少(すこ)し + ずつ 少(すこ)しずつ

ずつ · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - count first, ずつ second. The quantity always comes in front: 一(ひと)つずつ, never ❌ずつ一(ひと)つ. And there must be a quantity - ずつ cannot hang off a noun on its own.

Note - ずつ is written in hiragana. You will occasionally meet the old spelling づつ in older books and on shop signs; it is the same word and it is not the modern standard.

ずつ · Examples

Take one sweet each.

毎日(まいにち)漢字(かんじ)を五(いつ)つずつ覚(おぼ)えます。
I learn five kanji a day.

少(すこ)しずつ上手(じょうず)になっていますよ。
You're getting better bit by bit.

二人(ふたり)ずつグループを作(つく)ってください。
Please form groups of two.

一日(いちにち)に三(さん)ページずつ読(よ)めば、一(いっ)か月(げつ)で終(お)わります。

ずつ · Examples (cont.)

貯金(ちょきん)は毎月(まいつき)少(すこ)しずつ増(ふ)えている。
My savings are growing a little at a time.

新(あたら)しい仕事(しごと)にも、少(すこ)しずつ慣(な)れてきました。
I'm gradually getting used to the new job too.

お土産(みやげ)のクッキーは、一人(ひとり)二(に)枚(まい)ずつ配(くば)ってください。
Please hand out the souvenir cookies, two per person.

ずつ · Nuance & register

ずつ is register-neutral and unremarkable - it appears in classroom instructions, in recipes, in business email and in conversation without changing shape.

Its two senses are worth separating in your head, because they behave differently:

Distributive - "each". 一人(ひとり)二(に)枚(まい)ずつ. There is a set of recipients and everyone gets the same amount. This is the sense you need when handing things out, forming groups, or dividing anything up. Japanese uses ずつ here where English often uses nothing at all: "take one" is ambiguous in English, 一(ひと)つずつとって is not.

Incremental - "at a time". 少(すこ)しずつ. There is no set of recipients; the repetition is over time. This is by far the most common use of the word, and 少(すこ)しずつ is close to being a fixed adverb meaning "gradually".

ずつ · Nuance & register (cont.)

The casual variant is ちょっとずつ, which is warmer and softer than 少(すこ)しずつ and belongs to speech only. You will also hear the emphatic 一(ひと)つ一(ひと)つ ("one by one, individually"), which is not the same thing: that stresses handling each item separately rather than distributing equally.

One small pitfall: ずつ needs the sharing to be even. If two people get different amounts, ずつ is wrong and you should just name the amounts.

ずつ · 一(ひと)つ vs 一(ひと)つずつ

Rule of thumb - without ずつ, the number is the total. With ずつ, the number is the share per person or per occasion.

クッキーを二(ふた)つ買(か)いました。
I bought two cookies. (two in total)

クッキーを二(ふた)つずつ買(か)いました。
I bought two cookies each. (two per person, so more than two altogether)

The difference is not decoration - it changes the arithmetic. Leaving ずつ out of a sentence about handing things round is one of the errors most likely to produce an actual misunderstanding.

ずつ · 少(すこ)しずつ vs だんだん

Rule of thumb - 少(すこ)しずつ describes the size of the steps; だんだん describes the direction of the change. They overlap in English as "gradually".

スープに水(みず)を少(すこ)しずつ足(た)してください。
Add the water to the soup a little at a time.

だんだん暖(あたた)かくなってきました。
It's been getting warmer and warmer.

You cannot swap them here: ❌だんだん水(みず)を足(た)してください gives no instruction about portions at all.

ずつ · 少(すこ)しずつ vs だんだん (cont.)

少(すこ)しずつ implies a series of small increments you could in principle count; だんだん implies a smooth trend and carries no notion of portions. They combine happily - 少(すこ)しずつだんだん良(よ)くなる is redundant, but 少(すこ)しずつ覚(おぼ)える and だんだん覚(おぼ)える differ: the first is about the batches, the second about the trajectory.

ずつ · ずつ vs それぞれ

Rule of thumb - ずつ needs a quantity and says everyone gets the same amount. それぞれ needs no quantity and says only that each person has their own - possibly different - one.

学生(がくせい)に本(ほん)を一冊(いっさつ)ずつ渡(わた)しました。
I handed each student one book. (same number for everybody)

学生(がくせい)はそれぞれ違(ちが)う意見(いけん)を持(も)っています。
The students each have different opinions.

Because ずつ insists on equality, ❌それぞれ違(ちが)う数(かず)ずつ is self-contradictory. And because it needs a quantity, ずつ cannot replace それぞれ in the second sentence at all.

ずつ · Common mistakes

  • ❌ ずつ一(ひと)つとってください。 → ✅ 一(ひと)つずつとってください。
    ずつ follows the quantity; it never leads. Word order here is fixed.

  • ❌ りんごずつ買(か)いました。 → ✅ りんごを一(ひと)つずつ買(か)いました。
    ずつ attaches to an amount, not to a noun. Without a number and counter in front of it there is nothing for it to distribute.

  • ❌ 私(わたし)にケーキを一(ひと)つずつください。 → ✅ 私(わたし)にケーキを一(ひと)つください。
    With a single recipient there is nothing to distribute across, so ずつ has no work to do and sounds strange.

  • ❌ 少(すこ)しずつずつ良(よ)くなる → ✅ 少(すこ)しずつ良(よ)くなる
    One ずつ per quantity. Doubling it for emphasis is not a thing; if you want emphasis, use 少(すこ)しずつではあるが or simply repeat the verb.

ずつ · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 田中(たなか)さんは三(さん)枚(まい)、山田(やまだ)さんは二(に)枚(まい)ずつもらいました。 → ✅ …山田(やまだ)さんは二(に)枚(まい)もらいました。
    ずつ promises that the shares are equal. If they are not, drop it and state the amounts.

ずつ · Practice 1

1. クラスを四人(よにん)____のグループに分(わ)けます。

クラスを四人(よにん)ずつのグループに分(わ)けます。

The point is that every group has the same size, which is exactly what ずつ encodes. Without it the sentence would just describe one group of four rather than a whole class divided evenly.

ずつ · Practice 2

2. 学生(がくせい)に紙(かみ)を一枚(いちまい)（ずつ・それぞれ）渡(わた)してください。

学生(がくせい)に紙(かみ)を一枚(いちまい)ずつ渡(わた)してください。

There is a quantity here - 一枚(いちまい) - and the instruction is that everybody gets that same quantity. それぞれ cannot follow a counter phrase; it would have to go earlier and would lose the "one apiece" meaning.

ずつ · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "Please add the sugar a little at a time."

砂糖(さとう)を少(すこ)しずつ入(い)れてください。

This is the incremental sense: no recipients, just repeated small portions over time. 少(すこ)しずつ is close to a fixed adverb and is the standard phrase in recipes.

ずつ · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: みなさんにペンをずつ一本(いっぽん)配(くば)ります。

✅ みなさんにペンを一本(いっぽん)ずつ配(くば)ります。

ずつ goes after the quantity, not before it. The counter phrase 一本(いっぽん) has to be complete before ずつ can attach - and note the reading いっぽん, not ❌いちほん.

ずつ · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 子(こ)どもたちにあめを三(みっ)つあげました。 (b) 子(こ)どもたちにあめを三(みっ)つずつあげました。

(a) means three sweets were given to the children as a group - they will have to work out among themselves who gets what, and if there are four children someone is going without.

(b) means every child got three, so the total depends on how many children there were. The single word ずつ is doing all of that work, and English needs a whole phrase ("three each") to match it. When you are dividing anything up, leaving ずつ out changes the facts.
