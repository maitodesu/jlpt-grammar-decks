N5 - Who does what

が - subject marker

Meaning · marks the subject of a clause, and singles out which one it is

が names the thing that does the verb, or the thing an adjective is true of. Its second job is the one learners underestimate: が points. It presents its noun as the piece of news in the sentence - the answer to a "which one?" that may never have been asked out loud.

が - subject marker · Formation

が is a particle. It attaches directly to the noun, with no change to the noun.

が - subject marker · Formation (cont.)

Attaches to Form Example
Noun Noun + が 犬(いぬ) → 犬(いぬ)が好(す)きです
Question word Question word + が (never は) だれ → だれが来(き)ますか
Nominalised clause Clause + の / こと + が 走(はし)る → 走(はし)るのが速(はや)い
Subject of a noun-modifying clause Noun + が + verb + Noun 私(わたし)が作(つく)った → 私(わたし)が作(つく)ったケーキ
Object of 好(す)き・上手(じょうず)・ある・わかる・ほしい Noun + が + predicate 日本語(にほんご) → 日本語(にほんご)がわかります
Object of 見(み)える・聞(き)こえる・できる Noun + が + predicate 音(おと) → 音(おと)が聞(き)こえる

が - subject marker · Formation (cont.)

Note - が never combines with は, も or を. When a subject becomes the topic, が is replaced: 犬(いぬ)が → 犬(いぬ)は, 犬(いぬ)も. There is no がは or がも.

が - subject marker · Examples

雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っています。
It's raining.

だれが来(き)ますか。
Who's coming?

私(わたし)は犬(いぬ)が好(す)きです。
I like dogs.

あ、バスが来(き)た。
Oh - here comes the bus.

この部屋(へや)は窓(まど)が大(おお)きくて、明(あか)るいですね。
This room has big windows - it's nice and bright.

が - subject marker · Examples (cont.)

時間(じかん)がないから、また今度(こんど)話(はな)しましょう。
I don't have time, so let's talk another time.

去年(きょねん)買(か)ったパソコンが急(きゅう)に動(うご)かなくなって、困(こま)っています。
The laptop I bought last year suddenly stopped working, and I'm stuck.

すみません、あそこに立(た)っている人(ひと)が田中(たなか)さんですか。
Excuse me - is the person standing over there Tanaka-san?

が - subject marker · Nuance & register

が is completely register-neutral. It is identical in plain and polite speech, in men's and women's speech, and in writing. Nothing about choosing が makes you sound formal or casual.

What does vary is whether you say it at all. In relaxed conversation が is routinely dropped when the sentence is short and the subject is obvious:

  • 雨(あめ)、降(ふ)ってるよ。 - "It's raining."
  • 時間(じかん)ある? - "Got a minute?"

Dropping が is casual, not wrong. Keep it in writing, in ます-form speech, and any time the sentence is long enough that the listener needs the signpost.

が - subject marker · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two things to keep separate. This が is a particle that follows a noun. There is a completely different が - a conjunction meaning "but" - that follows a whole clause: 高(たか)いですが、買(か)います。 Same syllable, unrelated word. If what precedes が is a finished sentence, it is the "but" one.

Finally, が marks the subject of its own clause, which is why it turns up in places English speakers do not expect: inside relative clauses, inside と思(おも)う clauses, inside conditionals. 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ったら、中止(ちゅうし)です。

が - subject marker · が vs は

This is the hardest thing at N5, and the reason is that は is not a subject marker at all. は marks a topic - "about this thing, here's what I have to say." が marks a subject. The two often sit in the same slot in the sentence, which is why they look like alternatives, but they are answering different needs.

Rule of thumb - the new information goes after は, but before が.

That one line does most of the work. Look at where the news is:

田中(たなか)さんは京都(きょうと)へ行(い)きました。
Tanaka went to Kyoto.

We already know who Tanaka is; the news is Kyoto, and it comes after は. This answers "What did Tanaka do?"

が - subject marker · が vs は (cont.)

田中(たなか)さんが京都(きょうと)へ行(い)きました。
It was Tanaka who went to Kyoto.

Here we already know somebody went to Kyoto; the news is Tanaka, and it comes before が. This answers "Who went to Kyoto?"

The same logic explains why question words always take が, never は. A question word is the unknown, so it has to sit on the が side:

だれがこの窓(まど)を割(わ)りましたか。
Who broke this window?

And the answer keeps が, because the answer is still the news:

が - subject marker · が vs は (cont.)

弟(おとうと)が割(わ)りました。
My little brother broke it.

A second, related use: が is how you report something you have just noticed - an event presented whole, with no topic yet established. This is why stories begin with が and continue with は:

むかしむかし、ある村(むら)におじいさんが住(す)んでいました。
Long ago, in a certain village, there lived an old man.

おじいさんは毎日(まいにち)山(やま)へ木(き)を切(き)りに行(い)きました。
Every day the old man went to the mountain to cut wood.

Sentence one introduces him - brand new, so が. Sentence two talks about him - now known, so は.

が - subject marker · が vs は (cont.)

Because they do different jobs, both can appear in one sentence, and the classic example is worth memorising whole:

象(ぞう)は鼻(はな)が長(なが)い。
Elephants have long trunks. (As for elephants - the nose is long.)

象(ぞう) is the topic; 鼻(はな) is the subject of 長(なが)い. Neither particle could do the other's job here.

One last thing は can do that が cannot: contrast. は implicitly sets its noun against something else.

コーヒーは飲(の)みますが、お酒(さけ)は飲(の)みません。
I drink coffee, but I don't drink alcohol.

が - subject marker · が vs は (cont.)

Swap those to が and the contrast evaporates.

が - subject marker · が vs は inside a noun-modifying clause

Rule of thumb - inside a clause that modifies a noun, the subject takes が. は is not available there.

は scopes over a whole sentence, so it cannot be trapped inside a clause that is only decorating a noun. This produces one of the most common N5 errors.

これは私(わたし)が撮(と)った写真(しゃしん)です。
This is a photo I took.

私(わたし)が撮(と)った modifies 写真(しゃしん). Saying ❌私(わたし)は撮(と)った写真(しゃしん) breaks the sentence, because は would try to make 私(わたし) the topic of the outer sentence.

The same holds inside conditionals and quoted thoughts:

が - subject marker · が vs は inside a noun-modifying clause (cont.)

田中(たなか)さんが来(く)ると思(おも)います。
I think Tanaka is coming.

Note - in a noun-modifying clause, が can be swapped for の with no change in meaning: 私(わたし)の撮(と)った写真(しゃしん). This is common in writing and sounds slightly compact. It only works inside such clauses - never in a main sentence.

が - subject marker · が vs を

Rule of thumb - a small set of predicates take their "object" with が, not を, because grammatically that noun is the subject.

好(す)き, 嫌(きら)い, 上手(じょうず), 下手(へた), ほしい, わかる, できる, ある, 見(み)える, 聞(き)こえる all belong to this set. In English these look transitive ("I understand Japanese"), so を is a strong pull - and it is wrong.

私(わたし)は日本語(にほんご)がわかります。
I understand Japanese.

山(やま)の上(うえ)から海(うみ)が見(み)えます。
You can see the sea from the top of the mountain.

が - subject marker · が vs を (cont.)

Compare 見(み)る, an ordinary transitive verb, which does take を: 海(うみ)を見(み)ます - "I look at the sea." 見(み)える is "is visible", so the sea is what is - hence が.

が - subject marker · Common mistakes

  • ❌ だれは来(き)ましたか。 → ✅ だれが来(き)ましたか。
    A question word is the unknown, and unknowns cannot be topics. Question words take が.

  • ❌ これは私(わたし)は撮(と)った写真(しゃしん)です。 → ✅ これは私(わたし)が撮(と)った写真(しゃしん)です。
    Inside a clause modifying a noun, the subject takes が. は cannot live there.

  • ❌ 私(わたし)は日本語(にほんご)をわかります。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は日本語(にほんご)がわかります。
    English transitivity is not a guide. わかる, できる, 好(す)き, ほしい, 見(み)える all take が.

  • ❌ 犬(いぬ)がは好(す)きです。 → ✅ 犬(いぬ)は好(す)きです。
    が is replaced by は and も, never stacked with them.

  • ❌ ぼくはやります!（volunteering, "I'll do it!"）→ ✅ ぼくがやります!
    Volunteering means identifying who - you are the news. That is a が job.

が - subject marker · Practice 1

1. だれ____このケーキを作(つく)りましたか。

だれがこのケーキを作(つく)りましたか。

Question words are always the unknown information in the sentence, and the unknown sits on the が side. だれは is never possible.

が - subject marker · Practice 2

2. 「山田(やまだ)さんはどんな音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)きますか。」 「山田(やまだ)さん（は・が）ジャズをよく聞(き)きます。」

山田(やまだ)さんはジャズをよく聞(き)きます。

The question already put 山田(やまだ)さん on the table, so he is old information - the topic. The news is ジャズ, and news comes after は. Using が here would mean "it's Yamada who listens to jazz", answering a question nobody asked.

が - subject marker · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "Who made this? - I made it."

「だれがこれを作(つく)りましたか。」「私(わたし)が作(つく)りました。」

Both halves take が. The question word is the unknown, and the answer replaces that unknown - it is still the news, so it keeps が. Answering 「私(わたし)は作(つく)りました」 would sound like a contrast: "I made it (whatever the others did)."

が - subject marker · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 母(はは)は作(つく)った料理(りょうり)はとてもおいしいです。

✅ 母(はは)が作(つく)った料理(りょうり)はとてもおいしいです。 "The food my mother makes is delicious."

母(はは)が作(つく)った is a clause modifying 料理(りょうり), and a subject inside such a clause must take が. The outer sentence keeps its own topic particle on 料理(りょうり)は.

が - subject marker · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 山田(やまだ)さんは来(き)ました。 (b) 山田(やまだ)さんが来(き)ました。

(a) treats 山田(やまだ)さん as already-known and reports what he did - "Yamada came (whatever the others did)." It fits a conversation that was already about him, and with は it can carry a hint of contrast.

(b) presents 山田(やまだ)さん as the news - "Yamada is the one who came", or, said on noticing him, simply "Yamada's here!" It answers "who came?", and it is what you would say announcing his arrival to a room that did not know.

が - but

Meaning · joins two clauses - "…, but …"

This が is a conjunction, not a particle: it follows a whole finished clause and links it to the next one. Its core job is contrast, but a great deal of the time it is doing something softer - simply setting the scene for what you are about to say.

が - but · Formation

が attaches to a complete clause. Whatever politeness that clause ends in stays put, and が goes on after it.

Attaches to Form Example
Polite clause ～ます／～です + が 高(たか)いです → 高(たか)いですが
Plain clause (verb) dictionary / ～た / ～ない + が 行(い)った → 行(い)ったが
Plain clause (い-adj) ～い + が 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いが
Noun / な-adjective + だ／です first 学生(がくせい) → 学生(がくせい)だが／学生(がくせい)ですが
Sentence-final clause + が… (trailing off) 失礼(しつれい)ですが…
Casual equivalent replace が with けど 高(たか)いけど

が - but · Formation (cont.)

Note - the noun row is the one people get wrong. が cannot sit directly on a noun the way the subject particle can. ❌学生(がくせい)が、… meaning "I'm a student, but…" is not possible; the clause has to be finished with a copula first - 学生(がくせい)ですが.

が - but · Examples

この店(みせ)は安(やす)いですが、あまりおいしくないです。
This place is cheap, but the food isn't great.

外(そと)は寒(さむ)いですが、部屋(へや)の中(なか)は暖(あたた)かいです。
It's cold outside, but it's warm in the room.

すみませんが、ちょっと静(しず)かにしてください。
Sorry to bother you, but could you keep it down a little?

山田(やまだ)ですが、田中(たなか)さんはいらっしゃいますか。
This is Yamada - is Tanaka-san there?

何度(なんど)も電話(でんわ)しましたが、だれも出(で)ませんでした。
I called several times, but nobody picked up.

が - but · Examples (cont.)

行(い)きたいが、今週(こんしゅう)は時間(じかん)がない。
I'd like to go, but I've no time this week.

あの映画(えいが)は三時間(さんじかん)もありますが、全然(ぜんぜん)長(なが)く感(かん)じませんでした。
That film is a full three hours long, but it didn't feel long at all.

明日(あした)の予約(よやく)をお願(ねが)いしたいのですが…。
I'd like to make a booking for tomorrow, if I may…

が - but · Nuance & register

が is the formal member of a family. In descending order of stiffness: が → けれども → けれど → けども → けど. All mean the same thing. が is at home in writing, in speeches, in business email and in polite spoken Japanese attached to です / ます. Attached to a plain form in casual conversation (「行(い)きたいが」) it sounds bookish or old-fashioned; a friend would say 「行(い)きたいけど」.

Now the part that changes how you read Japanese. が very often does not mean "but". A huge share of its uses are what you might call a preamble: the first clause exists only to give the listener a place to stand, and there is no contrast at all.

が - but · Nuance & register (cont.)

  • 「失礼(しつれい)ですが、日本(にほん)の方(かた)ですか。」 - "Excuse me, are you
    Japanese?" Nothing is being contrasted; が is politely clearing its throat.
  • 「田中(たなか)ですが、…」 - introducing yourself on the phone before the request.
  • 「質問(しつもん)があるんですが、…」 - "I have a question…"

Translating those with "but" produces nonsense. Read が as a soft "and so" or a comma whenever the first clause is not actually being denied by the second.

Related to this is the trailing が, where the sentence simply stops: 「予約(よやく)したいのですが…」. Leaving the request unsaid is a politeness strategy - you set up the situation and let the listener supply the rest, which is less imposing than stating your demand outright. This is extremely common in shops, offices and phone calls, and a learner who finishes every sentence can sound blunt by comparison.

が - but · Nuance & register (cont.)

One caution about mixing levels. Writing a plain clause before が and a polite one after it (「安(やす)いが、おいしいです」) is normal in written Japanese but odd in speech. When speaking, keep both halves polite.

が - but · が vs けど

Rule of thumb - same meaning, different register. が for writing and polite speech; けど for conversation.

この漢字(かんじ)は読(よ)めますが、書(か)けません。
I can read this character, but I can't write it.

この漢字(かんじ)、読(よ)めるけど、書(か)けない。
I can read this one, but I can't write it.

けど also attaches happily to polite forms - 「読(よ)めますけど」 - which gives you a middle register: polite, but relaxed. That combination is very common in spoken Japanese at work. Going the other way, plain form + が in speech is the one combination to avoid unless you are deliberately sounding formal.

が - but · が vs でも

Rule of thumb - が joins two clauses inside one sentence. でも starts a new sentence.

昨日(きのう)は雨(あめ)でしたが、試合(しあい)はありました。
It rained yesterday, but the match went ahead.

昨日(きのう)は雨(あめ)でした。でも、試合(しあい)はありました。
It rained yesterday. But the match went ahead.

The meaning is the same; the packaging differs. でも puts a full stop in first, which makes the contrast land harder - it is the spoken equivalent of starting a new paragraph. ❌雨(あめ)でしたでも is impossible: でも cannot glue two clauses together the way が does. In formal writing, しかし plays the same sentence-initial role as でも.

が - but · が (but) vs が (subject marker)

Rule of thumb - look at what comes before the が. A bare noun means it is the subject marker; a finished clause means it is the conjunction.

The two are unrelated words that happen to share a syllable, and the test is mechanical.

時間(じかん)がありません。
I don't have time.

時間(じかん)はありますが、お金(かね)がありません。
I have the time, but not the money.

が - but · が (but) vs が (subject marker) (cont.)

In the first, 時間(じかん) is just a noun, so が is marking it as the subject of ありません. In the second, 時間(じかん)はあります is a whole sentence already, so the が after it can only be the conjunction. Sentences containing both are ordinary and cause no confusion once you have the test: 「雨(あめ)が降(ふ)っていましたが、傘(かさ)はありませんでした。」

が - but · Common mistakes

  • ❌ この店(みせ)は安(やす)いですでも、おいしくないです。 → ✅ 安(やす)いですが、…／安(やす)いです。でも、…
    でも cannot join clauses. Either use が inside one sentence, or break the sentence and start the next one with でも.

  • ❌ 学生(がくせい)が、お金(かね)がありません。 → ✅ 学生(がくせい)ですが、お金(かね)がありません。
    The conjunction needs a finished clause in front of it, so a noun must first take the copula.

  • ❌ Reading 「失礼(しつれい)ですが」 as "it's rude, but". → ✅ "Excuse me, but…"
    Preamble が is not adversative. If the second clause does not deny the first, do not translate it as "but".

  • ❌ 友(とも)だちに「安(やす)いが、おいしくないよ」と言(い)いました。 → ✅ 「安(やす)いけど、おいしくないよ」
    Plain form + が in casual speech sounds stiff and literary. Between friends it is けど.

が - but · Practice 1

1. 日本語(にほんご)は難(むずか)しいです____、面白(おもしろ)いです。

難(むずか)しいですが、面白(おもしろ)いです。

The two clauses genuinely pull against each other, and both are polite, so the polite conjunction が joins them inside a single sentence. In conversation けど would do the same job more casually.

が - but · Practice 2

2. Choose: 昨日(きのう)は忙(いそが)しかったです（が・でも）、今日(きょう)は暇(ひま)です。

忙(いそが)しかったですが、今日(きょう)は暇(ひま)です。

でも only works at the start of a new sentence. Since the two halves are being kept in one sentence with a comma, the connector has to be が.

が - but · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese, politely: "Excuse me, but is this seat taken?"

すみませんが、この席(せき)は空(あ)いていますか。

This is preamble が, not contrast - the first clause is only softening the approach. English uses "but" here too, which is a rare case of the two languages lining up.

が - but · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: この部屋(へや)は広(ひろ)いですでも、暗(くら)いです。

✅ この部屋(へや)は広(ひろ)いですが、暗(くら)いです。

でも cannot be attached to the end of a clause. Replace it with が, or split the sentence: 「広(ひろ)いです。でも、暗(くら)いです。」

が - but · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 田中(たなか)ですが、山田(やまだ)さんをお願(ねが)いします。 (b) 田中(たなか)です。山田(やまだ)さんをお願(ねが)いします。

Neither sentence contains any contrast, which is the point of the pair.

(a) uses the preamble が to hand the two pieces over as one smooth move: the name exists only to prepare the request, and the sentence flows into it. This is the standard shape of a phone call.

(b) states the name and then makes the request as a separate, self-contained sentence. It is not wrong, but it is more abrupt - two facts placed side by side rather than one leading into the other.

を

Meaning · marks the direct object - the thing the verb is done to

を points at what receives the action: the thing eaten, written, bought, opened. It has two further jobs that surprise learners - it marks the space you move through and the place you leave. It is written with its own kana but pronounced お.

を · Formation

を attaches straight to the noun. Which of its three jobs it is doing depends entirely on the verb.

を · Formation (cont.)

Attaches to Form Example
Direct object Noun + を + transitive verb ごはん → ごはんを食(た)べる
Space moved through Place + を + 歩(ある)く・走(はし)る・飛(と)ぶ・渡(わた)る 公園(こうえん) → 公園(こうえん)を歩(ある)く
Place left / exited Place + を + 出(で)る・降(お)りる・卒業(そつぎょう)する 家(いえ) → 家(いえ)を出(で)る
Under は or も を is replaced ごはんを → ごはんは／ごはんも
Casual speech を is often dropped ごはん食(た)べた?
Pronunciation written を, said お -

を · Formation (cont.)

Note - a single clause normally carries only one を. If you find yourself writing two, one of them is usually meant to be に, で, or a separate clause.

を · Examples

朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べました。
I ate breakfast.

手紙(てがみ)を書(か)きます。
I'm going to write a letter.

毎朝(まいあさ)、公園(こうえん)を歩(ある)きます。
I walk through the park every morning.

七時(しちじ)に家(いえ)を出(で)ます。
I leave the house at seven.

次(つぎ)の駅(えき)で電車(でんしゃ)を降(お)ります。
I get off the train at the next station.

を · Examples (cont.)

鳥(とり)が空(そら)を飛(と)んでいます。
A bird is flying across the sky.

すみません、その角(かど)を右(みぎ)に曲(ま)がってください。
Excuse me - please turn right at that corner.

大学(だいがく)を出(で)てから、三年間(さんねんかん)この会社(かいしゃ)で働(はたら)いています。
Since leaving university I've been working at this company for three years.

を · Nuance & register

を is register-neutral and carries no politeness of its own. It is written with a kana used for nothing else, and it is pronounced o, exactly like お. Saying "wo" marks you instantly as a learner; the only places you will hear it are singing and extremely careful enunciation.

The thing to internalise is how freely を is dropped in casual speech. Short sentences where the object is obvious lose it constantly:

  • 「ごはん、食(た)べた?」 - "Did you eat?"
  • 「これ、見(み)て。」 - "Look at this."
  • 「コーヒー飲(の)む?」 - "Want coffee?"

That is casual, not wrong. Keep を in writing, in ます-form speech, and any time the sentence is long enough that the listener needs the signpost. Also keep it when the sentence would otherwise be ambiguous about who is doing what to whom.

を · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two smaller notes. を never stacks with は, も or が - when the object becomes the topic it is replaced: ごはんは食(た)べました. And を is the marker used with the causative later on, which is where the one-を-per-clause tendency starts to bite; you do not need that yet, but it is why the rule is worth knowing early.

を · を vs が

Rule of thumb - a small set of predicates take their "object" with が, not を, because grammatically that noun is their subject.

English transitivity is a poor guide here, and this is one of the most persistent N5 errors.

毎日(まいにち)、新聞(しんぶん)を読(よ)みます。
I read the newspaper every day.

私(わたし)はスポーツが好(す)きです。
I like sports.

読(よ)む is an ordinary transitive verb and takes を. But 好(す)き, 嫌(きら)い, 上手(じょうず), ほしい, わかる, できる, ある, 見(み)える and 聞(き)こえる all take が, however transitive they look in English. Learn that set as a list; there is no shortcut.

を · を vs が (cont.)

A useful pair is 見(み)る and 見(み)える. Looking at something is an action, so 「海(うみ)を見(み)ます」. Something being visible is a state, so 「海(うみ)が見(み)えます」.

を · を vs で vs に for places

Rule of thumb - を = through it, で = at it, に = to it.

The same place noun takes three different particles depending on what the verb is doing with it, and this is where the "path" use of を earns its keep.

公園(こうえん)を走(はし)ります。
I run through the park.

公園(こうえん)で走(はし)ります。
I run at the park.

公園(こうえん)に行(い)きます。
I go to the park.

を · を vs で vs に for places (cont.)

With を, the park is the ground your route crosses - you are moving along and through it. With で, the park is merely the location where the running takes place. に marks the destination and cannot be used for either of the others.

Verbs that describe covering ground - 歩(ある)く, 走(はし)る, 飛(と)ぶ, 渡(わた)る, 通(とお)る, 曲(ま)がる - take を for the space they cover, even though none of them is transitive in English.

を · を出(で)る vs から出(で)る

Rule of thumb - を treats the place as something you leave behind; から treats it as the point you started from.

八時(はちじ)に会社(かいしゃ)を出(で)ました。
I left the office at eight.

会社(かいしゃ)から駅(えき)まで歩(ある)きます。
I walk from the office to the station.

With を, the focus is on the act of departure itself - you are done with that place. With から, the place is one end of a route, and the sentence is really about the journey. This is why 大学(だいがく)を出(で)る can mean "graduate" while 大学(だいがく)から出(で)る just means physically walking out of the building.

を · を出(で)る vs から出(で)る (cont.)

The same split shows up with vehicles: 電車(でんしゃ)を降(お)りる is the normal way to say "get off the train", because you are leaving the train behind.

を · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 私(わたし)は日本語(にほんご)をわかります。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は日本語(にほんご)がわかります。
    わかる, できる, 好(す)き, ほしい and friends take が even though English makes them look transitive.

  • ❌ 電車(でんしゃ)を乗(の)ります。 → ✅ 電車(でんしゃ)に乗(の)ります。
    Getting on takes に (you end up inside it); getting off takes を (電車(でんしゃ)を降(お)りる). The pair has to be learned together.

  • ❌ 道(みち)で渡(わた)ってください。 → ✅ 道(みち)を渡(わた)ってください。
    Crossing covers ground, so the road is the space traversed. で would make it the location where some other activity happens.

  • ❌ ごはんをも食(た)べました。 → ✅ ごはんも食(た)べました。
    を is replaced by は and も, never stacked with them.

を · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ Pronouncing を as wo. → ✅ It is o, identical to お.

を · Practice 1

1. 毎晩(まいばん)、寝(ね)る前(まえ)に少(すこ)し本(ほん)____読(よ)みます。

本(ほん)を読(よ)みます。

読(よ)む is a plain transitive verb and the book is what gets read, so it takes the direct-object marker. In casual speech the particle would often simply be dropped.

を · Practice 2

2. Choose: 橋(はし)（を・で）渡(わた)ると、右(みぎ)に郵便局(ゆうびんきょく)があります。

橋(はし)を渡(わた)ると…

渡(わた)る is a verb of covering ground, so the bridge is the space you move across and takes を. で would present the bridge as a location where something else was going on.

を · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "I get off the bus in front of the station."

駅(えき)の前(まえ)でバスを降(お)ります。

降(お)りる takes を for the thing you are leaving - the bus - while the place where this happens is marked with で. Note that getting on would flip to に.

を · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 私(わたし)は新(あたら)しいパソコンをほしいです。

✅ 私(わたし)は新(あたら)しいパソコンがほしいです。

ほしい is an adjective, not a verb - it describes a state of wanting, and the thing wanted is its subject. That puts it in the が group along with わかる, 好(す)き and 見(み)える.

を · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 公園(こうえん)を歩(ある)きました。 (b) 公園(こうえん)で歩(ある)きました。

(a) uses the "path" を: the park is the ground you covered, so the picture is of walking along its paths, moving through and across it. This is the natural way to describe a walk in the park.

(b) makes the park merely the location of the activity. It is not ungrammatical, but it sounds like you went to the park specifically in order to do some walking there - as exercise, say - rather than strolling through it. For ordinary "I walked in the park", (a) is what a Japanese speaker will produce.

ね

Meaning · invites the listener to agree - "…, isn't it?", "…, right?"

ね at the end of a sentence hands the sentence to the other person. It says: you know this too, don't you. That is why it warms a conversation up - and why it sounds wrong the moment you attach it to something only you could know.

ね · Formation

ね goes at the very end, after everything else, including よ.

Attaches to Form Example
Polite sentence ～です／～ます + ね いいです → いいですね
Plain sentence (verb / い-adj) plain + ね おいしい → おいしいね
Noun / な-adjective (plain) + だ + ね 元気(げんき) → 元気(げんき)だね
Request ～てください + ね 待(ま)ってください → 待(ま)ってくださいね
Mid-sentence phrase + ね (filler) あのね、昨日(きのう)ね…
Combined with よ よ + ね (never ねよ) そうだ → そうだよね
Lengthened ね → ねえ／ねー (casual) そうだねえ

ね · Formation (cont.)

Note - after a plain noun or な-adjective, men typically keep the だ (「元気(げんき)だね」) while women often drop it (「元気(げんき)ね」). Both are normal; the version without だ sounds softer and is more marked as feminine.

ね · Examples

いい天気(てんき)ですね。
Lovely weather, isn't it.

この料理(りょうり)、おいしいね。
This food is good, isn't it.

明日(あした)三時(さんじ)ですね。
Three o'clock tomorrow, right?

ちょっと待(ま)っていてね。
Hang on here a moment, okay?

それは大変(たいへん)でしたね。
That must have been rough.

ね · Examples (cont.)

あのね、話(はなし)があるんだけど。
Listen - there's something I want to talk to you about.

昨日(きのう)の試験(しけん)、思(おも)ったより難(むずか)しかったですね。
Yesterday's exam was harder than we expected, wasn't it.

すみません、この書類(しょるい)は金曜日(きんようび)までに出(だ)せばいいんですね。
Sorry - so I just need to hand this in by Friday, is that right?

ね · Nuance & register

ね works at every politeness level: 「そうですね」 to a client, 「そうだね」 to a friend. What it is sensitive to is not formality but shared ground. ね claims that the listener already knows or feels what you are saying, so it belongs on weather, on food you are both eating, on a fact you are checking. It does not belong on information only you have. 「わたしは田中(たなか)ですね」 is strange for the same reason English 「I'm Tanaka, isn't it?」 is strange.

Intonation splits ね into two uses that learners should keep apart:

  • Falling ね shares a feeling. 「暑(あつ)いですね。」 = "Hot, isn't it." You
    are not asking; you are including the other person.
  • Rising ね? checks a fact. 「明日(あした)三時(さんじ)ですね?」 = "Three
    o'clock tomorrow, right?" You are fairly sure and want confirmation.

ね · Nuance & register (cont.)

ね also softens. Attached to a request it takes the edge off (「気(き)をつけてね」), and it is the standard way adults speak to children. That same softness is what makes overuse a real risk. A speaker who ends every sentence with ね sounds as though they cannot commit to anything without approval - needy, or oddly ingratiating. Native speakers use it a lot, but interleaved with plain endings and with よ; a solid wall of ね is a learner tell.

Two register cautions. In business, 「そうですね…」 with a long vowel is a thinking noise ("well, let me see…") and often precedes a polite disagreement - do not hear it as agreement. And with a superior, ね on something that is their business can sound presumptuous: telling your boss 「忙(いそが)しいですね」 assumes you know how their week is going.

ね · Nuance & register (cont.)

Finally, the feminine variants. 「～わね」「～のね」 are marked as women's speech and now sound somewhat traditional; 「～だね」「～だよね」 are neutral and used by everybody. ねえ lengthened at the start of a sentence is a casual "hey".

ね · ね vs よ

Rule of thumb - ね = "you know this too". よ = "you don't know this, so I'm telling you".

This is the central axis of Japanese sentence-final particles, and getting it wrong is what makes learners sound blunt.

この店(みせ)、おいしいですね。
This place is good, isn't it. (we're both eating here)

この店(みせ)、おいしいですよ。
This place is good - you should try it. (you haven't been)

Swap them and both sentences misfire. ね to someone who has never been to the restaurant assumes knowledge they do not have; よ to the person sitting opposite you eating the same meal sounds like you are correcting them.

ね · ね vs よ (cont.)

よね combines the two, in that order and never ❌ねよ. It means "I'm telling you this, but check me": you supply the information and simultaneously ask for confirmation.

明日(あした)の集合(しゅうごう)は九時(くじ)だよね。
The meeting time tomorrow is nine, right?

Use よね when you believe something but want the other person to confirm it - it is softer than a bare よ and more committed than a bare ね. It is extremely common in everyday speech.

ね · ね vs か

Rule of thumb - ね checks something you already believe. か asks about something you do not know.

会議(かいぎ)は二時(にじ)からですね。
The meeting starts at two, doesn't it.

会議(かいぎ)は何時(なんじ)からですか。
What time does the meeting start?

The ね version says you have the information and want it confirmed; you are expecting "はい". The か version says you have nothing and are asking to be told. Using か when you already know the answer sounds like a test; using ね when you genuinely have no idea puts the listener in the odd position of confirming something you never knew.

ね · ね vs でしょう

Rule of thumb - ね invites agreement gently. でしょう? presses for it.

きれいな景色(けしき)ですね。
Beautiful view, isn't it.

きれいな景色(けしき)でしょう?
Beautiful view, right? - told you.

Rising でしょう? is used when you brought the listener here, recommended the film, or predicted the outcome: it asks them to confirm that you were right. That makes it warm among friends and slightly triumphant. ね claims no such credit. Note also that falling でしょう is a different thing entirely - a guess, "probably" - so intonation matters as much as it does with ね itself.

ね · Common mistakes

  • ❌ はじめまして。田中(たなか)ですね。 → ✅ はじめまして。田中(たなか)です。
    Your own name is not shared knowledge. ね on information only you have sounds as though you are asking the listener to confirm who you are.

  • ❌ ちょっと頭(あたま)が痛(いた)いですね。 → ✅ ちょっと頭(あたま)が痛(いた)いです。
    Nobody else can feel your headache. Use ね for the shared world - weather, food, the room you are both in.

  • ❌ 「学生(がくせい)ですか。」「はい、そうですね。」 → ✅ 「はい、そうです。」
    「そうですね」 is agreement with someone else's opinion, or a thinking noise. As an answer about yourself it sounds evasive.

  • ❌ そうだねよ。 → ✅ そうだよね。
    The order is fixed: よ first, ね last. ね is always the final particle.

ね · Practice 1

1. （on the phone, confirming）明日(あした)の会議(かいぎ)は十時(じゅうじ)から____。

十時(じゅうじ)からですね。

You already believe the meeting is at ten and want it confirmed, which is exactly ね's job. Rising intonation makes the checking function clear.

ね · Practice 2

2. Choose: your friend has never been to this café, and you have. このケーキ、おいしいです（ね・よ）。

このケーキ、おいしいですよ。

The friend has no experience of the cake, so there is nothing to agree with yet - you are supplying new information, which is よ. ね would wrongly assume they already know.

ね · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese, casually: "It's Monday tomorrow, right?"

明日(あした)、月曜日(げつようび)だよね。

You believe it is Monday but want a check, which is precisely よね - the よ supplies your belief and the ね asks for confirmation. A bare ね would work too, but sounds more like idle small talk than a real check.

ね · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 昨日(きのう)、わたしは京都(きょうと)へ行(い)きましたね。

✅ 昨日(きのう)、わたしは京都(きょうと)へ行(い)きましたよ。

Where you went yesterday is your own information, so there is nothing for the listener to agree with. よ presents it as news. ね would only work if the listener had been there with you.

ね · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 寒(さむ)いですね。 (b) 寒(さむ)いですよ。

(a) assumes you are both in the same cold - standing at the same bus stop, say. It is a piece of social glue, and the expected reply is 「そうですね」.

(b) informs the listener of something they have not felt yet: you have just come in from outside, or they are about to leave without a coat. Said to someone standing next to you in the same wind, it sounds like a correction.

よ

Meaning · tells the listener something they don't yet know

よ at the end of a sentence marks the content as news. It says: this is information you didn't have, and I'm giving it to you. Used well it is helpful and warm; used on something the listener already knows, it turns into a lecture.

よ · Formation

よ comes after the finished sentence, and before ね if both are present.

Attaches to Form Example
Polite sentence ～です／～ます + よ あります → ありますよ
Plain sentence (verb / い-adj) plain + よ 安(やす)い → 安(やす)いよ
Noun / な-adjective (plain) + だ + よ 休(やす)み → 休(やす)みだよ
Negative ～ない／～ません + よ 違(ちが)います → 違(ちが)いますよ
Request ～てください + よ (pushy) 待(ま)ってください → 待(ま)ってくださいよ
Combined with ね よ + ね (never ねよ) そうだ → そうだよね
Feminine variant Noun／な-adj + よ, no だ そう → そうよ

よ · Formation (cont.)

Note - the だ row is where register lives. 「そうだよ」 is neutral to masculine; 「そうよ」 without the だ is marked as women's speech and sounds somewhat traditional today. In polite speech the question does not arise, because です is always there: 「そうですよ」.

よ · Examples

あ、財布(さいふ)が落(お)ちましたよ。
Excuse me - you've dropped your wallet.

その店(みせ)、もう閉(し)まってるよ。
That shop's already closed.

大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)ですよ。心配(しんぱい)しないでください。
It's fine, really. Don't worry.

危(あぶ)ないよ!
Careful!

会議(かいぎ)は来週(らいしゅう)の火曜日(かようび)ですよ。
The meeting is next Tuesday, actually.

よ · Examples (cont.)

本当(ほんとう)に見(み)たよ。うそじゃない。
I really did see it. I'm not making it up.

電車(でんしゃ)より地下鉄(ちかてつ)のほうが早(はや)く着(つ)きますよ。
The subway will get you there faster than the train.

このお店(みせ)のケーキ、とてもおいしいから、一度(いちど)食(た)べてみるといいですよ。
The cake here is really good, so you ought to try it sometime.

よ · Nuance & register

よ attaches at any politeness level, and by itself it is neither casual nor formal: 「ありますよ」 is perfectly polite. What よ does carry is an attitude, and that is where it gets dangerous.

Because よ claims the listener does not know something, using it on information they obviously do have sounds condescending - the Japanese equivalent of "just so you know". 「今日(きょう)は月曜日(げつようび)ですよ」 said to a colleague who has been at work all morning is not helpful; it is a small correction. The higher the listener's status, the worse this lands. Telling your boss 「その資料(しりょう)は 机(つくえ)の上(うえ)にありますよ」 is fine if they were looking for it, and faintly impertinent if they were not.

よ · Nuance & register (cont.)

よ also sharpens under stress. On a negative or a correction it can sting: 「違(ちが)いますよ」 is a good deal harder than 「違(ちが)います」. On a request it turns into impatience: 「早(はや)くしてくださいよ」 is a complaint, not a polite ask. And with the plain imperative - 「早(はや)くしろよ」 - it is straightforwardly rough male speech.

The friendly uses are just as real. よ reassures (「大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)ですよ」), warns (「危(あぶ)ないよ」), offers (「あちらですよ」) and recommends (「おいしいですよ」). In all of these you genuinely have information the other person lacks, which is exactly the condition よ wants.

Intonation matters. Rising よ is light and helpful; a flat or falling よ is firm and can be read as insistent. A learner unsure of the intonation is safer leaving よ off entirely - a bare です never offends.

よ · Nuance & register (cont.)

Finally, よね: よ and ね in that order, never ❌ねよ. It offers information and asks for confirmation - "it's this, isn't it?" It is softer than よ alone, because you are leaving the listener room to correct you, and it is one of the most common endings in real conversation.

よ · よ vs ね

Rule of thumb - よ gives information the listener lacks. ね assumes the listener already has it.

田中(たなか)さんはもう帰(かえ)りましたよ。
Tanaka's already gone home. (you didn't know)

田中(たなか)さんはもう帰(かえ)りましたね。
Tanaka's gone home, hasn't he. (we both saw him leave)

The information is identical; only the assumption about the listener changes. Get it backwards and the effect is immediate: よ on shared knowledge sounds like a correction, and ね on private knowledge leaves the listener unable to answer.

よ · よ vs ね (cont.)

A quick test before you speak: does this person already know? If yes, ね or nothing. If no, よ.

よ · よ vs よね

Rule of thumb - よ asserts. よね asserts and asks you to confirm.

この漢字(かんじ)、「みち」と読(よ)みますよ。
This character is read "michi".

この漢字(かんじ)、「みち」と読(よ)みますよね。
This character is read "michi", isn't it?

The first is confident: you know, they don't, end of story. The second hands the listener a way out - you are fairly sure, but you would like a second opinion. よね is therefore much safer when talking to someone who might know better than you, and it is what you should reach for when correcting a superior.

よ · よ vs よね (cont.)

Note that よね is not the same as a bare ね. ね alone would assume the listener already had the reading in mind; よね says you are supplying it and checking at the same time.

よ · よ vs saying nothing

Rule of thumb - a sentence with no final particle is neutral. Adding よ pushes it at the listener.

Learners tend to treat よ as decoration and sprinkle it everywhere. It is not neutral, and the plain sentence is very often the better choice.

明日(あした)は休(やす)みです。
Tomorrow is a day off.

明日(あした)は休(やす)みですよ。
Tomorrow's a day off, you know.

よ · よ vs saying nothing (cont.)

The first states a fact - appropriate in a report, an announcement, or an answer to a direct question. The second addresses the listener personally and implies they were unaware, which is friendly if true and slightly pointed if not.

In writing, よ almost never appears outside dialogue and messages between friends; it is a spoken-language particle. And when answering a question you were just asked, the bare form is usually right - the questioner already knows they didn't know.

よ · Common mistakes

  • ❌ そうですねよ。 → ✅ そうですよね。
    The order is fixed. よ comes first, ね is always last.

  • ❌ 「今日(きょう)、金曜日(きんようび)ですよ。」 said to someone who plainly knows. → ✅ 「今日(きょう)、金曜日(きんようび)ですね。」
    よ claims the listener is missing the information. On shared knowledge it reads as a correction.

  • ❌ （to your manager）その仕事(しごと)はもう終(お)わりましたよ。 → ✅ もう終(お)わりました。
    Answering a superior's question with よ can sound flippant, as though the answer should have been obvious. The plain polite form is safer.

  • ❌ 学生(がくせい)よ。（male speaker） → ✅ 学生(がくせい)だよ。
    After a plain noun, よ needs だ in front of it. Dropping the だ is a marked, feminine-sounding pattern, not a neutral one.

よ · Practice 1

1. （your friend is about to leave without their umbrella）あ、傘(かさ)、忘(わす)れています____。

傘(かさ)、忘(わす)れていますよ。

They have not noticed, so you are supplying genuinely new information - the prototypical use of よ. Rising intonation keeps it helpful rather than scolding.

よ · Practice 2

2. Choose: you and a colleague both watched it rain all morning. よく降(ふ)ります（よ・ね）。

よく降(ふ)りますね。

The rain is shared experience, so there is nothing to inform anyone of. よ here would sound as though you were correcting a colleague who had somehow failed to notice the weather.

よ · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "Don't worry - there's still plenty of time."

心配(しんぱい)しないでください。まだ時間(じかん)がありますよ。

The listener believes time is short, so you are correcting their picture with information they lack - a reassuring よ. Without it, 「まだ時間(じかん)があります」 is a flat statement and does much less comforting.

よ · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)よね、と先生(せんせい)が言(い)いました。（male speaker, plain style）

✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)だよね、と先生(せんせい)が言(い)いました。

A plain noun predicate needs だ before よ. Leaving it out gives the feminine pattern 「雨(あめ)よね」, which does not match the speaker described.

よ · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) こちらのほうが安(やす)いですよ。 (b) こちらのほうが安(やす)いですよね。

(a) is you informing the listener: you know this one is cheaper and they do not. Said by a shop assistant it is helpful; said to someone who has already compared the prices, it sounds like a correction.

(b) softens the same claim into a check. You still believe this one is cheaper, but the ね invites the listener to confirm or contradict you. That makes it the safer choice when the other person may know the prices better than you do.
