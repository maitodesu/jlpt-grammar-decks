～(よ)うとする - try to

Meaning · make a move to do something - the attempt itself, with the result left hanging

The volitional form carries the will; とする turns that will into a physical move. ～(よ)うとする photographs the effort, not the outcome - and Japanese only bothers to photograph the effort when the outcome was not the obvious one. That is why so many of these sentences end in が or けれど.

～(よ)うとする - try to · Formation

The volitional is frozen. する at the end is an ordinary verb and carries all the tense and politeness for the whole phrase.

～(よ)うとする - try to · Nuance & register

The single most useful fact: in the past tense, the attempt normally failed. If you tried and got there, Japanese reports the arrival - 窓(まど)を閉(し)めました - and says nothing about trying. So 閉(し)めようとしました tells your listener the window is still open. English "I tried to" is often modest understatement for success; this pattern is not.

The て-form is where the pattern earns its keep at N3. ～(よ)うとして plus a following clause says the attempt is what caused what came next: 拾(ひろ)おうとして指(ゆび)を切(き)った - the cutting happened in the course of the reaching. This is the natural way to narrate a small accident, and it is far more common in speech than the textbook ～(よ)うとしたが.

The subject has to have a will. That is what separates this sense from the "about to" sense, where a typhoon or a sunset can be the subject. Here, only a creature that can decide something gets to try.

～(よ)うとする - try to · Nuance & register (cont.)

For your own standing effort the plain non-past is wrong: ❌毎日(まいにち)歩(ある)こうとしています. A policy you are keeping up is ～ようにしている. The plain non-past ～(よ)うとする is comfortable in general statements about people - 人(ひと)は楽(らく)な道(みち)を選(えら)ぼうとする - where it describes a tendency rather than one person's morning.

Register is neutral; する takes whatever politeness the sentence needs. In very casual speech the whole thing is often replaced by ～(し)かけた or by 一応(いちおう)やってみた, so if you hear 開(あ)けようとしたんだけど in conversation, note that it already sounds a shade more deliberate than the alternatives.

～(よ)うとする - try to · ～(よ)うとする vs ～ようと思(おも)う

Rule of thumb - ～ようと思(おも)う happens in your head; ～(よ)うとする happens with your hands. The first is a decision, the second is a movement.

来年(らいねん)、車(くるま)を買(か)おうと思(おも)っています。
I'm thinking of buying a car next year.

車(くるま)を買(か)おうとしたら、貯金(ちょきん)が足(た)りませんでした。
I went to buy a car and found I didn't have enough saved.

The first sentence is compatible with never going near a dealership. The second one puts you in front of the price tag. This is also why ～ようと思(おも)う is the polite way to announce a plan and ～(よ)うとする is not: telling your boss 辞(や)めようとしています sounds like you are halfway out of the building.

～(よ)うとする - try to · ～(よ)うとする vs ～てみる

Rule of thumb - both are "try", but they doubt different things. ～(よ)うとする doubts whether the action can happen at all. ～てみる takes the action for granted and doubts what it will be like.

新(あたら)しいアプリを使(つか)ってみましたが、前(まえ)のほうが便利(べんり)でした。
I gave the new app a go, but the old one was handier.

新(あたら)しいアプリを使(つか)おうとしましたが、登録(とうろく)できませんでした。
I tried to use the new app, but I couldn't even register.

In the first the app ran. In the second it never opened. Because they are about different things, they stack quite happily: 使(つか)ってみようとした - "I made an attempt at giving it a go."

～(よ)うとする - try to · ～(よ)うとする vs ～ようにする

Rule of thumb - ～(よ)うとする is one moment of trying. ～ようにする is a rule you have set yourself and are keeping to.

エレベーターを使(つか)わないようにしています。
I make a point of not using the lift.

階段(かいだん)を上(のぼ)ろうとしましたが、足(あし)が痛(いた)くて無理(むり)でした。
I tried to go up the stairs, but my leg hurt too much.

Anything with 毎日(まいにち), 最近(さいきん) or なるべく in it wants ～ようにする. Anything with その時(とき) in it wants ～(よ)うとする.

～(よ)うとする - try to · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 鍵(かぎ)を探(さが)すとした → ✅ 鍵(かぎ)を探(さが)そうとした
    とする here attaches to the volitional, not the dictionary form. Dictionary form + とする exists, but it is a separate written pattern meaning "supposing that", and a learner who mixes them will be misread.

  • ❌ 荷物(にもつ)を運(はこ)びましょうとしました → ✅ 荷物(にもつ)を運(はこ)ぼうとしました
    Only the final する gets the polite ending. The volitional inside stays plain - ましょう never appears in front of と.

  • ❌ その映画(えいが)を見(み)ようとしました。とても面白(おもしろ)かったです。 → ✅ その映画(えいが)を見(み)てみました。
    You watched it and formed an opinion, so the attempt succeeded. A successful "try it and see" is てみる.

～(よ)うとする - try to · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 夜(よる)十一時(じゅういちじ)までに寝(ね)ようとしています → ✅ 夜(よる)十一時(じゅういちじ)までに寝(ね)るようにしています
    A bedtime you are trying to keep is a standing policy, not a single lunge at the duvet.

  • ❌ 台風(たいふう)が島(しま)に近(ちか)づこうとしました → ✅ 台風(たいふう)が島(しま)に近(ちか)づこうとしていました
    A typhoon cannot attempt anything. With a subject that has no will, only the "on the verge of" reading is available, and that reading needs ている.

～(よ)うとする - try to · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 窓(まど)を開(あ)けてみました。 (b) 窓(まど)を開(あ)けようとしました。

(a) says the window opened. てみる is "do it and see what happens", so the interesting part is what followed - a breeze, a noise from the street, the smell of rain. The action itself was never in doubt.

(b) says the window did not open, or at least that something interrupted you. The interesting part is the obstacle, and a listener will wait for the が clause that explains it. Said with no follow-up, (b) sounds unfinished.

～(よ)うとする - be about to

Meaning · on the very point of happening - the instant before the event tips over

Same three syllables as the "try to" pattern, but a different job. Here nobody is trying anything: ～(よ)うとしている freezes the frame just before a change completes. The winter that has not quite ended, the doors that have not quite shut. Because no will is involved, the subject can be a season, a train or a storm.

～(よ)うとする - be about to · Formation

This sense almost always wears ている or ていた, or else feeds into とき or ところ. The bare non-past ～(よ)うとする does not carry the "about to" reading.

Just when I was on the point of quitting, my manager raised the idea of a transfer.

～(よ)うとする - be about to · Nuance & register

The reliable test for which sense you are in is the subject. If it could not possibly decide anything - a season, a candle, a train, a century - you are in the "about to" sense, and no amount of context will make it mean "tried to". With a person, the form is genuinely ambiguous, and this is harmless: both readings point at the same instant, and the sentence is normally interrupted by something anyway.

The register is narrative. This pattern belongs to news copy, novels, essays and the sort of speech that is consciously describing a scene. In ordinary conversation people reach for もうすぐ, ところ or ～そう: もうすぐ着(つ)くよ, not 着(つ)こうとしているよ. Saying ご飯(はん)が炊(た)けようとしています to a housemate sounds like you are narrating a documentary about your rice cooker.

今(いま)にも pairs with it naturally and raises the tension a notch: 今(いま)にも倒(たお)れようとしている. So does まさに in written Japanese.

～(よ)うとする - be about to · Nuance & register (cont.)

One boundary worth marking: this is about a moment, not about the near future. Next week's roadworks are not 始(はじ)まろうとしています; they simply 始(はじ)まります. If you can attach a date to it, you have left the pattern's territory.

Finally, the ところ frame is the most conversational corner of this sense. ～(よ)うとしたところに plus an interruption - a phone call, a visitor, rain - is a small, very common storytelling move, and it survives in casual speech where the plain ている form would sound stiff.

～(よ)うとする - be about to · ～(よ)うとしている vs ～ところだ

Rule of thumb - ところだ needs a subject with a schedule; it is the neutral "I'm just about to". ～(よ)うとしている is a camera angle, and it works on things that never planned anything.

今(いま)から出(で)かけるところです。
I'm just heading out now.

雪(ゆき)が屋根(やね)から落(お)ちようとしている。
The snow is on the point of sliding off the roof.

Swap them and both go wrong. Snow has no ところ of its own to be standing at, and narrating your own departure with 出(で)かけようとしています makes you sound like a character in a film rather than a person telling a friend where you are.

～(よ)うとする - be about to · ～(よ)うとしている vs ～かけている

Rule of thumb - ～(よ)うとしている means it has not started. ～かけている means it started and stopped partway.

ろうそくの火(ひ)が消(き)えようとしている。
The candle is about to go out.

ろうそくの火(ひ)が消(き)えかけている。
The candle is guttering out.

The first candle is still burning steadily; you can see that it has almost no wax left. The second is already halfway gone - the flame is failing as you watch. The same split explains 読(よ)みかけの本(ほん) (a book you are partway through) against 読(よ)もうとしている本(ほん) (one you have not opened).

～(よ)うとしている vs 今(いま)にも～そうだ

Rule of thumb - both mean "any second now", but ～そうだ is a guess read off the surface of something, while ～(よ)うとしている reports the event as genuinely underway.

棚(たな)の箱(はこ)が落(お)ちそうです。
That box on the shelf looks like it's going to fall.

棚(たな)の箱(はこ)が落(お)ちようとしています。
The box on the shelf is on the point of falling.

The first is your inference from how the box is sitting; it may sit there all year. The second commits: the box is going. In warnings and in speech, そうだ is almost always what you want - it is the one that leaves you room to be wrong.

～(よ)うとする - be about to · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 月(つき)が出(で)ようとする。 → ✅ 月(つき)が出(で)ようとしている。
    The "about to" reading lives in ている. Without it, the plain non-past reads as a general statement about attempting, which the moon cannot do.

  • ❌ バスが来(き)ようとしている。 → ✅ バスが来(こ)ようとしている。
    The volitional of 来(く)る is こよう, not きよう. The ます-stem reading き belongs to 来(き)ます and nowhere near this pattern.

  • ❌ 来週(らいしゅう)、駅前(えきまえ)の工事(こうじ)が始(はじ)まろうとしています。 → ✅ 来週(らいしゅう)、駅前(えきまえ)の工事(こうじ)が始(はじ)まります。
    A dated future event is just the plain non-past. This pattern is for the second before something happens, not for next week.

～(よ)うとする - be about to · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ もうすぐ映画(えいが)を見(み)ようとしています。 → ✅ もうすぐ映画(えいが)を見(み)るところです。
    About yourself, with a plan, ところだ is the natural form. ～(よ)うとしている here narrates you from outside, which is why it sounds oddly grand.

  • ❌ 会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わろうとしましたとき → ✅ 会議(かいぎ)が終(お)わろうとしたとき
    A clause in front of とき takes the plain form. Politeness goes on the end of the sentence, not inside it.

～(よ)うとする - be about to · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 花(はな)が散(ち)ろうとしている。 (b) 花(はな)が散(ち)りかけている。

(a) says none of the petals have come down yet, but the moment is here - the tree is at its last full day. It is a narrator's sentence, the kind that opens a paragraph.

(b) says the falling has already begun and is partway through: petals on the ground, gaps in the branches. かける is about an interrupted or incomplete process, so it reports a state you can photograph, while (a) reports a threshold about to be crossed.

～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない

Meaning · won't even try - the person is unwilling, and the speaker is not pleased about it

Negating the attempt does something the plain negative cannot: it puts the other person's will in the frame. 食(た)べない reports that no eating is happening. 食(た)べようとしない says they have decided against it, and you can hear the speaker's patience running out.

Strongest - "didn't even" ～(よ)うともしない 認(みと)めようともしない

～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - the negative goes on する at the end, never on the verb inside. ❌認(みと)めまいとする is not a variant of this pattern; the shape is always positive volitional + としない.

～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Examples

息子(むすこ)は部屋(へや)から出(で)ようとしない。
My son won't come out of his room.

誰(だれ)も席(せき)を立(た)とうとしなかった。
Nobody would get up from their seat.

その犬(いぬ)は知(し)らない人(ひと)に近(ちか)づこうとしない。
That dog won't go anywhere near strangers.

いくら謝(あやま)っても、彼女(かのじょ)は顔(かお)を上(あ)げようとはしなかった。
However much I apologised, she would not so much as look up.

同僚(どうりょう)は自分(じぶん)のミスを認(みと)めようとしません。
My colleague refuses to admit his own mistakes.

～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Examples (cont.)

祖母(そぼ)は足(あし)が痛(いた)いのに、病院(びょういん)へ行(い)こうとしないんです。
My grandmother's leg hurts, and still she won't go to the hospital.

市役所(しやくしょ)は住民(じゅうみん)の質問(しつもん)に答(こた)えようともしなかった。
The city office would not even attempt to answer the residents' questions.

話(はな)し合(あ)いは三時間(さんじかん)続(つづ)きましたが、どちらの側(がわ)も一歩(いっぽ)もゆずろうとしませんでした。
The talks ran for three hours, but neither side would give an inch.

新(あたら)しいやり方(かた)をどれだけ説明(せつめい)しても、ベテランの職人(しょくにん)たちは耳(みみ)を貸(か)そうとしなかった。
No matter how thoroughly the new method was explained, the veteran craftsmen wouldn't hear of it.

～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Nuance & register

This pattern is a complaint, and that is not a side effect - it is the reason the pattern exists. Using it means asserting that you know what is going on inside someone else's head. That makes it comfortable about children, animals, machines and institutions, awkward about equals, and genuinely risky about people you owe deference to. 部長(ぶちょう)は決(き)めようとしません is not a neutral status report; it is an accusation of obstruction, and it will be heard as one.

Unlike the "try to" sense, the non-past is completely natural here, because it describes a standing state of refusal rather than one moment of effort. 食(た)べようとする about a habit is odd; 食(た)べようとしない about a habit is the normal thing to say.

The three degrees are worth learning as a set:

～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Nuance & register (cont.)

  • ～(よ)うとしない - plain report of unwillingness.
  • ～(よ)うとはしない - the は sets this action against everything
    else the person did do: that one thing, they would not do. More pointed, and commoner in writing.
  • ～(よ)うともしない - "would not so much as". The most dismissive of
    the three, and often paired with どれだけ, いくら or 一度(いちど)も.

Applied to objects, the pattern is a deliberate personification and reads as exasperated rather than mistaken: 車(くるま)が動(うご)こうとしない casts the car as sulking. That is a stylistic choice, not the default - a bus that is merely late is なかなか来(き)ません.

About yourself, the present tense is strange - you would simply say やりたくない. The past is fine in reflective narration: あのころの私(わたし)は誰(だれ)の話(はなし)も聞(き)こうとしなかった, looking back at your younger self as if at someone else.

～(よ)うとしない vs ～たがらない

Rule of thumb - ～たがらない says they don't want to. ～(よ)うとしない says they won't do it, whatever they may want.

弟(おとうと)は写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)られたがりません。
My little brother doesn't like having his photo taken.

弟(おとうと)は写真(しゃしん)の前(まえ)に立(た)とうとしません。
My little brother won't stand in front of the camera.

たがる reports a desire you have inferred from the outside; the sentence is about his feelings. ～(よ)うとしない reports behaviour, and it works even where desire is irrelevant - someone may badly want to apologise and still 謝(あやま)ろうとしない. That gap is the useful part: it is how you describe pride, fear or stubbornness overriding what a person actually wants.

～(よ)うとしない vs ～てくれない

Rule of thumb - ～(よ)うとしない criticises the other person's attitude. ～てくれない complains that you did not get what you needed.

店員(てんいん)が説明(せつめい)してくれなかった。
The assistant didn't explain it to me.

店員(てんいん)が説明(せつめい)しようとしなかった。
The assistant wouldn't even try to explain.

The first is about the loss to you; the assistant might have been busy or new. The second is about the assistant's unwillingness. Stacking them makes the strongest version of the complaint: 説明(せつめい)しようとしてくれなかった - they would not make the effort for me.

～(よ)うとしない vs ～(よ)うとはしない vs ～(よ)うともしない

Rule of thumb - bare と states it, とは contrasts it against what they did do, とも says they did not even get as far as starting.

彼(かれ)は理由(りゆう)を話(はな)そうとしなかった。
He wouldn't tell me why.

彼(かれ)は言(い)い訳(わけ)はしたが、理由(りゆう)を話(はな)そうとはしなかった。
He made excuses, but the actual reason he would not give.

彼(かれ)は理由(りゆう)を話(はな)そうともしなかった。
He didn't even attempt to tell me why.

～(よ)うとしない vs ～(よ)うとはしない vs ～(よ)うともしない (cont.)

The middle sentence needs the first clause to earn its は; without something to contrast against, とはしない can sound as though a comparison has gone missing. The third needs no setup at all - も is doing the work of "not even" by itself.

～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 子(こ)どもが靴(くつ)を履(は)くとしない → ✅ 子(こ)どもが靴(くつ)を履(は)こうとしない
    The dictionary form never appears here. It is volitional + としない, so 履(は)く becomes 履(は)こう.

  • ❌ 私(わたし)は朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べようとしません。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べません。
    About your own present habit, the pattern is odd - it makes you sound like a bystander disapproving of yourself. Use the plain negative, or 食(た)べたくないんです if you want the reason in.

  • ❌ バスがなかなか来(こ)ようとしません。 → ✅ バスがなかなか来(き)ません。
    A late bus is not refusing anything. Reserve the pattern for subjects with a will, or for a personification you actually mean.

～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌（to your manager）部長(ぶちょう)はメールに返事(へんじ)をしようとしませんね。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、メールの件(けん)、まだお返事(へんじ)をいただいていないのですが。
    Said to the person themselves, this accuses them of deliberate obstruction. When the subject is someone you must be polite to, drop the pattern entirely and report the fact.

～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Practice 1

1. 赤(あか)ちゃんが眠(ねむ)____としないので、母(はは)は一晩中(ひとばんじゅう)だっこしていた。

赤(あか)ちゃんが眠(ねむ)ろうとしないので…

眠(ねむ)る is a う-verb, so the volitional is 眠(ねむ)ろう. The pattern fits a baby perfectly: it is exactly the sentence of a parent who has decided the child is doing this on purpose.

～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Practice 2

2. Choose: 彼(かれ)は人前(ひとまえ)で歌(うた)い（たがりません・ようとしません）。カラオケでもずっとすみに座(すわ)っています。

Both are possible, and they say different things. 歌(うた)いたがりません reports that he has no desire to sing, which fits someone quietly sitting in the corner. 歌(うた)おうとしません would add that people have been pushing him and he is holding out. Given that the second sentence only describes him sitting there, 歌(うた)いたがりません is the more natural fit.

～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "I offered several times, but he wouldn't take the money."

何度(なんど)もすすめましたが、彼(かれ)はお金(かね)を受(う)け取(と)ろうとしませんでした。

The point of the sentence is that he could have taken it and chose not to - that is ～(よ)うとしない rather than a plain 受(う)け取(と)りませんでした, which would merely say no money changed hands.

～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 雨(あめ)がやもうとしないので、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になりました。

✅ 雨(あめ)がやまないので、試合(しあい)は中止(ちゅうし)になりました。

Rain has no will, so it cannot refuse to stop. The plain negative is what you want. やもうとしない is possible only as a conscious personification in literary writing - in a report of a cancelled match it just reads as an error.

～(よ)うとしない・～(よ)うとはしない · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 彼女(かのじょ)はこちらを見(み)なかった。 (b) 彼女(かのじょ)はこちらを見(み)ようともしなかった。

(a) records that her eyes did not come this way. She may not have known you were there; the sentence takes no view on why.

(b) says she knew perfectly well and would not even turn her head. とも adds "not even", so the sentence measures her refusal against the smallest possible effort and finds she did not make it. It is the harshest of the three degrees, and it tells you as much about the speaker's feelings as about hers.

～べき

Meaning · ought to; is the right thing to do - measured against a principle, not against convenience

べき passes a judgement. It says the action is correct - morally, socially, professionally - and it does so with the weight of a standard behind it. That is what separates it from ほうがいい, which merely says the action would work out better for you.

～べき · Formation

べき is a survival from classical Japanese, which is why it attaches to the dictionary form and why する has two shapes.

～べき · Formation (cont.)

Attaches to Form Example
Verb dictionary form + べきだ 守(まも)る → 守(まも)るべきだ
Verb (する) するべき / すべき 報告(ほうこく)する → 報告(ほうこく)するべきだ・報告(ほうこく)すべきだ
Verb (来(く)る) 来(く)るべき 来(く)る → 来(く)るべきだ

Regret about the past ～べきだった 言(い)う → 言(い)うべきだった
Polite ～べきです・べきでした 守(まも)るべきです
Noun / な-adjective (written) ～である + べき 公平(こうへい) → 公平(こうへい)であるべきだ

～べき · Formation (cont.)

Note - するべき and すべき mean the same thing. すべき is the older form and is preferred in writing, editorials and formal speech; するべき is easier on the ear in conversation. Neither is wrong. In noun-modifying position the だ disappears - 言(い)うべきこと, not ❌言(い)うべきだこと.

～べき · Examples

もっと野菜(やさい)を食(た)べるべきだ。
You ought to eat more vegetables.

約束(やくそく)は守(まも)るべきです。
Promises should be kept.

こういうことは、早(はや)めに上司(じょうし)に報告(ほうこく)すべきだ。
Something like this ought to be reported to your manager early.

あの時(とき)、はっきり「できません」と言(い)うべきだった。
I should have said "I can't" clearly at the time.

学生(がくせい)のうちに読(よ)むべき本(ほん)を、先生(せんせい)が紹介(しょうかい)してくださいました。
My teacher recommended some books one ought to read while still a student.

～べき · Examples (cont.)

会議(かいぎ)の前(まえ)に、資料(しりょう)に目(め)を通(とお)しておくべきでした。
I should have looked through the handouts before the meeting.

災害(さいがい)に備(そな)えて、水(みず)と食料(しょくりょう)を用意(ようい)しておくべきだと思(おも)います。
I think we ought to keep water and food ready in case of a disaster.

本来(ほんらい)この制度(せいど)は、一番(いちばん)困(こま)っている人(ひと)を助(たす)けるべきものです。
This system is supposed, by its very nature, to help the people in the greatest difficulty.

失敗(しっぱい)したときにどうするかを、チーム全員(ぜんいん)で決(き)めておくべきだったと後悔(こうかい)しています。
I regret that we didn't agree as a team, in advance, what to do if it went wrong.

～べき · Nuance & register

べき is heavy. It appeals to something outside the speaker - a principle, a duty, the way things are done - and that is why it turns up constantly in newspaper editorials, essays, court judgements and meeting minutes, and why it can land badly in a kitchen conversation. Told もっと野菜(やさい)を食(た)べるべきだ, a friend hears a small sermon.

Aimed at a listener, therefore, べき needs softening, and Japanese has a standard kit for it:

  • ～べきだと思(おも)います - makes it your opinion rather than the
    law.
  • ～べきではないでしょうか - a rhetorical question, the politest form
    of the same claim, very common in meetings.
  • ～べきかもしれません - hedged almost to invisibility.

～べき · Nuance & register (cont.)

Bare ～べきだ pointed at someone senior to you is close to insubordinate. Between friends it is fine if you are half-joking, and it is completely fine when the subject is you - 私(わたし)が行(い)くべきでした - or an abstraction: 政治(せいじ)は公平(こうへい)であるべきだ.

The past form is the one learners under-use. ～べきだった is the everyday way to express regret about a choice you made: 買(か)うべきだった, 聞(き)いておくべきだった. It is neither formal nor preachy in that use, because the person being judged is yourself.

Note - you will meet the same word as ～べく ("in order to") and ～べからず ("do not") on signs and in stiff writing: 立(た)ち入(い)るべからず. Both are frozen classical leftovers; recognise them, but write ～ために and ～ないでください yourself.

～べき · ～べきだ vs ～なければならない

Rule of thumb - なければならない is an obligation imposed on you: a rule, a deadline, a law. べきだ is a judgement you are making: this is the right thing.

外国人(がいこくじん)は在留(ざいりゅう)カードを持(も)ち歩(ある)かなければなりません。
Foreign residents are required to carry their residence card.

留学生(りゅうがくせい)にもっと情報(じょうほう)を出(だ)すべきです。
More information ought to be made available to international students.

The first is the law; you have no view about it, you are reporting it. The second is an argument, and someone could disagree with you. Swap them and the first sentence becomes an opinion about immigration policy, while the second sounds as if a regulation exists that you have not cited.

～べき · ～べきだ vs ～なければならない (cont.)

This also explains a common error. If the requirement is already binding, べき is the weaker and stranger choice: a visa application that must include a photo takes 入(い)れなければなりません.

～べき · ～べきだ vs ～ほうがいい

Rule of thumb - ほうがいい compares outcomes for this person, this situation. べきだ measures the action against a standard and is indifferent to whether it suits you.

熱(ねつ)があるなら、今日(きょう)は休(やす)んだほうがいいですよ。
If you've got a fever, you'd be better off taking today off.

熱(ねつ)があるのに出勤(しゅっきん)させる会社(かいしゃ)のほうが、考(かんが)えを改(あらた)めるべきです。
It's the company making people come in with a fever that ought to change its thinking.

ほうがいい is advice and is heard as kindness. べき is a verdict and is heard as criticism - which is exactly why the second sentence uses it. For everyday suggestions to a friend, ほうがいい is almost always the safer form.

～べき · ～べきだった vs ～ばよかった

Rule of thumb - both look back at a wrong choice. ばよかった is wishing; べきだった is judging yourself.

もっと早(はや)く出(で)ればよかった。
I wish I'd left earlier.

もっと早(はや)く出(で)るべきだった。
I should have left earlier.

The first is a sigh; it fits missing a nice sunset. The second accepts fault and fits missing a flight you were responsible for. ばよかった can also be used about things nobody controlled - 晴(は)れればよかったのに - where べきだった is impossible, because weather cannot be blamed.

～べき · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 教師(きょうし)は公平(こうへい)べきだ。 → ✅ 教師(きょうし)は公平(こうへい)であるべきだ。
    べき needs a verb in front of it. Nouns and な-adjectives get there through である.

  • ❌ 注意(ちゅうい)しるべき → ✅ 注意(ちゅうい)するべき・注意(ちゅうい)すべき
    する gives two legitimate forms and neither is しる. Pick すべき for writing, するべき for speech.

  • ❌ ビザの申請(しんせい)には写真(しゃしん)を入(い)れるべきです。 → ✅ ビザの申請(しんせい)には写真(しゃしん)を入(い)れなければなりません。
    A published requirement is not your judgement. Reporting a rule takes なければならない.

  • ❌（to your manager）部長(ぶちょう)、もっと休(やす)むべきです。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、少(すこ)しお休(やす)みになったほうがよろしいのではないでしょうか。
    Bare べきだ aimed upwards is a reprimand. Move to ほうがいい, or at the very least to ～べきではないでしょうか.

～べき · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 言(い)うべきだことがあります。 → ✅ 言(い)うべきことがあります。
    In front of a noun, べき stands alone. The だ only appears at the end of a sentence.

～べき · Practice 1

1. 自分(じぶん)が悪(わる)いと分(わ)かっているなら、すぐに謝(あやま)____だ。

すぐに謝(あやま)るべきだ。

べき attaches to the dictionary form, so 謝(あやま)る stays whole. The sentence is a moral judgement - apologising is the right thing - which is precisely べき territory rather than ほうがいい.

～べき · Practice 2

2. Choose: 試験(しけん)の日(ひ)は受験票(じゅけんひょう)を持(も)ってこ（なければなりません・るべきです）。

持(も)ってこなければなりません。

The exam board requires the admission slip; this is a rule being reported, not an opinion you are advancing. 持(も)ってくるべきです would suggest you personally think it would be a good idea, which understates a hard requirement.

～べき · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "I should have asked her before I decided."

決(き)める前(まえ)に、彼女(かのじょ)に聞(き)くべきでした。

～べきだった／べきでした is the standard way to say "I should have", with the fault accepted. 聞(き)けばよかった is also possible but softer - closer to "I wish I'd asked" than to "that was my mistake".

～べき · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: この問題(もんだい)については、全員(ぜんいん)で話(はな)し合(あ)うべきだことだと思(おも)います。

✅ この問題(もんだい)については、全員(ぜんいん)で話(はな)し合(あ)うべきことだと思(おも)います。

べき modifies a noun directly, with no だ in between. The だ you already have at the end of ことだ is doing the sentence-final work.

～べき · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) この映画(えいが)は見(み)たほうがいいよ。 (b) この映画(えいが)は見(み)るべきだよ。

(a) is a friendly recommendation aimed at you: you would enjoy it, so go and see it. Nothing is at stake if you don't.

(b) appeals to a standard outside both of you - the film matters, and anyone serious about films is failing in some small duty by missing it. Between close friends this comes across as enthusiastic overstatement, and that is often exactly the joke; said flatly to someone you do not know well, it sounds like being told off.

～べきではない

Meaning · ought not to; it is wrong to do this - a verdict, not a rule posted on a wall

The negative of べき keeps everything that made べき heavy. It appeals to a principle and finds the action falls short of it. Nobody is forbidding you - べきではない has no authority behind it, only judgement - which is why it feels at once softer than a prohibition and harder to argue with.

～べきではない · Formation

The negative goes on べき, never on the verb inside it.

～べきではない · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - ❌言(い)わないべきだ does not exist. Japanese negates the judgement, not the action: 言(い)うべきではない. The same is true of the past - 言(い)うべきではなかった, never ❌言(い)わないべきだった.

～べきではない · Examples

人(ひと)の日記(にっき)を読(よ)むべきではない。
You shouldn't read other people's diaries.

そんなことを言(い)うべきじゃないよ。
You shouldn't say things like that.

運転(うんてん)するなら、お酒(さけ)を飲(の)むべきではありません。
If you're driving, you shouldn't be drinking.

子(こ)どもの前(まえ)で夫婦(ふうふ)げんかをするべきではなかった。
We shouldn't have had that argument in front of the children.

一人(ひとり)の意見(いけん)だけで全部(ぜんぶ)を決(き)めるべきではないと思(おも)います。
I don't think everything should be decided on one person's opinion alone.

～べきではない · Examples (cont.)

あんな高(たか)い時計(とけい)を、給料(きゅうりょう)が入(はい)る前(まえ)に買(か)うべきではありませんでした。
I shouldn't have bought a watch that expensive before payday.

会社(かいしゃ)は社員(しゃいん)の健康(けんこう)より利益(りえき)を優先(ゆうせん)すべきではない。
A company should not put profit ahead of its employees' health.

知(し)らないことを、知(し)っているふりをして話(はな)すべきではありません。
You shouldn't talk about things you don't know as if you did.

うわさだけで人(ひと)を判断(はんだん)すべきではないというのが、祖父(そふ)の口(くち)ぐせでした。
"Never judge someone on rumour alone" was my grandfather's favourite saying.

～べきではない · Nuance & register

The force of べきではない is moral, not institutional. It is what an editorial says about a government, what a parent says about lying, what you say to yourself at three in the morning. There is no penalty attached and no authority cited; the sentence simply asserts that the action is wrong, and invites you to agree.

Because of that, aimed straight at a listener it preaches. Between close friends 言(い)うべきじゃないよ is normal and even warm - the casual contraction takes most of the weight off - but べきではありません to someone you barely know reads as a dressing-down. Softening works the same way as with the positive: put と思(おも)います on it, or move to ないほうがいい.

The past form is the everyday one. ～べきではなかった is how you own a mistake, and it carries no preachiness at all when the subject is yourself: 言(い)うべきではなかった, 信(しん)じるべきではなかった.

～べきではない · Nuance & register (cont.)

Note - one trap that catches almost everybody. ～べきではないでしょうか and ～べきではないか are rhetorical questions, and they argue for the action, not against it. もっと話(はな)し合(あ)うべきではないでしょうか means "shouldn't we talk about it more?" - that is, we should. If you want to argue against something politely, you need ～べきではないと思(おも)います.

Register is otherwise straightforward: すべきではない for writing and formal speech, するべきではない and べきじゃない for conversation. The word is at home in essays, opinion columns and meetings, and slightly stiff in small talk.

～べきではない · ～べきではない vs ～てはいけない

Rule of thumb - てはいけない is a prohibition: someone with authority says no, and there are consequences. べきではない is an opinion about right and wrong, with nobody enforcing it.

館内(かんない)では写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)ってはいけません。
Photography is not permitted inside the building.

相手(あいて)の許可(きょか)なく写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)るべきではありません。
You shouldn't photograph someone without their permission.

The first is the museum's rule; you would find it on a sign, and breaking it gets you stopped. The second is an argument about how people should behave, and it holds in places where no such sign exists. Put べきではない on a notice and it reads oddly - as if the building were expressing a hope rather than setting a rule.

～べきではない · ～べきではない vs ～てはいけない (cont.)

てはいけない also works for immediate, physical danger, where べきではない is far too abstract: 熱(あつ)いから触(さわ)ってはいけません.

～べきではない · ～べきではない vs ～ないほうがいい

Rule of thumb - ないほうがいい weighs outcomes for this person. べきではない weighs the action against a principle and does not care whether it suits you.

夜(よる)遅(おそ)くにコーヒーを飲(の)まないほうがいいですよ。
You're better off not drinking coffee late at night.

人(ひと)の失敗(しっぱい)を笑(わら)うべきではありません。
You should not laugh at other people's mistakes.

Swap them and both go wrong in an instructive way. コーヒーを飲(の)むべきではない turns caffeine into a moral failing. 笑(わら)わないほうがいい turns cruelty into a tactical error - as though the problem were that you might get caught. For advice to a friend, ないほうがいい is nearly always the right register; save べきではない for when you actually mean to make a judgement.

～べきではない · ～べきではない vs ～べきではないでしょうか

Rule of thumb - adding でしょうか flips the meaning. The statement opposes the action; the question recommends it.

この案(あん)は今(いま)決(き)めるべきではない。
We shouldn't decide this proposal now.

この案(あん)は今(いま)決(き)めるべきではないでしょうか。
Shouldn't we decide this proposal now?

The second sentence is arguing for a decision today. Japanese does this with negative questions generally - いいんじゃない? is approval, not doubt - but with べき the reversal catches learners because the words look so much like a refusal. In a meeting, hearing 見直(みなお)すべきではないでしょうか means someone wants a review, and nodding along while believing the opposite is an expensive mistake.

～べきではない · Common mistakes

  • ❌ そのことは言(い)わないべきだ。 → ✅ そのことは言(い)うべきではない。
    The negation attaches to べき, not to the verb. There is no ないべき form.

  • ❌（on a notice）ここでたばこを吸(す)うべきではありません。 → ✅ ここでたばこを吸(す)ってはいけません。
    A posted rule is a prohibition. べきではない on a sign sounds like the building is sharing its personal views.

  • ❌ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)るべきではない。 → ✅ 明日(あした)は雨(あめ)が降(ふ)らないでほしい。
    べき judges a chosen action. Weather chooses nothing, so there is nothing to judge - what you mean is a wish.

～べきではない · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌「この計画(けいかく)はやめるべきではないでしょうか」（intending "we shouldn't cancel this plan"） → ✅ この計画(けいかく)はやめるべきではないと思(おも)います。
    The でしょうか version argues for cancelling. To argue against something politely, keep the statement and soften it with と思(おも)います.

  • ❌ 課長(かちょう)、そんな言(い)い方(かた)をするべきではありません。 → ✅ 課長(かちょう)、少(すこ)し言(い)い方(かた)を変(か)えていただけないでしょうか。
    Aimed upwards, べきではない is a reprimand. Reframe it as a request rather than a verdict.

～こと - written rules and instructions

Meaning · you are to do this - the sentence-ending こと of notices, house rules and exam papers

A verb followed by a bare こと and a full stop is not a noun phrase that has lost its verb. It is a complete sentence, and it issues an instruction. Nobody in particular is being addressed: the rule simply exists, and anyone it applies to is expected to follow it.

～こと - written rules and instructions · Formation

Nothing follows the こと. No だ, no です, no 。ます. The sentence stops.

～こと - written rules and instructions · Nuance & register

This is a written form, and specifically the written form of an institution talking to whoever is covered by its rules. You meet it on classroom walls, in staff handbooks, on the front page of exam papers, in club regulations and in the list of conditions on an application form. It is often set out as a bulleted list, one こと per line.

It has no politeness level, because it has no addressee. That is worth pausing on: てください is polite because there is a person you are being polite to, whereas こと speaks past everyone to the rule itself. The effect is impersonal and slightly cold, which in a notice is exactly right.

Said to an actual human being, it is another matter. A manager who writes 明日(あした)までに出(だ)すこと in an email to one named person sounds curt and rather military. It is not ungrammatical - some workplaces use it deliberately for terse internal memos - but a learner should assume it will be heard as brusque, and write 出(だ)してください instead.

～こと - written rules and instructions · Nuance & register (cont.)

Spoken, it survives in one narrow place: a teacher or leader reading a list of rules aloud, essentially quoting the notice. Outside that, it is not conversational Japanese.

Note - the same word ends several other patterns that look identical on the page. ～ことだ gives advice, ～ことがある reports occasional events, ～ことにする announces a decision. The instruction こと is the one with nothing after it.

～こと - written rules and instructions · ～こと vs ～てください

Rule of thumb - てください asks a person; こと states a rule. If you could name who you are talking to, use てください.

すみません、この紙(かみ)にお名前(なまえ)を書(か)いてください。
Excuse me - please write your name on this form.

受付(うけつけ)で用紙(ようし)を受(う)け取(と)り、名前(なまえ)を書(か)くこと。
Collect a form at reception and write your name on it.

The first is a receptionist speaking to the person in front of her. The second is what the form itself says. Note also that てください can be softened (書(か)いていただけますか) and こと cannot - there is no polite version, because there is nobody to be polite to.

～こと vs ～ようにしてください

Rule of thumb - こと is a requirement. ～ようにしてください asks people to make an effort in that direction, and leaves room for exceptions.

会議(かいぎ)の十分(じゅっぷん)前(まえ)までに集(あつ)まること。
Assemble by ten minutes before the meeting.

なるべく早(はや)めに集(あつ)まるようにしてください。
Please try to gather a little early if you can.

The first admits no exceptions and reads as policy; the second is a request from one person to a group and expects some slippage. A written instruction in a customer-facing context - a hotel, a hospital - will usually choose ようにしてください or the still politer お願(ねが)いいたします, because こと addresses people as rule subjects rather than as guests.

～こと - written rules and instructions · ～こと vs ～なければならない

Rule of thumb - こと issues the requirement. なければならない reports that a requirement exists.

参加者(さんかしゃ)は保険(ほけん)に入(はい)ること。
Participants are to take out insurance.

参加者(さんかしゃ)は保険(ほけん)に入(はい)らなければなりません。
Participants have to take out insurance.

Both appear in regulations, and they are close enough that many documents use them interchangeably. The difference in feel: the first is the rule speaking in its own voice, and belongs in the list of conditions itself; the second is someone explaining the rule, and belongs in the covering letter or in speech. Only the second can be said aloud comfortably to an applicant on the phone.

～こと - written rules and instructions · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 教室(きょうしつ)を出(で)るときは電気(でんき)を消(け)すことです。 → ✅ 電気(でんき)を消(け)すこと。
    Adding です turns a notice into personal advice. On a wall, the bare form is what you want.

  • ❌ 辞書(じしょ)を使(つか)わなくこと → ✅ 辞書(じしょ)を使(つか)わないこと
    The negative is the plain ない-form. なく belongs to なくて and なくても, not here.

  • ❌（email to one colleague）山田(やまだ)さん、水曜日(すいようび)までに返事(へんじ)をすること。 → ✅ 山田(やまだ)さん、水曜日(すいようび)までにお返事(へんじ)をお願(ねが)いします。
    Aimed at a named person, こと reads as an order barked down a corridor. Use a request form.

  • ❌ 部屋(へや)はきれいなこと。 → ✅ 部屋(へや)はきれいにしておくこと。
    Only verbs take the instruction こと. Turn the adjective into an action first.

～ことだ

Meaning · the thing to do is… - advice offered as the obvious answer to a problem

～ことだ hands over a remedy. It does not weigh options or hedge; it names the one action that solves the problem, in the tone of somebody who has seen this situation before. That confidence is the whole flavour of the pattern, and it is also the reason it has to be used carefully.

～ことだ · Formation

Only verbs. The sentence almost always follows a clause that sets up the problem - a たら, a なら, a ければ.

If kanji won't stick, write them out by hand over and over.

If you're nervous about the interview, run through it out loud the day before.

On a day when something horrible has happened, don't force yourself to think it through - just go to bed early.

～ことだ · Nuance & register

The speaker of a ことだ sentence has taken a position: I know the answer to this. That is fine coming from a coach, a teacher, a doctor, a parent, or a friend who has been through the same thing, and it is the standard voice of advice columns and self-help writing. It is much less comfortable between strangers, and it is not appropriate aimed upwards - telling your section chief 休(やす)むことです puts you in the role of the person who knows better.

ことです is polite in form, but politeness of form does not remove the stance. If you want to advise a senior colleague, leave this pattern alone and use お休(やす)みになってはいかがでしょうか or ほうがよろしいかと思(おも)います.

The advice is general, not a request about one occasion. It answers "what should I do about X?" rather than "will you do X for me?" So 窓(まど)を開(あ)けることだ is not a way to ask someone to open a window; it is what you say when the room's stuffiness is a recurring problem.

～ことだ · Nuance & register (cont.)

You cannot use it about yourself. ❌私(わたし)は早(はや)く寝(ね)ることです is not a plan; for that you want つもりだ or ようにしている.

Note - a different ことだ turns up in exclamations: なんと静(しず)かなことだろう, 困(こま)ったことだ. That one takes adjectives and な-adjectives, sits at the end of a written or old-fashioned sentence, and expresses feeling rather than advice. Context separates them easily - advice always has an action verb in front.

～ことだ · ～ことだ vs ～ほうがいい

Rule of thumb - ほうがいい compares: this option beats that one. ことだ prescribes: this is what solves it, and no comparison is offered.

薬(くすり)を飲(の)んだほうがいいですよ。
You'd be better off taking something for it.

熱(ねつ)が下(さ)がらないなら、病院(びょういん)へ行(い)くことです。
If the fever isn't coming down, go to a doctor.

ほうがいい leaves the decision with the listener and is the gentler everyday choice - it is what you say to a friend at their desk. ことだ closes the question. That is why it fits the second sentence, where the situation has already gone past the point of weighing things up.

～ことだ · ～ことだ vs ～べきだ

Rule of thumb - ことだ is practical: here is the route to the goal you said you wanted. べきだ is moral: here is what is right, whether you want it or not.

試験(しけん)に受(う)かりたいなら、過去(かこ)問(もん)を解(と)くことだ。
If you want to pass the exam, work through past papers.

約束(やくそく)した以上(いじょう)、最後(さいご)までやるべきだ。
Having promised, you ought to see it through.

The first is conditional on your goal: no goal, no advice, and you are free to drop the exam. The second holds regardless of what you want - that is what makes it a judgement. In practice ことだ follows ～たいなら and べきだ follows ～のだから.

～ことだ · ～ことだ vs ～ものだ

Rule of thumb - ことだ advises this person about this problem. ものだ states a general truth or social norm that applies to everyone.

先輩(せんぱい)に会(あ)ったら、自分(じぶん)から挨拶(あいさつ)するものだ。
When you meet someone senior, you greet them first - that's how it's done.

職場(しょくば)になじみたいなら、自分(じぶん)から挨拶(あいさつ)することだ。
If you want to settle in at work, be the one who says hello first.

The same action, two different arguments. ものだ appeals to custom and would be just as true if you did not care about fitting in; ことだ is a piece of tactical advice tied to the goal in the first clause. ものだ therefore sounds old-fashioned and moralising in a way ことだ does not - and can be dropped into a scolding, which ことだ cannot.

  • ❌ 私(わたし)は明日(あした)早(はや)く起(お)きることです。 → ✅ 私(わたし)は明日(あした)早(はや)く起(お)きるつもりです。
    ことだ is advice you give someone else. It cannot announce your own plan.

  • ❌ すみません、この書類(しょるい)をコピーすることです。 → ✅ すみません、この書類(しょるい)をコピーしていただけますか。
    A request about one occasion is not advice. ことだ prescribes a general solution, not a favour.

  • ❌ 元気(げんき)なことです。 → ✅ 元気(げんき)を出(だ)すことです。
    Advice ことだ takes a verb. An adjective has to become an action first.

  • ❌ 課長(かちょう)、たまには休(やす)むことですよ。 → ✅ 課長(かちょう)、たまにはお休(やす)みになってはいかがでしょうか。
    Polite endings do not change the stance. Advising upwards needs a different pattern altogether.

  • ❌ 遅(おく)れる場合(ばあい)は連絡(れんらく)することです。（as a posted rule） → ✅ 遅(おく)れる場合(ばあい)は連絡(れんらく)すること。

～ことはない

Meaning · there's no need to; it never happens - two readings of one shape, sorted out by who the subject is

With you as the subject, ～ことはない is reassurance: the effort or the worry is unnecessary, so stop. With somebody or something else as the subject, it is a statement about the world: that does not happen. The words are identical; the subject decides.

～ことはない · Formation

The verb is in the dictionary form in both senses.

Note - with 決(けっ)して, めったに or ほとんど in front of it, only the "never happens" reading survives: 決(けっ)して遅(おく)れることはない. With よ, だいじょうぶ or a comforting tone, only the "no need to" reading survives. Japanese almost never leaves the two genuinely ambiguous.

There's no need to worry.

There's really no need for you to come all the way to the station to meet me.

～ことはない · Examples (cont.)

あの人(ひと)が約束(やくそく)の時間(じかん)に遅(おく)れることはない。
He is never late for an appointment.

この辺(へん)は冬(ふゆ)でも雪(ゆき)が積(つ)もることはありません。
Round here the snow never settles, even in winter.

部長(ぶちょう)は厳(きび)しい人(ひと)ですが、理由(りゆう)もなく怒(おこ)ることはありません。
Our manager is strict, but he never gets angry without a reason.

失敗(しっぱい)しても誰(だれ)かに笑(わら)われることはないので、安心(あんしん)して挑戦(ちょうせん)してみてください。
Nobody is going to laugh at you if it goes wrong, so give it a try without worrying.

～ことはない · Nuance & register

The "no need to" sense is consolation, and it is one of the kindest patterns in the language. Its natural home is comforting someone who is upset, anxious, or apologising too much: 泣(な)くことないよ, 謝(あやま)ることはありません. In speech the は regularly drops - 気(き)にすることないよ - and that shortened version is what you will actually hear between friends.

The same words can turn cool. Said flatly about someone else's plan, わざわざ行(い)くことはない means "there's no point going", and the warmth disappears. Tone and context carry that difference entirely; the grammar does not mark it.

The "never happens" sense is a statement of reliability. It is stronger than あまり～ない and softer than an absolute: it claims a pattern, not a law of physics. It reads well in descriptions, product blurbs and character sketches, and it takes ほとんど comfortably - 故障(こしょう)することはほとんどありません.

～ことはない · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two register cautions. Turning down help from someone senior with ことはありません is grammatical but a little blunt; the set humble phrase is ～には及(およ)びません. And ことはない never means "must not" - reading 遅(おく)れることはない as a warning about lateness reverses the sentence.

Note - the negative row in the table is a separate, very common idiom. ～ないことはない is a double negative meaning "it's not that I can't": 辛(から)い物(もの)が食(た)べられないことはないんですが、あまり 得意(とくい)ではありません. It is a hedge, and it usually signals a polite reluctance rather than genuine ability.

～ことはない · ～ことはない vs ～なくてもいい

Rule of thumb - なくてもいい permits you to skip it. ことはない says the action would be pointless or unwarranted, and normally comes with a reason.

明日(あした)は来(こ)なくてもいいですよ。
You don't have to come in tomorrow.

熱(ねつ)があるのに来(く)ることはありませんよ。
There's no call for you to come in with a fever.

The first grants permission - the speaker has the authority to excuse you, and is doing so. The second argues that coming would be unreasonable, and it carries concern for the listener. That is why ことはない fits consolation and なくてもいい fits a manager approving a day off. Note also that なくてもいい answers a question; ことはない heads off an intention.

～ことはない · ～ことはない vs ～ことがない

Rule of thumb - ことがない with the た-form is experience: you have never done it. ことはない with the dictionary form is about need or frequency.

北海道(ほっかいどう)へ行(い)ったことがありません。
I've never been to Hokkaido.

北海道(ほっかいどう)では、夏(なつ)に冷房(れいぼう)を使(つか)うことはありません。
In Hokkaido nobody uses air conditioning in summer.

The first looks back over your life; the second describes a standing state of affairs. The は/が swap is not decorative - が presents the experience as a fact you possess, は sets the situation against expectation ("as for that happening: it doesn't"). If you meet 行(い)ったことはない with は and the た-form, it is still experience, now with a contrastive edge: "been there, no - heard of it, yes."

～ことはない · ～ことはない vs ～はずがない

Rule of thumb - ことはない reports that something does not happen, from observation. はずがない says it could not possibly be so, from reasoning.

彼女(かのじょ)が約束(やくそく)を忘(わす)れることはない。
She never forgets an arrangement.

彼女(かのじょ)が約束(やくそく)を忘(わす)れるはずがない。
There's no way she's forgotten the arrangement.

The first is a general character reference and could be said about her at any time. The second is about this occasion and is an inference - you are ruling out a possibility that has just been raised. So はずがない answers "do you think she forgot?", while ことはない answers "what's she like?"

～ことはない · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 会議(かいぎ)に遅(おく)れることはない。（intending "you mustn't be late"） → ✅ 会議(かいぎ)に遅(おく)れてはいけない。
    ことはない never prohibits. As written, the sentence praises someone's punctuality.

  • ❌ 泣(な)いたことはないよ、大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)。 → ✅ 泣(な)くことはないよ、大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)。
    Consolation takes the dictionary form. With the た-form the sentence flips to experience - "I have never cried" - which is not what a crying friend needs to hear.

  • ❌ この問題(もんだい)は難(むずか)しいことはない。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)は難(むずか)しくはない。
    The pattern takes verbs. An い-adjective negates on its own stem.

～ことはない · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌（to a senior colleague）部長(ぶちょう)、わざわざお越(こ)しいただくことはありません。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、お越(こ)しいただくには及(およ)びません。
    Declining a superior's offer with ことはありません sounds like you are telling them not to bother. ～には及(およ)びません is the set humble phrase for exactly this.

～しかない

Meaning · there's nothing for it but to… - the circumstances have closed every other door

しか means "nothing other than", and it always drags a negative along with it. Put it after a verb and the sentence says: of all the things one might do here, exactly one remains. Nobody ordered it and nobody judged it - the situation simply ran out of options.

～しかない · Formation

The verb stays in the dictionary form. しか here does the same work it does after a noun, which you already know from 千円(せんえん)しかない.

Note - there is no positive form. ❌待(ま)つしかある does not exist; しか is grammatically bound to ない. The negative is doing the work of "other than", not of "not".

～しかない · Examples

バスがないから、歩(ある)くしかない。
There's no bus, so we'll just have to walk.

こうなったら、やるしかありません。
Now that it's come to this, we just have to do it.

終電(しゅうでん)を逃(のが)したので、タクシーで帰(かえ)るしかなかった。
I missed the last train, so there was nothing for it but to take a taxi home.

雨(あめ)がやむまで、ここで待(ま)つしかないですね。
Looks like we've no choice but to wait here until the rain stops.

部品(ぶひん)がもう作(つく)られていないので、新(あたら)しいのを買(か)うしかありません。
The part isn't made any more, so there's nothing for it but to buy a new one.

～しかない · Examples (cont.)

誰(だれ)も手伝(てつだ)ってくれないなら、一人(ひとり)で全部(ぜんぶ)やるしかない。
If nobody's going to help, I'll just have to do the whole thing on my own.

説明書(せつめいしょ)を何度(なんど)読(よ)んでも分(わ)からないので、メーカーに電話(でんわ)して聞(き)くしかなさそうです。
I've read the manual over and over and still don't understand it, so I'll probably just have to ring the maker.

店(みせ)の売上(うりあげ)がここまで落(お)ちてしまった以上(いじょう)、営業(えいぎょう)時間(じかん)を短(みじか)くするしかないでしょう。
With takings down this far, we're going to have to cut the opening hours.

試合(しあい)に勝(か)つには、残(のこ)り五分(ごふん)で二点(にてん)取(と)るしかない。
To win the match, we have to score twice in the last five minutes - there's no other way.

～しかない · Nuance & register

The feeling is resignation, but not always gloom. The same sentence can be a sigh or a squaring of the shoulders, depending on delivery: やるしかない is one of the most common things a Japanese speaker says before starting something difficult, and it is closer to "right, let's get on with it" than to despair. What is constant is that the alternatives have been considered and found gone.

That is the pattern's boundary. しかない reports what circumstances have left you. It does not report a rule, an order, or a moral duty. A deadline set by your boss is 出(だ)さなければならない; a duty you feel is 行(い)かないわけにはいかない; a situation in which the only remaining option is to hand it in unfinished is 出(だ)すしかない.

～しかない · Nuance & register (cont.)

Register is neutral, and the polite form is simply しかありません. Two siblings sit above it in formality: ～ほかない and ～よりほかない are written, slightly literary equivalents, and ～ざるを得(え)ない is formal and adds a sense of being compelled against your will - an inspection you must accept, a resignation you must tender. In conversation, しかない covers almost everything those two do.

One small caution. Applied to somebody else's situation, しかない can sound dismissive - あきらめるしかないですよ tells a person their problem is over. Softening it to しかないかもしれませんね leaves them their options.

～しかない · ～しかない vs ～なければならない

Rule of thumb - なければならない comes from outside: a rule, an order, a deadline. しかない comes from the situation: the other options have disappeared.

外国人(がいこくじん)登録(とうろく)の書類(しょるい)は本人(ほんにん)が出(だ)さなければなりません。
The registration paperwork has to be submitted by the person themselves.

郵便(ゆうびん)が間(ま)に合(あ)わないので、自分(じぶん)で持(も)っていくしかありません。
The post won't get there in time, so I'll have to take it in myself.

The first would still be true if the post were quick, the office were near and you had all week; a regulation does not care. The second exists only because of a timing problem, and it would evaporate if the problem did. Ask yourself: who says so? If the answer is a person or a rulebook, use なければならない.

～しかない · ～しかない vs ～ざるを得(え)ない

Rule of thumb - both mean "have no choice", but しかない is about options running out, while ～ざるを得(え)ない is about being compelled against your own wishes, usually by logic, duty or authority. It is also distinctly formal.

終(お)わらないので、明日(あした)続(つづ)きをやるしかない。
It isn't going to be finished, so I'll just carry on tomorrow.

これだけの証拠(しょうこ)がそろえば、事実(じじつ)だと認(みと)めざるを得(え)ない。
With this much evidence, one is forced to accept it as fact.

ざるを得(え)ない belongs to reports, editorials and formal speech, and it carries a note of reluctance that しかない does not require. Saying it about tomorrow's leftover work would sound as though the work had defeated you in some tragic way. Conversely, しかない in the evidence sentence would make the conclusion sound like a practical inconvenience rather than an intellectual surrender.

～しかない · ～しかない vs ～だけだ

Rule of thumb - だけだ says that is all that's left to do, neutrally, and often happily. しかない says there is nothing else you could do, with the other options mourned.

準備(じゅんび)は終(お)わった。あとは本番(ほんばん)を待(ま)つだけだ。
The preparation's done. All that's left is to wait for the day.

準備(じゅんび)が間(ま)に合(あ)わなかった。このまま本番(ほんばん)に出(で)るしかない。
We didn't get everything ready. We'll just have to go on as we are.

だけだ looks forward from a job well done; しかない looks back at options that closed. The two are not interchangeable even where both are grammatical - telling a nervous performer 楽(たの)しむだけだよ is encouragement, while 楽(たの)しむしかないよ implies the performance itself is a write-off.

～しかない · Common mistakes

  • ❌ バスがないから歩(ある)かないしかない。 → ✅ バスがないから歩(ある)くしかない。
    しかない attaches to the dictionary form. The negative is already inside ない at the end; putting another one on the verb reverses the sentence.

  • ❌ さいふには百円(ひゃくえん)しかあります。 → ✅ さいふには百円(ひゃくえん)しかありません。
    しか can never end in a positive. It is one half of a pair, and ない is the other half.

  • ❌ パスポートの申請(しんせい)は本人(ほんにん)が行(い)くしかありません。（reporting the rule） → ✅ パスポートの申請(しんせい)は本人(ほんにん)が行(い)かなければなりません。
    A published requirement is an obligation from outside, not a situation that ran out of options.

  • ❌ このお菓子(かし)はここにしか売(う)っています。 → ✅ このお菓子(かし)はここでしか売(う)っていません。
    Two things: the sentence needs its negative, and しか follows the particle it attaches to - ここで, since the shop is the location of selling.

～わけにはいかない

Meaning · I can't very well do that - it is perfectly possible, and I still can't

This is not a pattern about ability and not a pattern about rules. What stops you is a promise, a position, a debt of gratitude, or your own sense of what is decent. It is obligation as you feel it, rather than obligation as somebody imposes it.

～わけにはいかない · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - ❌わけにはいけない is the single most common error with this pattern. The verb is いく in its negative いかない; there is no potential form here.

～わけにはいかない · Examples

今日(きょう)は休(やす)むわけにはいかない。
I can't very well take today off.

これは人(ひと)に話(はな)すわけにはいきません。
This isn't something I can tell anyone.

I drove here, so I can hardly have a drink.

図書館(としょかん)の本(ほん)だから、線(せん)を引(ひ)くわけにはいかない。
It's a library book, so I can't go underlining it.

後輩(こうはい)が残(のこ)って働(はたら)いているのに、先(さき)に帰(かえ)るわけにはいきません。
My juniors are still here working - I can't just leave ahead of them.

～わけにはいかない · Examples (cont.)

自分(じぶん)から言(い)い出(だ)した以上(いじょう)、今(いま)さらやめるわけにはいかない。
I was the one who proposed it, so backing out now is out of the question.

大事(だいじ)な資料(しりょう)を預(あず)かっているので、この部屋(へや)を空(あ)けるわけにはいきません。
I'm looking after some important documents, so I can't leave this room unattended.

わざわざ招待(しょうたい)してくれたのだから、出席(しゅっせき)しないわけにはいかない。
They went to the trouble of inviting me, so I can't really not go.

熱(ねつ)はありますが、今日(きょう)の打(う)ち合(あ)わせにだけは出(で)ないわけにはいきません。
I do have a fever, but today's meeting is the one I can't get out of.

～わけにはいかない · Nuance & register

Ask, of any sentence you are tempted to build with this: could I physically do it? If the answer is no, the pattern is wrong. A person who cannot swim does not say 泳(およ)ぐわけにはいかない; they say 泳(およ)げません. The pattern's whole content is the gap between could and can't bring myself to.

For the same reason the subject is nearly always the speaker. You are reporting the inside of your own head - the obligation you feel, the awkwardness you would not survive. Said about somebody else it presumes to know their conscience, which is why 君(きみ)は帰(かえ)るわけにはいかない sounds less like an observation than like pressure. Describing a third person's position is possible when you have grounds: 社長(しゃちょう)としては認(みと)めるわけにはいかないだろう - note the だろう doing the hedging.

～わけにはいかない · Nuance & register (cont.)

The も variant is worth having. ～わけにもいかない adds this impossibility to one already mentioned, and it is how the pattern usually appears when you are boxed in from both sides: 断(ことわ)るわけにもいかず、引(ひ)き受(う)けてしまった.

Register is neutral in itself. The polite わけにはいきません is completely ordinary at work; わけにはまいりません is humble and belongs to formal apologies and customer-facing speech. In casual conversation the は often drops - 寝(ね)るわけにいかないんだよ.

Note - the double negative ～ないわけにはいかない means "I have to", and the reluctance is part of the meaning. It is the sentence of someone who has looked for a way out and not found one. Do not use it for things you are glad to do: ❌好(す)きな人(ひと)に会(あ)わないわけにはいかない is a strange thing to say about a date.

～わけにはいかない · ～わけにはいかない vs ～てはいけない

Rule of thumb - てはいけない is a prohibition from outside: a rule, a parent, a sign. わけにはいかない is you stopping yourself, for reasons you would have to explain.

館内(かんない)では飲食(いんしょく)してはいけません。
Eating and drinking are not allowed inside.

会議(かいぎ)の途中(とちゅう)なので、席(せき)を立(た)つわけにはいきません。
We're in the middle of a meeting, so I can hardly get up and leave.

Nothing forbids leaving a meeting; you simply cannot do it and remain the person you want to be at that table. Put てはいけない there and you invent a rule that does not exist. Conversely, わけにはいかない on a museum notice would be peculiar - the building has no feelings to protect.

A quick test: if you can name who forbids it, use てはいけない.

～わけにはいかない · ～ないわけにはいかない vs ～なければならない

Rule of thumb - なければならない is neutral necessity; it says nothing about how you feel. ～ないわけにはいかない says you would rather not, and cannot see a way out.

申込書(もうしこみしょ)には印鑑(いんかん)を押(お)さなければなりません。
You have to put your seal on the application form.

部長(ぶちょう)に直接(ちょくせつ)頼(たの)まれたので、引(ひ)き受(う)けないわけにはいかなかった。
The manager asked me personally, so I couldn't very well refuse.

The first is a form's requirement and carries no emotion at all - it would be identical on a website's help page. The second is a small story about pressure: you did not want the job, you looked for an exit, and the way it was asked closed it. Swap them and the form sentence becomes oddly resentful, while the second becomes a flat report that misses the whole point.

～わけにはいかない · ～ないわけにはいかない vs ～なければならない (cont.)

This is also why ないわけにはいかない almost always has a reason clause in front of it. Without one, the reluctance has nothing to attach to.

～わけにはいかない · ～わけにはいかない vs ～ことができない

Rule of thumb - ことができない is genuine inability: the skill, the permission or the physics is missing. わけにはいかない is unwillingness on principle, with the ability intact.

漢字(かんじ)が読(よ)めないので、この書類(しょるい)に署名(しょめい)することができません。
I can't read the kanji, so I'm unable to sign this document.

中身(なかみ)を確認(かくにん)していないので、この書類(しょるい)に署名(しょめい)するわけにはいきません。
I haven't checked what's in it, so I'm not prepared to sign this document.

The first speaker cannot sign. The second could sign in a second, and is refusing on principle. That distinction is often the difference between an apology and a stand, and choosing the wrong one misrepresents you badly - the first sentence sounds helpless where you meant to sound careful.

～わけにはいかない · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 今日(きょう)は帰(かえ)るわけにはいけません。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は帰(かえ)るわけにはいきません。
    The verb is いく, negated as いかない／いきません. There is no potential form in this pattern.

  • ❌ 泳(およ)げないので、向(む)こう岸(ぎし)まで行(い)くわけにはいかない。 → ✅ 泳(およ)げないので、向(む)こう岸(ぎし)まで行(い)くことができない。
    Real inability is ことができない. わけにはいかない requires that you could do the thing.

  • ❌（on a sign）ここに車(くるま)を止(と)めるわけにはいきません。 → ✅ ここに車(くるま)を止(と)めてはいけません。
    A notice issues a prohibition. わけにはいかない reports a private conflict, and car parks do not have those.

～わけにはいかない · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 君(きみ)は約束(やくそく)を破(やぶ)るわけにはいかないよ。 → ✅ 君(きみ)は約束(やくそく)を破(やぶ)ってはいけないよ。
    Aimed at someone else, the pattern claims to know what their conscience will allow. If you mean to tell them not to, say so directly.

  • ❌ 大好(だいす)きなケーキだから、食(た)べないわけにはいかない。 → ✅ 大好(だいす)きなケーキだから、食(た)べずにはいられない。
    ないわけにはいかない carries reluctance. For something you cannot resist, you want the "can't help it" pattern instead.

～わけにはいかない · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 明日(あした)は早(はや)く起(お)きなければなりません。 (b) 明日(あした)は早(はや)く起(お)きないわけにはいきません。

(a) is a plain statement of necessity. There is an early train or an early meeting, and the speaker reports the fact without any attitude to it. You could say it cheerfully.

(b) adds a person who does not want to. It implies you have already thought about staying in bed, considered whether anyone would mind, and concluded that you cannot get away with it. The obligation in (a) comes from the timetable; in (b) it comes from your own sense of what you owe someone.

～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません

Meaning · it's fine either way; I don't mind - permission given by disclaiming any objection

かまう means "to mind, to be bothered". So ～てもかまわない does not say the action is good; it says the speaker has no objection to it. That small difference in angle is why the pattern is at home in written rules and business Japanese, and why it can sound a shade cooler than a warm "go ahead".

～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Formation

かまわない sits on top of the same ～ても you already know. Everything before it takes the ordinary ても shapes.

You may use a dictionary in the exam.

If you're short of time, you don't have to do all of it.

部屋(へや)は狭(せま)くてもかまわないので、駅(えき)に近(ちか)いところがいいです。
I don't mind a small flat - I'd rather have somewhere near the station.

お支払(しはら)いは現金(げんきん)でもカードでもかまいません。
You're welcome to pay by cash or by card.

～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Examples (cont.)

書(か)き方(かた)が少(すこ)しくらい違(ちが)ってもかまいませんから、まず出(だ)してみてください。
It doesn't matter if the format isn't quite right - just hand something in first.

明日(あした)の集合(しゅうごう)は八時(はちじ)でも九時(くじ)でもかまわないと部長(ぶちょう)は言(い)っていました。
The manager said eight or nine would both be fine for tomorrow's start.

写真(しゃしん)を撮(と)ってもかまいませんか。後(あと)でブログに載(の)せたいのですが。
Would you mind if I took a photo? I'd like to put it on my blog later.

急(いそ)ぎではありませんので、お返事(へんじ)は来週(らいしゅう)になってもかまいません。
There's no rush, so it's perfectly all right if you reply next week.

～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Nuance & register

てもいい and てもかまわない are close, but they are not saying quite the same thing. てもいい calls the action good - a green light. てもかまわない says the speaker is not bothered - the light was never red in the first place. Out of that comes the pattern's characteristic flatness: it is generous without being warm.

That flatness is an advantage in writing and business, which is where you meet it most: exam instructions, application forms, hotel policies, emails from suppliers. キャンセルは前日(ぜんじつ)までにご連絡(れんらく)いただければかまいません sounds like an institution, and is meant to.

～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Nuance & register (cont.)

It is also, spoken with the wrong intonation, the sentence of someone who is politely annoyed. 別(べつ)にかまいませんけど ("I don't mind, but…") is a recognisable way of not being fine at all. Learners often reach for かまいません when they mean to be reassuring and land somewhere colder than intended; if warmth is the goal, もちろんいいですよ or 大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)ですよ does the job better.

As a question, ～てもかまいませんか is politer and more formal than ～てもいいですか, and slightly less formal than ～てもよろしいでしょうか. All three are usable at work; the middle one is the safe everyday choice with someone you do not know well.

One direction to avoid: granting permission to someone senior. Telling your manager 座(すわ)ってもかまいませんよ puts you in charge of the chairs. Answering their question with a bare かまいません is normal and fine - it is the unprompted granting that presumes.

～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · ～てもかまわない vs ～てもいい

Rule of thumb - てもいい says the action is all right. てもかまわない says the speaker doesn't mind. The first is permission; the second is the absence of objection.

そのペン、使(つか)ってもいいよ。
Sure, use that pen.

どのペンを使(つか)ってもかまいません。
It doesn't matter which pen you use.

The first is a friend handing something over. The second is an invigilator stating that pen choice is not regulated. Notice how naturally かまわない takes どの, どちら, いつ and でも - it is the form of choice for saying "any of these is acceptable", because indifference is exactly what it expresses.

～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · ～てもかまわない vs ～てもいい (cont.)

In casual conversation between friends, てもいい and simply いいよ are far commoner. かまわないよ is not wrong there, but it sounds a little grown-up and measured.

～てもかまいませんか vs ～てもよろしいでしょうか

Rule of thumb - same request, three rungs of politeness: ～てもいいですか ＜ ～てもかまいませんか ＜ ～てもよろしいでしょうか.

窓(まど)を開(あ)けてもいいですか。
Can I open the window?

少(すこ)し窓(まど)を開(あ)けてもかまいませんか。
Would you mind if I opened the window a little?

恐(おそ)れ入(い)りますが、窓(まど)を開(あ)けてもよろしいでしょうか。
Excuse me - would it be all right if I opened the window?

～てもかまいませんか vs ～てもよろしいでしょうか (cont.)

Treat these as a ladder rather than as synonyms. The first is what you say to a friend or a shop assistant, the second to a colleague or a stranger, the third to a customer or in a formal setting. Going too far up the ladder is a mild error too: よろしいでしょうか to a close friend sounds like a joke.

～てもかまわない vs ～ても大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)

Rule of thumb - same meaning, different worlds. ても大丈夫(だいじょうぶ) is the spoken, everyday version; てもかまわない is the written and formal one.

明日(あした)、少(すこ)し遅(おく)れても大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)ですか。
Is it OK if I'm a bit late tomorrow?

提出(ていしゅつ)が明日(あした)になってもかまいません。
It is acceptable for submission to be tomorrow.

大丈夫(だいじょうぶ) has spread enormously in modern spoken Japanese and now covers most of what かまわない used to do in conversation. The reverse swap is what sounds off: an official notice reading 大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)です feels chatty, and in a contract it would be unusable.

～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 明日(あした)行(い)くでもかまいません。 → ✅ 明日(あした)行(い)ってもかまいません。
    Verbs come in on the て-form. でもかまわない is for nouns and な-adjectives: 明日(あした)でもかまいません.

  • ❌ 値段(ねだん)が高(たか)いでもかまいません。 → ✅ 値段(ねだん)が高(たか)くてもかまいません。
    An い-adjective becomes ～くても first. The でも route is not open to it.

  • ❌ 全部(ぜんぶ)読(よ)まなくでもかまいません。 → ✅ 全部(ぜんぶ)読(よ)まなくてもかまいません。
    The negative is ～なくても. なくで does not exist.

  • ❌（to your manager, unprompted）部長(ぶちょう)、こちらの席(せき)に座(すわ)ってもかまいませんよ。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、こちらの席(せき)にどうぞ。
    Handing out permission upwards presumes authority you do not have. Offer, don't permit.

～てもかまわない・～てもかまいません · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 明日(あした)でもいいですよ。 (b) 明日(あした)でもかまいませんよ。

(a) offers tomorrow as a positively acceptable option. It is warm and helpful, the sort of thing you say to put someone at ease about a deadline.

(b) says you would not be bothered by tomorrow. The content is the same, but the tone is a step cooler and more businesslike - it disclaims an objection rather than extending an offer. In an email to a client that neutrality is an advantage; to a friend who is apologising for being late, (a) is the kinder sentence.

～てごらん

Meaning · go on, try it; have a look - a warm, gentle command, always aimed downward

ごらん comes from the honorific verb for "look", but do not let that mislead you: in ～てごらん the respect has been worn away completely. What is left is an encouraging imperative that an adult uses to a child, a teacher to a student, a senior to a junior. It is kind. It is also unmistakably from above.

～てごらん · Formation

Attaches to Form Example

Rule of thumb - there is no negative. ❌見(み)ないでごらん does not exist. If you want someone not to do something, you are outside this pattern altogether.

Have a think about it yourself. You'll get there.

お母(かあ)さんに聞(き)いてごらん。知(し)っているかもしれないよ。
Go and ask your mum. She might know.

～てごらん · Examples (cont.)

It looks difficult, but try following the instructions - it takes ten minutes.

～てごらん · Nuance & register

The one thing to fix in your head is direction. ～てごらん flows downhill: parent to child, teacher to pupil, coach to player, an older friend to a much younger one. Between adults of equal standing it sounds patronising, and pointed at anyone senior it is close to rude - no amount of politeness elsewhere in the sentence rescues it. This surprises learners, because the word inside it is an honorific one; the honorific is directed at the looking, historically, not at the listener.

Within its range, though, it is genuinely affectionate. Compared with the plain imperative 食(た)べろ or even with 食(た)べなさい, ～てごらん invites rather than orders. It very often comes with a reason or a reassurance attached - おいしいよ, 大丈夫(だいじょうぶ)だから - and that is part of its character: it is the grammar of coaxing.

～てごらん · Nuance & register (cont.)

～てごらんなさい is the fuller form. It is a little more formal and a little more instructional, which is why it turns up in children's books, teaching materials and the speech of teachers; ～てごらん is the clipped, homelier version you hear at a kitchen table. Neither is polite in the ます sense - adding です or ます to them is not possible.

You will also meet it addressed to nobody in particular, in lyrics, adverts and narration: 空(そら)を見(み)てごらん. There it reads as gentle, slightly poetic, and the downward angle becomes warmth rather than authority.

～てごらん · ～てごらん vs ～てみて

Rule of thumb - てみて is neutral and works in any direction. てごらん adds warmth and a difference in standing, and only works downward.

これ、おいしいので食(た)べてみてください。
Do try this, it's delicious.

これ、おいしいから食(た)べてごらん。
Go on, try this - it's delicious.

To a guest, a colleague or a customer, the first is the only safe option. The second is what you would say to your nephew. Note that they are not doing the same grammatical work either: てみる means "do it and find out", so 食(た)べてみて is literally "eat it and see", while 食(た)べてごらん is closer to "let's see you eat it".

～てごらん · ～てごらん vs ～てみなさい

Rule of thumb - both are downward. てみなさい is an instruction and can be stern; てごらん is an invitation and is warm.

もう一度(いちど)、最初(さいしょ)から読(よ)んでみなさい。
Read it again from the beginning.

もう一度(いちど)、最初(さいしょ)から読(よ)んでごらん。
Have another go at it from the start.

A teacher who has said the same thing three times uses the first; a teacher sitting beside a struggling child uses the second. なさい carries the authority of an order that expects compliance, which is why parents reach for it when patience is running out. ごらん expects willingness rather than obedience.

～てごらん · ～てごらん vs ご覧(らん)ください

Rule of thumb - same word, opposite directions. ご覧(らん)ください and ご覧(らん)になる are honorific: they raise the listener. ～てごらん does not.

資料(しりょう)の三(さん)ページ目(め)をご覧(らん)ください。
Please look at page three of the handout.

ここ、見(み)てごらん。名前(なまえ)が書(か)いてあるよ。
Look here - your name's written on it.

The first is what you say to an audience, a client or your manager; the second is what you say to a seven-year-old. Because they look so similar, learners produce the fatal hybrid ❌見(み)てごらんください, which staples an honorific ending onto a downward command. It is not a politer てごらん; it is a sentence that cannot be repaired without changing pattern.

～てごらん · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 先生(せんせい)、この写真(しゃしん)を見(み)てごらんください。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)、この写真(しゃしん)をご覧(らん)ください。
    てごらん cannot be made polite by adding ください. To ask a superior to look, use the honorific verb on its own.

  • ❌ 部長(ぶちょう)、こちらの席(せき)に座(すわ)ってごらん。 → ✅ 部長(ぶちょう)、こちらの席(せき)にお座(すわ)りください。
    The pattern is downward only. Aimed at a superior it reads as though you were addressing a child.

  • ❌ その箱(はこ)は開(あ)けないでごらん。 → ✅ その箱(はこ)は開(あ)けないでね。
    There is no negative form. Use an ordinary negative request instead.

  • ❌ もう一度(いちど)言(い)うごらん。 → ✅ もう一度(いちど)言(い)ってごらん。
    ごらん attaches to the て-form, like てみる and てください.

～てごらん · Practice 1

1. 自転車(じてんしゃ)、こわくないから一人(ひとり)で乗(の)っ____。

一人(ひとり)で乗(の)ってごらん。

The reassurance こわくないから in front of it is the giveaway: this is an adult coaxing a child onto a bike, which is the pattern's home ground. The fuller 乗(の)ってごらんなさい would sound more like an instructor than a parent.

～てごらん · Practice 2

2. Choose: （お客様(きゃくさま)に）こちらの新製品(しんせいひん)を試(ため)し（てごらん・てみてください）。

試(ため)してみてください。

A customer must never be spoken to downward, and てごらん is downward by definition. てみてください is neutral and polite; in a shop you would often go further still with お試(ため)しください.

～てごらん · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese, said by a parent to a small child: "Look - the moon's come out."

見(み)てごらん。お月(つき)さまが出(で)てきたよ。

てごらん fits the speaker and the listener exactly, and the final よ matches its warm, coaxing tone. 見(み)なさい would turn the same sentence into an instruction.

～てごらん · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 社長(しゃちょう)、こちらのグラフを見(み)てごらんください。

✅ 社長(しゃちょう)、こちらのグラフをご覧(らん)ください。

てごらんください combines a downward command with an honorific ending, and the two cannot coexist. The honorific verb ご覧(らん)になる supplies the polite request on its own.

～てごらん · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) もう少(すこ)し待(ま)ってみて。 (b) もう少(すこ)し待(ま)ってごらん。

(a) is neutral. You are asking someone - anyone, of any standing - to wait and see what happens. Between adults it is the ordinary way to say it.

(b) adds a difference in position and a note of encouragement. The speaker knows something the listener does not, and is reassuring them that waiting will be worthwhile. Said to a child or a junior colleague it is warm; said to your boss it would sound as though you were patting them on the head.

～ずに

Meaning · without doing - the written, slightly formal twin of ～ないで

ずに and ないで mean the same thing. What separates them is texture: ずに is tighter, older and more literary, and it belongs to writing, to careful speech and to a large family of set expressions. Learn the two irregular stems - せずに and 来(こ)ずに - and the rest is straightforward.

～ずに · Formation

Take the ない-form, drop the ない, add ずに. Two verbs break the rule.

Attaches to Form Example
Verb (う-verb) ～ない stem + ずに 言(い)わない → 言(い)わずに
Verb (る-verb) ～ない stem + ずに 食(た)べない → 食(た)べずに
Verb (する) せずに 連絡(れんらく)する → 連絡(れんらく)せずに
Verb (来(く)る) 来(こ)ずに 来(く)る → 来(こ)ずに

～ずに · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - ❌しずに is the commonest error on this page. する does not use its ordinary stem here; the form is せずに, from an older negative. Likewise 来(く)る gives 来(こ)ずに, never ❌きずに.

Note - ある and adjectives have no ずに form. "Because there wasn't any" is なくて, and that is a different pattern with a different job.

～ずに · Examples

朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べずに家(いえ)を出(で)た。
I left the house without eating breakfast.

何(なに)も言(い)わずに帰(かえ)ってしまった。
He went home without saying a word.

傘(かさ)を持(も)たずに出(で)かけて、ずぶぬれになりました。
I went out without an umbrella and got soaked.

誰(だれ)にも相談(そうだん)せずに、一人(ひとり)で決(き)めてしまった。
I decided it on my own, without consulting anybody.

息子(むすこ)は靴(くつ)もぬがずに部屋(へや)に上(あ)がってきた。
My son came into the room without even taking his shoes off.

～ずに · Examples (cont.)

辞書(じしょ)を使(つか)わずにこの文章(ぶんしょう)が読(よ)めたら、なかなかの実力(じつりょく)です。
If you can read this passage without a dictionary, you're doing rather well.

昨夜(さくや)は一睡(いっすい)もせずに原稿(げんこう)を書(か)いていました。
I was up writing the manuscript last night without a wink of sleep.

事前(じぜん)に連絡(れんらく)せずに訪(たず)ねるのは、日本(にほん)では失礼(しつれい)にあたります。
Calling on someone without letting them know in advance counts as rude in Japan.

あきらめずに続(つづ)けていれば、いつか結果(けっか)はついてくるものです。
If you keep going without giving up, results do come eventually.

～ずに · Nuance & register

ずに is a survival from classical Japanese, and it still carries a faint tightness that ないで does not. In writing - essays, news, instructions, business email - it is the default. In conversation, ないで is the default, and ずに sounds a shade formal or deliberate: 寝(ね)ずに待(ま)っていた is perfectly sayable, just slightly more composed than 寝(ね)ないで待(ま)っていた.

Certain combinations, however, are ずに only or nearly so, and they are worth learning whole:

  • ～ずにすむ - "get away without having to":
    払(はら)わずにすんだ.
  • ～ずにはいられない - "can't help doing".
  • ～ずじまい - "ended up never doing it":
    会(あ)えずじまいだった.

The bare ず without に is a further step into literary Japanese. It links clauses rather than modifying a verb - 振(ふ)り返(かえ)らず、歩(ある)き続(つづ)けた - and it belongs to novels, speeches and song lyrics. Recognise it; write ずに.

～ずに · Nuance & register (cont.)

The one thing ずに cannot do is make a request. ないで has a second life as a negative command - 触(さわ)らないでください - and ずに has no share in it. ❌触(さわ)らずにください is not a formal version of anything.

～ずに · ～ずに vs ～ないで

Rule of thumb - same meaning, different register: ずに written and formal, ないで spoken and neutral. But only ないで can form a request.

薬(くすり)を飲(の)まないで寝(ね)てしまった。
I fell asleep without taking my medicine.

所定(しょてい)の欄(らん)に記入(きにゅう)せずに提出(ていしゅつ)しないでください。
Please do not submit the form without filling in the designated fields.

The second sentence shows both at once, and the division of labour is exact: ずに does the "without", ないで does the "please don't". Swapping them produces ❌記入(きにゅう)しないで提出(ていしゅつ)せずにください, which is not repairable.

～ずに · ～ずに vs ～ないで (cont.)

Note also that the choice is partly one of rhythm: a formal written sentence that has already used ないで for its request will reach for ずに for the "without", simply to avoid saying ない twice.

～ずに · ～ずに vs ～なくて

Rule of thumb - ずに says without doing it, describing how the main action happened. なくて says because it didn't happen, giving a cause.

地図(ちず)を見(み)ずに駅(えき)まで行(い)けました。
I made it to the station without looking at a map.

地図(ちず)が読(よ)めなくて、駅(えき)に着(つ)けませんでした。
I couldn't read the map, so I never got to the station.

The first describes your method; the second explains a failure. A quick test: if you could insert "because" into the English, you want なくて. なくて also takes adjectives and ある (お金(かね)がなくて), which ずに cannot touch at all.

～ずに · ～ずに vs ～ないまま

Rule of thumb - ずに modifies the action: you did B without doing A. ないまま describes a state left unchanged: A never got done, and things stayed that way.

電気(でんき)を消(け)さずに出(で)かけてしまった。
I went out without turning the light off.

電気(でんき)が消(き)えないまま、三日(みっか)留守(るす)にしていた。
The light stayed on the whole three days I was away.

ずに is tied to a single moment - the moment of leaving. ないまま stretches over a period and emphasises that nothing changed during it. Where both are possible, ないまま adds a sense of something being left unresolved: 理由(りゆう)を聞(き)かないまま別(わか)れた lingers in a way 聞(き)かずに別(わか)れた does not.

～ずに · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 予約(よやく)しずに行(い)ったら、満席(まんせき)でした。 → ✅ 予約(よやく)せずに行(い)ったら、満席(まんせき)でした。
    する becomes せずに. This is irregular and there is no way to derive it from the ordinary stem - memorise it.

  • ❌ 昨日(きのう)は誰(だれ)も来(き)ずに、一人(ひとり)で片付(かたづ)けた。 → ✅ 昨日(きのう)は誰(だれ)も来(こ)ずに、一人(ひとり)で片付(かたづ)けた。
    来(く)る uses こ- here, the same stem as 来(こ)ない. き- belongs to 来(き)ます.

  • ❌ 廊下(ろうか)を走(はし)らずにください。 → ✅ 廊下(ろうか)を走(はし)らないでください。
    ずに never forms a request. There is no formal-sounding upgrade of ないでください along these lines.

～ずに · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 時間(じかん)がなずに、全部(ぜんぶ)見(み)られなかった。 → ✅ 時間(じかん)がなくて、全部(ぜんぶ)見(み)られなかった。
    ある has no ずに form, and in any case this sentence is giving a reason, which is なくて territory.

～ずに · Practice 1

1. 急(いそ)いでいたので、朝(あさ)ごはんも食(た)べ____会社(かいしゃ)へ向(む)かった。

朝(あさ)ごはんも食(た)べずに会社(かいしゃ)へ向(む)かった。

The sentence describes how you set off - without eating - so it wants ずに, not なくて. 食(た)べないで would be equally correct and simply sound more conversational.

～ずに · Practice 2

2. Choose: レポートは下書(したが)きを（せずに・しなくて）いきなり書(か)き始(はじ)めました。

下書(したが)きをせずにいきなり書(か)き始(はじ)めました。

This is the method you used, not the reason for something, so ずに is right - and note the irregular せずに for する. しなくて would try to make the missing draft the cause of starting, which is not what the sentence means.

～ずに · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "He left without telling anyone where he was going."

彼(かれ)はどこへ行(い)くのか誰(だれ)にも言(い)わずに出(で)ていった。

The "without" attaches to the leaving, so ずに (or the more colloquial 言(い)わないで) is the form. In a novel you might see the bare literary 言(い)わず、出(で)ていった.

～ずに · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 危(あぶ)ないですから、工事中(こうじちゅう)の場所(ばしょ)には近(ちか)づかずにください。

✅ 危(あぶ)ないですから、工事中(こうじちゅう)の場所(ばしょ)には近(ちか)づかないでください。

ずに cannot carry a request. However formal the notice, a negative instruction takes ないでください - or, in stiffer written style, 近(ちか)づかないこと.

～ずに · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 返事(へんじ)を待(ま)たずに出発(しゅっぱつ)した。 (b) 返事(へんじ)が来(こ)なくて出発(しゅっぱつ)できなかった。

(a) describes the manner of departing: you chose to go, and the reply had not come yet. The action succeeded, and the missing reply is background.

(b) gives a cause: no reply arrived, and therefore you could not set off. The action failed, and the missing reply is the reason. ずに never carries that causal weight - it only tells you what accompanied the action, or rather what didn't.

～ずにはいられない

Meaning · can't help doing it; can't stop myself - an urge that overrides you

This is the only "no choice" pattern in which the pressure comes from inside. Nobody has forbidden anything, no rule applies, and the circumstances have not closed off your options - you simply cannot keep yourself from doing it. Laughing, crying, saying the thing you should not say: ずにはいられない is the grammar of being overruled by your own feelings.

～ずにはいられない · Formation

Built on the ずに you already know, plus the potential of いる: literally "cannot be, without doing it".

Attaches to Form Example
Verb (う-verb) ～ない stem + ずにはいられない 笑(わら)う → 笑(わら)わずにはいられない
Verb (る-verb) ～ない stem + ずにはいられない 考(かんが)える → 考(かんが)えずにはいられない

Colloquial variant ～ないではいられない 笑(わら)わないではいられない
Shorter variant ～ずにいられない 笑(わら)わずにいられない
Polite ～ずにはいられません 笑(わら)わずにはいられません

～ずにはいられない · Formation (cont.)

Rule of thumb - する gives せずには, never ❌しずには, exactly as it does in plain ずに. Since half the verbs that fit this pattern are する-verbs - 心配(しんぱい)する, 同情(どうじょう)する, 応援(おうえん)する - this is the form to drill.

～ずにはいられない · Examples

この映画(えいが)を見(み)ると、泣(な)かずにはいられない。
I can't watch this film without crying.

息子(むすこ)のことを心配(しんぱい)せずにはいられません。
I can't help worrying about my son.

あの店(みせ)の前(まえ)を通(とお)ると、パンを買(か)わずにはいられない。
I can't walk past that shop without buying bread.

彼女(かのじょ)の話(はな)し方(かた)がおかしくて、笑(わら)わずにはいられなかった。
The way she told it was so funny I couldn't keep a straight face.

こんなひどい記事(きじ)を読(よ)むと、一言(ひとこと)言(い)わずにはいられません。
When I read an article this bad, I can't let it pass without saying something.

～ずにはいられない · Examples (cont.)

子(こ)どもが一人(ひとり)で泣(な)いているのを見(み)て、声(こえ)をかけずにはいられなかった。
Seeing a child crying on his own, I couldn't not go over and speak to him.

試合(しあい)の最後(さいご)の五分間(ごふんかん)は、立(た)ち上(あ)がって応援(おうえん)せずにはいられませんでした。
For the last five minutes of the match I simply had to get up and cheer.

災害(さいがい)のニュースを見(み)ていると、自分(じぶん)に何(なに)ができるのかを考(かんが)えずにはいられない。
Watching the news about the disaster, I can't stop asking myself what I could do.

祖母(そぼ)の手紙(てがみ)を読(よ)み返(かえ)すたびに、あの夏(なつ)の台所(だいどころ)を思(おも)い出(だ)さずにはいられません。
Every time I reread my grandmother's letters, I find myself back in that kitchen, that summer.

～ずにはいられない · Nuance & register

The compulsion is emotional, and the verbs that appear here reflect that: to laugh, to cry, to worry, to remember, to sympathise, to say something, to cheer. Physical needs and practical necessities do not belong - 傘(かさ)を買(か)わずにはいられなかった is odd if it merely started raining, and natural if you saw an umbrella so beautiful you had no defence against it.

Because it reports an inner state, the subject is normally you, or a narrator speaking of themselves. About a third person you need a hedge, exactly as with たい: 彼(かれ)も笑(わら)わずにはいられなかったようだ. Written flat about someone else, it claims access to a mind you cannot see into.

The register leans written and expressive: essays, reviews, letters, blog posts, novels. Spoken, it is entirely usable but a touch dramatic - in casual conversation people say つい笑(わら)っちゃった instead. That is worth knowing, because a learner who uses ずにはいられない about a mildly amusing thing sounds as though they are narrating their own memoir.

～ずにはいられない · Nuance & register (cont.)

Note - two neighbours, distinguished mainly by register. ～ないではいられない means the same and is a little more conversational. ～ずにいられない, without the は, is a shortened form that turns up in speech and in lyrics. The は adds a touch of emphasis; none of the three differs in meaning.

～ずにはいられない · ～ずにはいられない vs ～てしまう

Rule of thumb - てしまう reports that the action happened despite you. ずにはいられない reports the pressure to do it, and usually says something about you as a person.

おかしくて笑(わら)ってしまいました。
It was so funny I ended up laughing.

彼(かれ)の顔(かお)を見(み)るたびに笑(わら)わずにはいられません。
I can't look at his face without laughing.

てしまう is about one occasion and often carries regret. ずにはいられない states a standing susceptibility - every time, without fail - and carries no regret at all, only recognition. That is why the second sentence takes たびに comfortably and the first does not.

～ずにはいられない · ～ずにはいられない vs ～ないわけにはいかない

Rule of thumb - both come out as "I have to" in English, and they are opposites underneath. ずにはいられない is a want you cannot suppress; ないわけにはいかない is a duty you cannot escape.

友達(ともだち)の作品(さくひん)なので、見(み)に行(い)かないわけにはいかない。
It's my friend's work, so I can hardly not go and see it.

評判(ひょうばん)がよすぎて、見(み)に行(い)かずにはいられない。
Everyone's raving about it - I can't stay away.

In the first you would rather stay home; friendship has removed the exit. In the second nothing would keep you away. Choosing wrongly says something unfortunate about your feelings: telling a friend 見(み)に行(い)かないわけにはいかなくて admits you went out of obligation.

～ずにはいられない · ～ずにはいられない vs ～しかない

Rule of thumb - しかない is about the world running out of options. ずにはいられない is about you running out of self-control.

終電(しゅうでん)がないので、歩(ある)いて帰(かえ)るしかない。
There's no last train, so I'll have to walk home.

あんまり夜(よる)がきれいだったので、遠回(とおまわ)りせずにはいられなかった。
The night was so lovely I couldn't help taking the long way round.

Both sentences end with the speaker walking, and they explain the walking in opposite ways. If the sentence would still be true of someone with a completely different personality, you want しかない; if it depends on how this person feels, you want ずにはいられない.

～ずにはいられない · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 結果(けっか)が気(き)になって、確認(かくにん)しずにはいられない。 → ✅ 結果(けっか)が気(き)になって、確認(かくにん)せずにはいられない。
    する takes the irregular せずに. There is no しずに anywhere in Japanese.

  • ❌ 明日(あした)までにレポートを出(だ)さずにはいられない。 → ✅ 明日(あした)までにレポートを出(だ)さなければならない。
    A deadline is an obligation, not an urge. This pattern reports a feeling you cannot suppress, and nobody is emotionally overwhelmed by a report.

  • ❌ 遅(おく)れそうだったので、タクシーに乗(の)らずにはいられなかった。 → ✅ 遅(おく)れそうだったので、タクシーに乗(の)るしかなかった。
    Circumstances closing off the alternatives is しかない. ずにはいられない would suggest you have an emotional weakness for taxis.

～ずにはいられない · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 山田(やまだ)さんは感動(かんどう)せずにはいられません。 → ✅ 山田(やまだ)さんも感動(かんどう)せずにはいられなかったようです。
    About someone else, the claim needs hedging - ようだ, だろう, らしい - because you cannot see inside their head.

～ずにはいられない · Practice 1

1. あんなに一生懸命(いっしょうけんめい)練習(れんしゅう)していたのを知(し)っているので、応援(おうえん)____。

応援(おうえん)せずにはいられません。

する gives the irregular せずに. The sentence describes an emotional pull rather than a duty, which is what makes this pattern right - nobody has asked the speaker to cheer.

～ずにはいられない · Practice 2

2. Choose: 台風(たいふう)で電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まったので、会社(かいしゃ)に泊(と)まる（しかなかった・ずにはいられなかった）。

会社(かいしゃ)に泊(と)まるしかなかった。

A typhoon closing the railway is the world removing your options, which is しかない. 泊(と)まらずにはいられなかった would claim you were seized by a longing to sleep at the office.

～ずにはいられない · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "Whenever I hear that song, I can't help remembering my school days."

その曲(きょく)を聞(き)くと、学生(がくせい)時代(じだい)を思(おも)い出(だ)さずにはいられません。

Memory arriving uninvited is a textbook use of the pattern. 思(おも)い出(だ)さないではいられません says the same thing a little more conversationally.

～ずにはいられない · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 部長(ぶちょう)に頼(たの)まれたので、残業(ざんぎょう)せずにはいられませんでした。

✅ 部長(ぶちょう)に頼(たの)まれたので、残業(ざんぎょう)しないわけにはいきませんでした。

Being asked by your manager creates a duty, and duty you cannot escape is ないわけにはいかない. As written, the sentence says you were unable to restrain your appetite for overtime.

～ずにはいられない · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) その手紙(てがみ)を読(よ)んで、泣(な)いてしまった。 (b) その手紙(てがみ)を読(よ)むと、泣(な)かずにはいられない。

(a) reports one occasion. You read the letter, and the tears arrived without your consent; てしまう adds a faint sense of "I didn't mean to", which can shade into embarrassment.

(b) reports a rule about yourself. Note the non-past 読(よ)むと: this happens every time, and the speaker is stating a known feature of their own reaction. (a) is an event, (b) is a disposition.
