～っぽい

Meaning · has the quality of X; comes across as X-ish

～っぽい says that something gives off the character of the word it attaches to, without necessarily being it. Attached to a verb stem it names a personal disposition - the kind of person who keeps doing that thing. Both uses lean mildly critical: ～っぽい is what you reach for when a thing is X in a way you would rather it were not.

～っぽい · Formation

Whatever it attaches to, the result is an い-adjective and conjugates like one: 水(みず)っぽい → 水(みず)っぽくない → 水(みず)っぽかった → 水(みず)っぽくて. There is no だ after it.

～っぽい · Formation (cont.)

Attaches to Form Example
Noun Noun + っぽい 水(みず) → 水(みず)っぽい
Noun (person type) Noun + っぽい 子供(こども) → 子供(こども)っぽい
い-adjective (stem, drop い) Stem + っぽい 安(やす)い → 安(やす)っぽい
Colour word (stem) Stem + っぽい 白(しろ)い → 白(しろ)っぽい
Verb (ます-stem) ます-stem + っぽい 忘(わす)れます → 忘(わす)れっぽい
Result before a noun っぽい + Noun 子供(こども)っぽい話(はなし)
Result as an adverb っぽく + Verb 安(やす)っぽく見(み)える
Casual speech only - plain form + っぽい Verb/Noun/Adj plain + っぽい 降(ふ)る → 降(ふ)るっぽい（"looks like it'll rain"）

～っぽい · Formation (cont.)

Note - this is not a suffix you can bolt onto any noun you like. The everyday stock is short and worth learning as vocabulary: 水(みず)っぽい・油(あぶら)っぽい・湿(しめ)っぽい・ほこりっぽい・安(やす)っぽい・荒(あら)っぽい・子供(こども)っぽい・色(いろ)っぽい・白(しろ)っぽい, and from verbs 忘(わす)れっぽい・飽(あ)きっぽい・怒(おこ)りっぽい・惚(ほ)れっぽい. The last row of the table is a separate, very productive slang pattern that young speakers use for "seems like"; keep it out of writing and out of exams.

～っぽい · Examples

このスープはちょっと水(みず)っぽい。
This soup is a bit watery.

弟(おとうと)は忘(わす)れっぽくて、いつも鍵(かぎ)をさがしている。
My little brother is forgetful and is always hunting for his keys.

そんな子供(こども)っぽいことを言(い)うのはやめなさい。
Stop saying such childish things.

父(ちち)は年(とし)をとってから、前(まえ)より怒(おこ)りっぽくなった。
My father has got quicker-tempered as he's aged.

霧(きり)の向(む)こうに白(しろ)っぽい建物(たてもの)がぼんやり見(み)えた。
A whitish building showed faintly through the fog.

～っぽい · Examples (cont.)

今日(きょう)の揚(あ)げ物(もの)は油(あぶら)っぽくて、半分(はんぶん)しか食(た)べられなかった。
Today's fried food was so greasy I could only get through half of it.

値段(ねだん)のわりに安(やす)っぽく見(み)えるので、お祝(いわ)いには向(む)かないと思(おも)う。
For the price it looks cheap and nasty, so I don't think it works as a celebration gift.

姉(あね)は飽(あ)きっぽいので、今年(ことし)始(はじ)めた習(なら)い事(ごと)はもう三(みっ)つ目(め)だ。
My sister loses interest quickly - the class she started this year is already her third.

～っぽい · Nuance & register

The default flavour is mildly negative. 子供(こども)っぽい is not "sweetly childlike", it is "immature"; 安(やす)っぽい is not "affordable", it is "cheap-looking". If you want the approving version of "properly X", that is a different suffix (らしい), and mixing them up is how learners accidentally insult people.

Two families escape the negativity. Colour words - 白(しろ)っぽい, 黒(くろ)っぽい, 赤(あか)っぽい - are purely descriptive "-ish", the polite way to hedge a colour you cannot name exactly. And 色(いろ)っぽい ("alluring") is a compliment, though a fairly forward one.

～っぽい · Nuance & register (cont.)

Register is neutral-to-casual. ～っぽい in its ordinary sense appears in novels and newspapers without comment. The slang extension does not: attaching っぽい to a whole plain-form clause - 無理(むり)っぽい, 行(い)くっぽい, 終(お)わったっぽい - is conversational shorthand for みたい that spread through younger speech and still reads as loose. It is worth being able to understand; it is not worth writing in an email to a client.

Because the result is an い-adjective, everything an い-adjective does is available: 水(みず)っぽくないです, 油(あぶら)っぽかったら残(のこ)してください, 忘(わす)れっぽくなる. Learners who mentally file っぽい as "a noun ending" produce ❌子供(こども)っぽいだ, which is the same error as ❌高(たか)いだ.

～っぽい · ～っぽい vs ～らしい

Rule of thumb - らしい says the thing has the qualities it ought to have and approves; っぽい says it has the surface flavour of something it is not supposed to be, and disapproves.

The two attach to the same nouns and mean nearly opposite things about the speaker's attitude:

田中(たなか)さんは男(おとこ)らしい人(ひと)だと、みんなが言(い)う。
Everyone says Tanaka is a manly sort of person.

話(はな)し方(かた)が男(おとこ)っぽいとよく言(い)われるが、気(き)にしていない。
People often tell me I talk in a blokey way, but it doesn't bother me.

The clearest test is to apply both to the same subject. A child described as 子供(こども)らしい is behaving exactly as a child should - lively, honest, unaffected. An adult described as 子供(こども)っぽい is behaving as a child would, and should not be.

～っぽい · ～っぽい vs ～らしい (cont.)

大人(おとな)なのに子供(こども)っぽいところが、彼(かれ)の欠点(けってん)だ。
His weakness is that he's childish for a grown man.

Note that らしい has a second, unrelated job - hearsay, "apparently" - attached to a whole clause. っぽい never carries hearsay, only quality.

～っぽい · ～っぽい vs ～みたい / ～のよう

Rule of thumb - みたい compares one thing to another thing; っぽい reports a quality the thing has in itself.

彼(かれ)は先生(せんせい)みたいだ。
He's like a teacher. (he resembles one - maybe he isn't one)

話(はな)し方(かた)が先生(せんせい)っぽいね。
You've got a rather teacherly way of talking.

みたい makes a comparison you could spell out ("like a teacher does"). っぽい picks out an attribute and hangs it on the subject directly, which is why it works on materials and textures where a comparison would be strange: 水(みず)っぽいスープ is a soup that is thin, not a soup that resembles water. ❌水(みず)みたいなスープ would be an exaggeration about a completely different thing.

～っぽい · ～っぽい vs ～みたい / ～のよう (cont.)

The one place they overlap is the slang use, where 雨(あめ)っぽい and 雨(あめ)みたい both mean "looks like rain" - and there みたい is the safe choice in any register above chatting with friends.

～っぽい · ～っぽい vs ～やすい

Rule of thumb - verb-stem + っぽい describes a person's standing disposition; verb-stem + やすい describes how easy the action is, for anyone.

やすい was covered at N4 as "easy to do", and it does double duty as "prone to happen". That second sense is what collides with っぽい:

母(はは)は忘(わす)れっぽいので、買(か)い物(もの)メモを渡(わた)している。
My mother is forgetful, so I hand her a shopping list.

このパスワードは長(なが)すぎて忘(わす)れやすい。
This password is so long it's easy to forget.

～っぽい · ～っぽい vs ～やすい (cont.)

The first is a claim about my mother. The second is a claim about the password - anybody would forget it. Swap them and both sentences go wrong: a password does not have a personality, and ❌母(はは)は忘(わす)れやすい sounds like my mother is an object that is easily mislaid.

Only a handful of verbs take っぽい at all （忘(わす)れる・飽(あ)きる・怒(おこ)る・惚(ほ)れる）, and every one of them names a temperament. やすい attaches to almost any verb.

～っぽい · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 忘(わす)れるっぽい（meaning "forgetful"） → ✅ 忘(わす)れっぽい
    With verbs, っぽい takes the ます-stem. 忘(わす)れるっぽい is not ungrammatical, but it is the slang "seems like they'll forget" - a different sentence.

  • ❌ この人(ひと)は子供(こども)っぽいだ。 → ✅ この人(ひと)は子供(こども)っぽい。
    The result is an い-adjective. No だ, and the polite form is 子供(こども)っぽいです.

  • ❌ 白(しろ)いっぽい服(ふく) → ✅ 白(しろ)っぽい服(ふく)
    With an い-adjective, the final い drops before っぽい - the same trim you make before さ or すぎる.

  • ❌ お父(とう)さんは男(おとこ)っぽくて尊敬(そんけい)しています。 → ✅ お父(とう)さんは男(おとこ)らしくて尊敬(そんけい)しています。
    Praise wants らしい. っぽい would read as "he's a bit of a lad", which is not what you meant to say about someone's father.

～っぽい · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ この部屋(へや)はほこりっぽいだらけだ。 → ✅ この部屋(へや)はほこりっぽい。
    Two different suffixes cannot stack. Pick one: っぽい for the stuffy feel of the air, だらけ for visible dust lying on everything.

～っぽい · Practice 1

1. このみそ汁(しる)、お湯(ゆ)を入(い)れすぎて水(みず)____なってしまった。

水(みず)っぽくなってしまった。

っぽい builds an い-adjective, so before なる it takes the adverbial form っぽく - exactly like 安(やす)くなる. The sentence complains that the soup has gone thin and watery.

～っぽい · Practice 2

2. Choose: 妹(いもうと)は（飽(あ)きっぽい・飽(あ)きやすい）ので、どの楽器(がっき)も長(なが)く続(つづ)かない。

妹(いもうと)は飽(あ)きっぽいので、どの楽器(がっき)も長(なが)く続(つづ)かない。

The sentence is about my sister's temperament, and a standing personal disposition is っぽい territory. 飽(あ)きやすい would describe something that is easily got bored of - a property of the instrument, not of the player.

～っぽい · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "The room smelled dusty, so I opened the window."

部屋(へや)がほこりっぽかったので、窓(まど)を開(あ)けた。

ほこりっぽい is the standing expression for air that feels dusty and stale. The past tense is the ordinary い-adjective past, っぽかった. ほこりだらけ would be a different complaint - dust you can see lying on the furniture.

～っぽい · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 新入生(しんにゅうせい)のスピーチは元気(げんき)で、とても学生(がくせい)っぽくてよかった。

✅ 新入生(しんにゅうせい)のスピーチは元気(げんき)で、とても学生(がくせい)らしくてよかった。

The speaker is praising the speech, and っぽい cannot carry praise - 学生(がくせい)っぽい would suggest the speech was amateurish. When the point is that something has the qualities it should have, use らしい.

～っぽい · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 彼(かれ)は大人(おとな)っぽい。 (b) 彼(かれ)は大人(おとな)らしい。

(a) says he gives off a grown-up air without actually being one - the natural reading is that he is young, and that his manner, clothes or voice are more adult than his age. It is often said of teenagers, and can be admiring or faintly uneasy.

(b) says he is an adult and behaves as an adult properly should: responsible, measured, the way the role is supposed to be filled. It is straightforwardly approving, and it presupposes that he really is one.

～ぎみ

Meaning · showing a slight touch of; a bit on the ～ side

～ぎみ (written 気味(きみ) when it stands alone, voiced to ぎみ as a suffix) reports that something is a little way toward a state, right now. Not fully there - just far enough that you can see it. It is the word for a cold you have not properly caught yet and a train that is running four minutes behind.

～ぎみ · Formation

The result is a noun. It predicates with だ/です, modifies a following noun with の, and takes な before ので and のに exactly as any noun does.

Attaches to Form Example
Noun Noun + ぎみ 風邪(かぜ) → 風邪(かぜ)ぎみ
Noun (する-verb noun) Noun + ぎみ 緊張(きんちょう) → 緊張(きんちょう)ぎみ
Verb (ます-stem) ます-stem + ぎみ 太(ふと)ります → 太(ふと)りぎみ
Predicate ～ぎみ + だ / です 疲(つか)れぎみです
Before a noun ～ぎみ + の + Noun 疲(つか)れぎみの顔(かお)
Before ので・のに ～ぎみ + な + ので 遅(おく)れぎみなので
As an adverb ～ぎみ + に + Verb 焦(あせ)りぎみに話(はな)す

～ぎみ · Formation (cont.)

Note - the reading is ぎみ, with rendaku. The unvoiced 気味(きみ) is a separate word meaning "sensation", and it survives in 気味(きみ)が悪(わる)い - "creepy, unsettling" - which has nothing to do with this suffix. Say きみわるい for that and ぎみ for this.

～ぎみ · Examples

今日(きょう)はちょっと風邪(かぜ)ぎみです。
I've got a bit of a cold today.

最近(さいきん)、少(すこ)し太(ふと)りぎみだ。
I've put on a little weight lately.

会議(かいぎ)は予定(よてい)より遅(おく)れぎみで進(すす)んでいる。
The meeting is running slightly behind schedule.

疲(つか)れぎみの顔(かお)をしていたので、早(はや)く帰(かえ)るように言(い)った。
He looked a bit worn out, so I told him to go home early.

緊張(きんちょう)ぎみの声(こえ)で、新人(しんじん)は自己紹介(じこしょうかい)を始(はじ)めた。
In a slightly nervous voice, the new employee began her self-introduction.

～ぎみ · Examples (cont.)

このところ売(う)り上(あ)げが下(さ)がりぎみなので、来月(らいげつ)から広告(こうこく)を増(ふ)やします。
Sales have been trending a little down lately, so we'll step up the advertising from next month.

息子(むすこ)は受験(じゅけん)が近(ちか)づいてから寝不足(ねぶそく)ぎみで、朝(あさ)なかなか起(お)きられない。
Since the entrance exams got close my son has been a bit short of sleep and struggles to get up.

部長(ぶちょう)は焦(あせ)りぎみに話(はな)していたが、結局(けっきょく)結論(けつろん)は出(で)なかった。
The manager spoke with a hint of impatience, but in the end nothing was decided.

～ぎみ · Nuance & register

～ぎみ almost always points away from normal in an unwelcome direction. Health that is slipping, a schedule that is slipping, a number that is slipping. You will not hear ❌元気(げんき)ぎみ or ❌上手(じょうず)ぎみ: there is no such thing as being slightly too well.

It is also about degree, not frequency. 風邪(かぜ)ぎみ does not mean "I often catch colds"; it means "right now, a small amount of cold". That is why it pairs so naturally with 少(すこ)し and ちょっと, which reinforce rather than contradict it: 少(すこ)し疲(つか)れぎみです.

The register is neutral and it is entirely at home in business Japanese: 下(さ)がりぎみ, 遅(おく)れぎみ and 伸(の)びぎみ are ordinary vocabulary in a sales report. In writing it is normally spelled with the kanji 気味(きみ); the reading of the suffix is still ぎみ.

～ぎみ · Nuance & register (cont.)

One social use worth knowing: ～ぎみ softens. Telling a colleague 疲(つか)れぎみですね is far gentler than 疲(つか)れていますね, because it presents the observation as slight and provisional rather than as a verdict on how they look. The same hedging instinct makes 風邪(かぜ)ぎみなので休(やす)みます a polite way to call in sick without claiming to be seriously ill.

～ぎみ · ～ぎみ vs ～がち

Rule of thumb - ぎみ is how much, がち is how often. ぎみ says one state is slightly present now; がち says an event keeps recurring.

今朝(けさ)の電車(でんしゃ)は遅(おく)れぎみだった。
This morning's train was running a bit late.

この路線(ろせん)の電車(でんしゃ)は遅(おく)れがちだ。
Trains on this line tend to run late.

The first sentence is about one morning and a small number of minutes. The second is about a pattern across many mornings and says nothing about how late. Use the wrong one and you make a claim you did not intend: telling your boss 遅(おく)れがちです about a single delayed delivery accuses the whole operation of being unreliable.

～ぎみ · ～ぎみ vs ～がち (cont.)

They also behave differently in a sentence. ぎみ builds a noun and takes の before a following noun; がち builds a な-adjective and takes な: 疲(つか)れぎみの顔(かお) but 病気(びょうき)がちな子(こ)ども.

～ぎみ · ～ぎみ vs ～っぽい

Rule of thumb - ぎみ says a little of the real thing is present; っぽい says it feels like the thing, whether or not it is.

The pair that shows this best is a cold, because both are said constantly:

昨日(きのう)から風邪(かぜ)ぎみで、薬(くすり)を飲(の)んでいる。
I've had a touch of a cold since yesterday, so I'm taking medicine.

何(なん)となく風邪(かぜ)っぽいから、今日(きょう)は早(はや)く寝(ね)る。
I feel like I might be coming down with something, so I'm going to bed early.

ぎみ states a mild fact: there is a cold, in small quantity. っぽい states an impression: something about how I feel resembles a cold. Which is why 風邪(かぜ)っぽい is what you say before you are sure, and 風邪(かぜ)ぎみ once you are.

～ぎみ · ～ぎみ vs ～っぽい (cont.)

Grammatically they part company completely. っぽい produces an い-adjective - 風邪(かぜ)っぽかった, 風邪(かぜ)っぽくない - while ぎみ produces a noun: 風邪(かぜ)ぎみだった, 風邪(かぜ)ぎみではない.

～ぎみ · ～ぎみ vs ちょっと・すこし

Rule of thumb - ちょっと turns any predicate down a notch. ぎみ only attaches to a fixed kind of word, and it carries a judgement that ちょっと does not.

For a plain matter of degree, an adverb is the natural choice, and ぎみ is simply not available:

このコーヒーはちょっと苦(にが)い。
This coffee is a little bitter.

❌苦(にが)いぎみ is impossible - ぎみ does not attach to い-adjectives at all. Nor does it attach to happy states: ❌うれしいぎみ, ❌楽(たの)しみぎみ.

Where both are possible, ぎみ adds the sense of a deviation from where things should be:

体重(たいじゅう)が増(ふ)えぎみなので、夜(よる)は食(た)べないようにしている。
My weight has been creeping up, so I'm trying not to eat at night.

～ぎみ · ～ぎみ vs ちょっと・すこし (cont.)

体重(たいじゅう)が少(すこ)し増(ふ)えた is a neutral measurement. 増(ふ)えぎみ is a measurement plus a frown. And the two combine happily: 少(すこ)し増(ふ)えぎみ.

～ぎみ · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 太(ふと)るぎみ → ✅ 太(ふと)りぎみ
    With verbs, ぎみ attaches to the ます-stem, never the dictionary form.

  • ❌ 風邪(かぜ)ぎみのです。 → ✅ 風邪(かぜ)ぎみです。
    の goes between ～ぎみ and a following noun （風邪(かぜ)ぎみの学生(がくせい)）, not before です.

  • ❌ 今日(きょう)は元気(げんき)ぎみです。 → ✅ 今日(きょう)は元気(げんき)です。
    ぎみ marks a slide toward something unwanted. There is no polite way to be a little bit too healthy.

  • ❌ 彼(かれ)は学校(がっこう)を休(やす)みぎみだ。（meaning "he's often absent"） → ✅ 彼(かれ)は学校(がっこう)を休(やす)みがちだ。
    Repeated absences are frequency, and frequency is がち.

  • ❌ かぜきみです。 → ✅ かぜぎみです。
    The suffix voices. きみ unvoiced belongs to 気味(きみ)が悪(わる)い, an unrelated expression.

～ぎみ · Practice 1

1. 昨日(きのう)あまり寝(ね)ていないので、今日(きょう)は疲(つか)れ____です。

疲(つか)れぎみです。

疲(つか)れる takes its ます-stem 疲(つか)れ before ぎみ, and the result is a noun, so です attaches directly. The sentence claims mild tiredness right now, not a habit of being tired.

～ぎみ · Practice 2

2. Choose: この時計(とけい)は少(すこ)し（進(すす)みぎみ・進(すす)みがち）なので、時間(じかん)を信(しん)じすぎないほうがいい。

この時計(とけい)は少(すこ)し進(すす)みぎみなので…

The clock is permanently a small amount fast - that is degree, so ぎみ. がち would need repeated separate events, which does not fit a clock that simply runs slightly ahead. Note the な before ので, which is the noun pattern.

～ぎみ · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "The numbers have been a little low this month, so let's meet on Friday."

今月(こんげつ)は数字(すうじ)が下(さ)がりぎみなので、金曜日(きんようび)に集(あつ)まりましょう。

下(さ)がりぎみ is standard business phrasing for a mild downward trend, and it is softer than 下(さ)がっています - which is exactly why people reach for it in meetings.

～ぎみ · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 最近(さいきん)の天気(てんき)は曇(くも)りぎみで、洗濯物(せんたくもの)が乾(かわ)かない日(ひ)が多(おお)い。

✅ 最近(さいきん)の天気(てんき)は曇(くも)りがちで、洗濯物(せんたくもの)が乾(かわ)かない日(ひ)が多(おお)い。

The clue is 日(ひ)が多(おお)い - the sentence is counting days, so it is about how often, not how much. Cloudiness recurring across many days is 曇(くも)りがち, a fixed and very common combination.

～ぎみ · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 彼女(かのじょ)は緊張(きんちょう)ぎみだった。 (b) 彼女(かのじょ)は緊張(きんちょう)していた。

(a) reports a small, visible amount of nerves - her voice a little tight, her hands a little busy - and it hedges. The speaker is describing an impression from the outside and leaving room to be wrong.

(b) states plainly that she was nervous, with no ceiling on how much. It is the neutral description and could cover anything from mild to badly shaken. Between colleagues, (a) is the kinder thing to say out loud.

～がち

Meaning · has a habit of ～ing; keeps ending up that way

～がち says that something happens often, and shouldn't. It is not a measurement of degree and not a statement about one occasion - it is a complaint about a pattern, whether the pattern belongs to a person, a machine or the weather.

～がち · Formation

The result is a な-adjective: がちだ as a predicate, がちな before a noun, がちに before a verb.

Attaches to Form Example
Verb (ます-stem) ます-stem + がち 忘(わす)れます → 忘(わす)れがち
Verb (ます-stem of a する-verb) Noun + し + がち 無理(むり)します → 無理(むり)しがち
Noun (short fixed list) Noun + がち 病気(びょうき) → 病気(びょうき)がち

Before a noun ～がち + な + Noun 見落(みお)としがちな点(てん)

Note - with verbs, がち is broadly productive. With nouns it is not: the usable list is essentially 病気(びょうき)がち, 曇(くも)りがち, 留守(るす)がち, 遠慮(えんりょ)がち, 伏(ふ)し目(め)がち, and the lexicalised ありがち ("all too common"). Inventing new noun combinations - ❌ 元気(げんき)がち, ❌雨(あめ)がち - does not work.

～がち · Examples

冬(ふゆ)は運動不足(うんどうぶそく)になりがちだ。
In winter you end up short of exercise.

弟(おとうと)は小(ちい)さいころ病気(びょうき)がちでした。
My little brother was ill a lot when he was small.

今日(きょう)は曇(くも)りがちで、洗(あら)ったシーツがなかなか乾(かわ)かない。
It's cloudy on and off today, and the sheets I washed just won't dry.

忙(いそが)しいと、朝(あさ)ごはんを抜(ぬ)きがちになる。
When you're busy you get into the habit of skipping breakfast.

彼(かれ)は遠慮(えんりょ)がちに手(て)を挙(あ)げて、小(ちい)さな声(こえ)で質問(しつもん)した。
He raised his hand hesitantly and asked his question in a small voice.

～がち · Examples (cont.)

メールの返事(へんじ)を後回(あとまわ)しにすると、そのまま忘(わす)れがちだ。
If you put off replying to an email, you tend to forget about it altogether.

説明書(せつめいしょ)の細(こま)かい注意(ちゅうい)は見落(みお)としがちなところなので、声(こえ)に出(だ)して読(よ)んでください。
The small print in the manual is the sort of thing people miss, so read it aloud.

一人(ひとり)暮(ぐ)らしを始(はじ)めてから、野菜(やさい)が足(た)りなくなりがちで、自分(じぶん)でも反省(はんせい)している。
Since I started living alone I keep ending up short on vegetables, and I'm not proud of it.

～がち · Nuance & register

The judgement built into がち is the thing to remember. Every ordinary use points at something the speaker would rather did not keep happening: illness, absence, oversight, delay, cloud. Turn it on a good outcome and the sentence collapses - ❌成功(せいこう)しがち, ❌元気(げんき)になりがち - not because the grammar breaks but because the word carries a frown that has nowhere to land.

The frown can be aimed at yourself, and often is: 私(わたし)も 無理(むり)しがちなので気(き)をつけます is a standard, slightly rueful thing to say. It can also be aimed at people in general, which is where the pattern earns its keep in writing - ～と考(かんが)えがちだが, "we tend to think that, but", is a staple opening move in essays and newspaper columns.

～がち · Nuance & register (cont.)

Register is neutral. がち appears in conversation, in business email and in print without shifting tone. Two combinations have drifted into fixed vocabulary and no longer feel like grammar at all: 遠慮(えんりょ)がち ("diffident, hanging back") and ありがち ("the sort of thing that happens all the time" - usually said of a predictable plot or a familiar mistake).

Finally, がち needs repetition. It cannot describe one event. Reporting a single late train as 遅(おく)れがちだった is wrong in the same way that saying "the train tends to be late" about one specific morning is wrong in English.

～がち · ～がち vs ～やすい

Rule of thumb - やすい is about how easily something can happen, and can be good or bad. がち is about how often it does happen, and is always bad.

N4 taught やすい as "easy to do". Its second sense, "liable to", is what overlaps here - and the two still split cleanly:

この皿(さら)は薄(うす)いので割(わ)れやすい。
These plates are thin, so they break easily.

息子(むすこ)にまかせると、皿(さら)を割(わ)りがちだ。
If I leave it to my son, he's forever breaking plates.

～がち · ～がち vs ～やすい (cont.)

The first is a property of the plates that would hold even if nobody ever broke one. The second counts actual breakages and blames somebody for them. This is also why やすい is happy in praise - 使(つか)いやすい, 分(わ)かりやすい - and がち never is.

They also build different words: やすい makes an い-adjective （割(わ)れやすかった, 割(わ)れやすくない）, がち a な-adjective （割(わ)りがちだった, 割(わ)りがちな人(ひと)）.

～がち · ～がち vs ～ぎみ

Rule of thumb - がち counts occasions; ぎみ measures amount. If you could answer with "three times a week", it is がち. If you could answer with "just a little", it is ぎみ.

彼(かれ)は去年(きょねん)から会社(かいしゃ)を休(やす)みがちだ。
He's been off work a lot since last year.

今日(きょう)は少(すこ)し疲(つか)れぎみなので、残業(ざんぎょう)はやめておく。
I'm a bit worn out today, so I'll skip the overtime.

Some verbs take both, and then the difference is unmistakable. 太(ふと)りぎみ means I am currently a couple of kilos heavier than I should be; 太(ふと)りがち means I am the kind of person who puts weight on again and again. Only one of those is a comment about a body shape, and only one is a comment about a life.

～がち · ～がち vs ことが多(おお)い

Rule of thumb - ことが多(おお)い reports frequency as a neutral fact. がち reports the same frequency and disapproves of it.

冬(ふゆ)は山(やま)に雪(ゆき)が降(ふ)ることが多(おお)い。
It often snows in the mountains in winter.

That sentence is a piece of information. Nothing is wrong with the snow. Swap in がち and you have implied that the snow is a nuisance - fine if you are a driver, odd in a geography textbook.

The neutral phrase is therefore the safe default for objective writing, and it takes a whole clause rather than a stem, so it works with verbs がち cannot reach: 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)ることが多(おお)い is fine, while ❌雨(あめ)がちだ is not Japanese.

～がち · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 遅(おく)れるがち → ✅ 遅(おく)れがち
    がち takes the ます-stem, not the dictionary form.

  • ❌ 病気(びょうき)がち子(こ)ども → ✅ 病気(びょうき)がちな子(こ)ども
    The result is a な-adjective, so it needs な before a noun.

  • ❌ 今日(きょう)の電車(でんしゃ)は遅(おく)れがちだった。（about one morning） → ✅ 今日(きょう)の電車(でんしゃ)は遅(おく)れていた。
    がち requires a repeated pattern. One late train is just a late train.

  • ❌ 最近(さいきん)よく眠(ねむ)れて、元気(げんき)になりがちです。 → ✅ 最近(さいきん)よく眠(ねむ)れて、元気(げんき)になってきました。
    がち only fits tendencies the speaker regrets. Nobody regrets getting healthier.

～がち · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 雨(あめ)がちな天気(てんき) → ✅ 雨(あめ)が降(ふ)りがちな天気(てんき)
    Only a handful of nouns take がち directly. 曇(くも)り is on the list; 雨(あめ) is not, so go through the verb.

～だらけ - covered all over

Meaning · with X all over it; caked in X

～だらけ attaches to a noun and says that the stuff named is all over the surface of something, in an amount nobody wanted. Mud on a coat, dust on a shelf, scratches on a table. The word is a complaint before it is a description.

～だらけ - covered all over · Formation

だらけ attaches to a bare noun - no particle in front of it - and the result is itself a noun. It predicates with だ/です, modifies a following noun with の, and pairs constantly with なる.

Attaches to Form Example
Noun Noun + だらけ 泥(どろ) → 泥(どろ)だらけ
Predicate ～だらけ + だ / です 机(つくえ)がほこりだらけだ

Becoming that way ～だらけ + に + なる 砂(すな)だらけになる

Note - だらけ never attaches to a verb or an adjective, and never takes な before a noun. It is also not freely productive in the way it looks: the noun has to name something you would rather were not there. 泥(どろ)・砂(すな)・血(ち)・ほこり・傷(きず)・毛(け)・しわ are all natural; ❌花(はな)だらけ and ❌光(ひかり)だらけ are not, unless you are being pointedly sarcastic.

子(こ)どもの服(ふく)が泥(どろ)だらけになった。
The kids' clothes ended up covered in mud.

The top of the shelf is thick with dust.

I fell over and my hands ended up covered in blood.

When I came out of the sea I was covered in sand from head to foot.

猫(ねこ)を抱(だ)き上(あ)げたら、コートが毛(け)だらけになった。
I picked the cat up and my coat ended up covered in fur.

工事(こうじ)が終(お)わったばかりの部屋(へや)は、窓(まど)わくまで白(しろ)い粉(こな)だらけだった。
The room where the work had just finished was covered in white dust, right up to the window frames.

My grandfather's hands were covered in scars - you could tell at once he'd worked the fields for years.

雨(あめ)の中(なか)を走(はし)ってきたらしく、弟(おとうと)は靴(くつ)も鞄(かばん)も泥(どろ)だらけのまま玄関(げんかん)に立(た)っていた。
He must have run through the rain - my brother was standing in the entryway with his shoes and bag still caked in mud.

～だらけ - covered all over · Nuance & register

だらけ is evaluative. Even when the fact reported is neutral, the word supplies the reaction: dismay, disgust, or a weary "look at the state of this". A parent says 泥(どろ)だらけ about a child's trousers with the sigh already in it.

It is also hyperbolic. 傷(きず)だらけ does not mean every square centimetre is scarred; it means enough of them that the scars are what you notice. Nobody counts.

Register is ordinary spoken and written Japanese. It is not slangy, but it is blunt, so in a formal report or a polite complaint to a company you would soften it - 汚(よご)れがひどい状態(じょうたい)でした rather than 泥(どろ)だらけでした. Between friends and inside a family it is completely standard.

～だらけ - covered all over · Nuance & register (cont.)

Note the shape of the sentence: what is covered is the subject, and what covers it goes in front of だらけ with nothing between. 机(つくえ)がほこりだらけだ - the desk is the topic of the complaint, the dust is the incriminating substance. Learners often reverse this and produce ❌ほこりが机(つくえ)だらけだ, which says the dust is covered in desks.

～だらけ - covered all over · ～だらけ vs ～まみれ

Rule of thumb - まみれ is for a liquid or a fine powder smeared over a surface. だらけ is for many separate things scattered all over, and it also reaches abstract nouns, which まみれ cannot.

Where the substance is genuinely a coating, both work, and まみれ is the more vivid, slightly more literary of the two:

工場(こうじょう)から出(で)てきた作業員(さぎょういん)は、顔(かお)まで油(あぶら)まみれだった。
The worker who came out of the factory was covered in oil right up to his face.

台所(だいどころ)の床(ゆか)が油(あぶら)だらけで、滑(すべ)りそうだった。
The kitchen floor was greasy all over and felt like it would be slippery.

～だらけ - covered all over · ～だらけ vs ～まみれ (cont.)

The line shows itself at the edges. Sweat forms a film, so 汗(あせ)まみれ is normal and ❌汗(あせ)だらけ is odd. Scars are countable marks, so 傷(きず)だらけ is normal and ❌傷(きず)まみれ is odd. Dust, mud and blood sit in the middle and take either - with 血(ち)まみれ sounding like a crime scene and 血(ち)だらけ sounding like a kitchen accident.

まみれ extends metaphorically to only a very small set of fixed expressions （借金(しゃっきん)まみれ, 罪(つみ)にまみれる）, always with the image of being smeared. It does not take scattered abstract defects: ❌間違(まちが)いまみれ, ❌穴(あな)まみれ.

～だらけ - covered all over · ～だらけ vs ～っぽい

Rule of thumb - だらけ says the stuff is physically there, all over it. っぽい says the thing merely has the quality of the stuff.

The clearest pair is dust, because both are everyday complaints about a room:

使(つか)っていない部屋(へや)はほこりだらけで、まず掃除(そうじ)から始(はじ)めた。
The unused room was thick with dust, so we started with the cleaning.

窓(まど)を閉(し)めきっていたので、部屋(へや)がほこりっぽくて息苦(いきぐる)しい。
We'd kept the windows shut, so the room feels dusty and stuffy.

The first is about what is lying on the surfaces; you could photograph it. The second is about how the air feels; you could not. Grammatically they are unrelated too - だらけ builds a noun, っぽい an い-adjective - so the endings never look alike: ほこりだらけだった against ほこりっぽかった.

～だらけ - covered all over · ～だらけ vs ～がついている

Rule of thumb - ～がついている reports that some of the stuff is on there. だらけ reports that it is everywhere on there. The difference is amount, and with it, tone.

シャツに醤油(しょうゆ)がついているよ。
You've got soy sauce on your shirt.

試合(しあい)のあと、ユニフォームは泥(どろ)だらけだった。
After the match the uniform was caked in mud.

The first is a helpful heads-up about one spot. The second is a verdict on the whole garment. This is why ついている is the polite way to tell a stranger about a stain and だらけ is not: pointing at someone's jacket and saying 泥(どろ)だらけですよ makes it sound as though they had rolled in a field.

～だらけ - covered all over · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 泥(どろ)がだらけ → ✅ 泥(どろ)だらけ
    Nothing goes between the noun and だらけ - no が, no で, no の.

  • ❌ 泥(どろ)だらけな靴(くつ) → ✅ 泥(どろ)だらけの靴(くつ)
    The result is a noun, not a な-adjective, so it links with の.

  • ❌ 部屋(へや)が汚(よご)れているだらけだ。 → ✅ 部屋(へや)がごみだらけだ。
    だらけ attaches only to nouns. A verb or an adjective in front of it is not possible.

  • ❌ 汗(あせ)だらけになって走(はし)っていた → ✅ 汗(あせ)まみれになって走(はし)っていた
    Sweat is a film over the skin, not a scatter of separate marks, so it takes まみれ. 汗(あせ)びっしょり is the everyday alternative.

  • ❌ 庭(にわ)が花(はな)だらけできれいだ。 → ✅ 庭(にわ)が花(はな)でいっぱいできれいだ。
    だらけ cannot be used approvingly. Saying it about flowers turns a pretty garden into an infestation.

説明(せつめい)は分(わ)かりやすかったが、計画(けいかく)自体(じたい)は問題(もんだい)だらけだと思(おも)う。
The explanation was clear, but the plan itself strikes me as riddled with problems.

夏(なつ)の終(お)わりに山小屋(やまごや)へ行(い)ったら、窓(まど)の内側(うちがわ)が虫(むし)だらけで、中(なか)に入(はい)る気(き)になれなかった。
When we got to the mountain hut at the end of summer the inside of the windows was crawling with insects, and I couldn't face going in.

一年(いちねん)手(て)を入(い)れていない庭(にわ)は雑草(ざっそう)だらけで、どこから片(かた)づければいいのか分(わ)からない。
The garden hasn't been touched for a year and is choked with weeds; I've no idea where to start.

～だらけ - full of · Nuance & register

This sense of だらけ is a verdict. Calling a document 間違(まちが)いだらけ is not the same as saying it contains errors; it is saying the document is not worth reading in its present state. Aimed at a colleague's work it is genuinely rude, and Japanese office language has softer replacements - 間違(まちが)いが多(おお)いようです, 少(すこ)し直(なお)すところがあります. Aimed at your own work it is a normal, slightly theatrical form of apology.

Like the physical sense it is hyperbolic: 穴(あな)だらけ needs three or four holes, not fifty. And it is saturating, not gradable, so it does not take degree adverbs. ❌とても間違(まちが)いだらけ is impossible for the same reason "very full of holes" is odd in English - the phrase already means "as many as matters".

～だらけ - full of · Nuance & register (cont.)

One extension worth knowing because it looks like an exception: まみれ, which otherwise refuses abstract nouns, does take 借金(しゃっきん) - 借金(しゃっきん)まみれ is as common as 借金(しゃっきん)だらけ. The image there is of debt smeared over a person like grease, which is why that one combination works and ❌間違(まちが)いまみれ does not.

Register: everyday spoken and written Japanese, blunt but not vulgar. Headlines and opinion columns use it freely; contracts and formal reports do not.

～だらけ - full of · ～だらけ vs ～ばかり

Rule of thumb - ばかり says nothing but X, to the exclusion of other things. だらけ says X everywhere you look, and adds a complaint.

弟(おとうと)は肉(にく)ばかり食(た)べて、野菜(やさい)に手(て)をつけない。
My brother eats nothing but meat and won't touch the vegetables.

冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)の中(なか)が古(ふる)い食(た)べ物(もの)だらけで、捨(す)てるところから始(はじ)めた。
The fridge was full of old food, so I started by throwing things out.

ばかり sets up a contrast with what is absent, and it is neutral about whether that is good. You can say 本(ほん)ばかり読(よ)んでいる about a studious child admiringly. だらけ has no such flexibility, and it never comments on absence - only on how much of the offending thing is present.

～だらけ - full of · ～だらけ vs ～ばかり (cont.)

They also fit differently into a sentence. ばかり follows verbs, particles and quantities as well as nouns（見(み)てばかりいる, 東京(とうきょう)にばかり）; だらけ follows a bare noun and nothing else.

～だらけ - full of · ～だらけ vs ～でいっぱい

Rule of thumb - でいっぱい reports a container being full, with no judgement, and works for good things. だらけ reports a mess.

会場(かいじょう)は開演(かいえん)前(まえ)から人(ひと)でいっぱいだった。
The hall was packed with people well before the performance began.

駅前(えきまえ)は人(ひと)だらけで、前(まえ)に進(すす)めなかった。
The area in front of the station was jammed with people and I couldn't get through.

Both describe crowds; only the second is annoyed about it. That is why でいっぱい is the one that can carry positive nouns - 幸(しあわ)せでいっぱい, 花(はな)でいっぱいの公園(こうえん) - while だらけ cannot go near them.

Note the particle: でいっぱい takes で before it, だらけ takes nothing.

～だらけ - full of · ～だらけ vs ～が多(おお)い

Rule of thumb - 多(おお)い is a gradable, neutral measurement. だらけ is an ungradable verdict. Anywhere you need to be polite or precise, use 多(おお)い.

この報告書(ほうこくしょ)は数字(すうじ)の間違(まちが)いが少(すこ)し多(おお)いようです。
There seem to be rather a lot of numerical errors in this report.

自分(じぶん)で読(よ)み返(かえ)したら、一(いち)枚目(まいめ)から間違(まちが)いだらけだった。
When I read it back myself, it was riddled with mistakes from page one.

The first sentence can be handed to the person who wrote the report. The second cannot - but it is exactly right for talking about your own draft. The grammatical consequence of the difference is that 多(おお)い takes degree words （少(すこ)し多(おお)い, とても多(おお)い）and だらけ refuses them.

～だらけ - full of · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 間違(まちが)いがだらけの作文(さくぶん) → ✅ 間違(まちが)いだらけの作文(さくぶん)
    No particle before だらけ, and の after it when a noun follows.

  • ❌ この町(まち)は静(しず)かだらけだ。 → ✅ この町(まち)はとても静(しず)かだ。
    だらけ needs a countable or concrete noun. Adjectives and states cannot go in front of it.

  • ❌ 机(つくえ)の上(うえ)がとても本(ほん)だらけだ。 → ✅ 机(つくえ)の上(うえ)が本(ほん)だらけだ。
    The suffix already means "saturated", so it takes no degree adverb.

  • ❌ 新(あたら)しい公園(こうえん)は花(はな)だらけで、気持(きも)ちがいい。 → ✅ 新(あたら)しい公園(こうえん)は花(はな)でいっぱいで、気持(きも)ちがいい。
    Praise wants でいっぱい. だらけ would make the flowers sound like a problem the council should deal with.

～だらけ - full of · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 先生(せんせい)のレポートは間違(まちが)いだらけですね。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)のレポート、少(すこ)し直(なお)すところがあるようです。
    Grammatically fine, socially not. Save だらけ for your own work, or for things rather than people.

でいっぱい describes a container being full and is neutral, so it fits badly with an abstract defect like 問題(もんだい). だらけ supplies exactly the disapproval the second half of the sentence assumes.

～だらけ - full of · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) この辞書(じしょ)は間違(まちが)いが多(おお)い。 (b) この辞書(じしょ)は間違(まちが)いだらけだ。

～まま

Meaning · in the same state, unchanged; while still ～

まま freezes a state. Something reached a condition, nobody changed it, and whatever happens next happens with that condition still in place. Shoes still on, light still burning, town still exactly as it was ten years ago.

～まま · Formation

まま is a formal noun. What comes in front of it must be in a form that can modify a noun, which is why verbs use the た-form and not the dictionary form.

～まま · Formation (cont.)

Attaches to Form Example
Verb (past) た-form + まま 立(た)つ → 立(た)ったまま
Verb (negative) ない-form + まま 食(た)べない → 食(た)べないまま
Noun Noun + の + まま 昔(むかし) → 昔(むかし)のまま
い-adjective Plain form + まま 熱(あつ)い → 熱(あつ)いまま
な-adjective Stem + な + まま 静(しず)か → 静(しず)かなまま
Demonstrative この / その / あの + まま そのまま
As a predicate ～まま + だ / です 昔(むかし)のままです
Deliberately keeping it so ～まま + に + する / しておく そのままにしておく

～まま · Formation (cont.)

Note - the た-form here is not past tense. 立(た)ったまま does not mean "having stood in the past"; it means the standing-up has happened and the resulting posture is still current. This is why only verbs that leave a visible result work well: 着(き)たまま, つけたまま, 開(あ)けたまま. Verbs that leave nothing behind - ❌食(た)べたまま話(はな)す for "talk while eating" - produce nonsense.

～まま · Examples

靴(くつ)をはいたまま上(あ)がらないでください。
Please don't come up with your shoes on.

電気(でんき)をつけたまま寝(ね)てしまった。
I fell asleep with the light on.

この町(まち)は十年(じゅうねん)前(まえ)のままだ。
This town is exactly as it was ten years ago.

何(なに)も言(い)わないまま、彼(かれ)は部屋(へや)を出(で)て行(い)った。
He left the room without ever saying anything.

スープは熱(あつ)いまま運(はこ)んでください。
Please carry the soup while it's still hot.

～まま · Examples (cont.)

窓(まど)を開(あ)けたまま出(で)かけてしまい、一日中(いちにちじゅう)気(き)になっていた。
I went out having left the window open, and it bothered me all day.

借(か)りた本(ほん)を読(よ)まないまま返(かえ)すことになりそうだ。
It looks as though I'll end up returning the book I borrowed without having read it.

祖母(そぼ)の部屋(へや)は亡(な)くなったときのままにしてあり、机(つくえ)の上(うえ)のめがねも動(うご)かしていない。
My grandmother's room has been left just as it was when she died - even the glasses on her desk haven't been moved.

～まま · Nuance & register

まま itself is completely neutral and register-free. It appears in children's speech, in polite requests, in legal notices（そのままお待(ま)ちください） and in literary description without changing colour.

What supplies the colour is the combination. When the frozen state is one that ought to have been undone, the sentence sounds careless: 靴(くつ)をはいたまま, 電気(でんき)をつけたまま, 化粧(けしょう)をしたまま寝(ね)る. When the frozen state is one being deliberately preserved, it sounds respectful or careful: 昔(むかし)のまま, 熱(あつ)いまま. The suffix is only holding the state still; the judgement comes from what the state is.

～まま · Nuance & register (cont.)

Three connected forms are worth separating. Bare まま and ままで are largely interchangeable in the adverbial slot - 立(た)ったまま食(た)べる and 立(た)ったままで食(た)べる are both ordinary, with で a shade more explicit. ままに is different: it is literary, and it also heads a small family of fixed expressions about following someone else's lead - 言(い)われるままに書類(しょるい)にサインした, "I signed the papers just as I was told to."

Finally, そのまま is so common that it has become a word in its own right: "as is, leave it, don't touch it". そのままでいいです ("it's fine like that") is one of the most useful short sentences in the language.

～まま · ～まま vs ～ながら

Rule of thumb - ながら joins two actions happening at once. まま holds one state in place while an action happens. If both halves are things you do, you want ながら.

音楽(おんがく)を聞(き)きながら宿題(しゅくだい)をしている。
He's doing his homework while listening to music.

電車(でんしゃ)が込(こ)んでいて、立(た)ったまま本(ほん)を読(よ)んだ。
The train was crowded, so I read standing up.

Listening and doing homework are two ongoing activities, so ながら. Standing is not an activity you keep performing - you stand up once and then you are standing - so it takes まま. That is why ❌立(た)ちながら読(よ)む sounds wrong to a native ear, and why ❌聞(き)いたまま宿題(しゅくだい)をする does too.

～まま · ～まま vs ～ながら (cont.)

A second, purely grammatical difference: ながら needs the same subject for both halves, while まま does not care - 窓(まど)を開(あ)けたまま雨(あめ)が降(ふ)り出(だ)した is fine.

～まま · ～まま vs ～っぱなし

Rule of thumb - っぱなし is まま with blame attached, and it only attaches to verb stems. まま is neutral and attaches to nouns and adjectives too.

テレビをつけたまま、台所(だいどころ)で洗(あら)い物(もの)をしていた。
I was doing the washing-up in the kitchen with the TV left on.

テレビをつけっぱなしにして出(で)かけるのはやめてほしい。
I wish you'd stop going out and leaving the TV on.

The first simply reports the arrangement; the TV may well have been on for a reason. The second is a complaint about carelessness, and っぱなし is what carries it.

～まま · ～まま vs ～っぱなし (cont.)

There is one thing っぱなし can do that まま cannot: describe an action going on without a break. 立(た)ちっぱなしだった means "I was on my feet the whole time", which is about duration, not about a posture held during some other action. 立(た)ったまま cannot mean that.

～まま · ～ないまま vs ～ないで

Rule of thumb - ないで says you did B instead of A. ないまま says the un-done state of A persisted - usually longer than it should have.

朝(あさ)ごはんを食(た)べないで家(いえ)を出(で)た。
I left the house without eating breakfast.

朝(あさ)から何(なに)も食(た)べないまま、夜(よる)になってしまった。
I got all the way to evening without having eaten anything since morning.

The first is about one moment: at the point of leaving, breakfast had not happened. The second stretches the emptiness across a whole day and invites you to notice how long it went on. Where a stretch of time is involved - an unread book, an unanswered letter, an unresolved argument - ないまま is the natural choice and carries a faint regret that ないで does not.

～まま · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 立(た)つまま待(ま)つ → ✅ 立(た)ったまま待(ま)つ
    Verbs take the た-form. The dictionary form before まま is not possible.

  • ❌ 昔(むかし)ままの建物(たてもの) → ✅ 昔(むかし)のままの建物(たてもの)
    A noun needs の to reach まま, exactly as it does to reach any other noun.

  • ❌ 静(しず)かままだった → ✅ 静(しず)かなままだった
    A な-adjective keeps its な.

  • ❌ ご飯(はん)を食(た)べたまま話(はな)さないでください。（meaning "don't talk while eating"） → ✅ ご飯(はん)を食(た)べながら話(はな)さないでください。
    Eating is an ongoing action, not a state left behind, so it takes ながら. The た-form version would mean "don't talk in the state of having eaten", which is not a thing.

  • ❌ 窓(まど)を開(あ)けたままする → ✅ 窓(まど)を開(あ)けたままにする
    To say you are deliberately leaving something in a state, the pattern is ～ままにする or ～ままにしておく.

～っぱなし

Meaning · left in that state and never put right; kept going without a break

～っぱなし comes from 放(はな)す, "to let go", and it keeps the word's flavour. Something was set going or set open and then abandoned - the tap still running, the drawer still hanging out. A second use stretches an action across a whole span of time with no pause: on your feet the entire journey, losing every match for three years.

～っぱなし · Formation

っぱなし attaches to the ます-stem of a verb and produces a noun.

Attaches to Form Example
Verb (ます-stem) ます-stem + っぱなし 出(だ)します → 出(だ)しっぱなし
る-verb (ます-stem) ます-stem + っぱなし 開(あ)けます → 開(あ)けっぱなし
Predicate ～っぱなし + だ / です 窓(まど)が開(あ)けっぱなしだ
Before a noun ～っぱなし + の + Noun つけっぱなしのテレビ
Deliberate / accusing ～っぱなし + に + する 出(だ)しっぱなしにする
Ending up that way ～っぱなし + に + なる つけっぱなしになる

～っぱなし · Formation (cont.)

Note - two families of verb take this suffix and they give the two senses. Transitive verbs that put something into a state give the "left that way" reading: つけっぱなし・開(あ)けっぱなし・出(だ)しっぱなし・脱(ぬ)ぎっぱなし・置(お)きっぱなし. Intransitive or durative verbs give the "without a break" reading: 立(た)ちっぱなし・降(ふ)りっぱなし・鳴(な)りっぱなし・負(ま)けっぱなし・言(い)われっぱなし. Verbs outside those two groups do not take it at all - ❌食(た)べっぱなし, ❌知(し)りっぱなし.

～っぱなし · Examples

テレビをつけっぱなしにして寝(ね)てしまった。
I fell asleep with the TV left on.

水(みず)を出(だ)しっぱなしにしないでね。
Don't leave the tap running.

窓(まど)が開(あ)けっぱなしだったので、部屋(へや)がすっかり冷(ひ)えていた。
The window had been left open, so the room had gone completely cold.

東京(とうきょう)から名古屋(なごや)まで席(せき)が空(あ)かず、立(た)ちっぱなしだった。
No seat came free from Tokyo to Nagoya, so I was on my feet the whole way.

昨日(きのう)は朝(あさ)から電話(でんわ)が鳴(な)りっぱなしで、仕事(しごと)がまったく進(すす)まなかった。
The phone rang nonstop from morning yesterday and I got nothing done.

～っぱなし · Examples (cont.)

上着(うわぎ)を脱(ぬ)ぎっぱなしにしておくと、母(はは)に必(かなら)ずしかられる。
If I leave my jacket where I took it off, my mother tells me off without fail.

会議(かいぎ)では部長(ぶちょう)が話(はな)しっぱなしで、こちらは一言(ひとこと)も意見(いけん)を言(い)えなかった。
The manager talked for the whole meeting and I couldn't get a single word in.

今度(こんど)の試合(しあい)こそ勝(か)ちたい。ここ三年(さんねん)、あのチームには負(ま)けっぱなしなのだ。
I really want to win this one. We've lost to that team every single time for three years.

～っぱなし · Nuance & register

The first sense is never neutral. っぱなし is the word you use when you are annoyed, or admitting fault. Saying 電気(でんき)がつけっぱなしだ is not reporting that a light is on; it is saying somebody left it on and should not have. Parents, flatmates and office managers use it constantly, and it is one of the standard building blocks of Japanese nagging.

The second sense - "without a break" - trades blame for endurance. 立(た)ちっぱなし, 歩(ある)きっぱなし, 走(はし)りっぱなし are complaints about being worn out. 負(ま)けっぱなし and 言(い)われっぱなし add a note of being on the receiving end and doing nothing about it, which is why 言(い)われっぱなしではいられない - "I can't just take this lying down" - is a fixed expression.

～っぱなし · Nuance & register (cont.)

Register is casual to neutral. It is perfectly normal at work between colleagues, but in a written report or a formal apology you would step up to something flatter: 電源(でんげん)を切(き)り忘(わす)れておりました rather than つけっぱなしでした. The kanji spelling ～っ放(ぱな)し exists but is rare; kana is the norm.

Watch the small tsu. 出(だ)す gives 出(だ)しっぱなし, not ❌出(だ)すっぱなし; the っ belongs to the suffix and the stem stays short.

～っぱなし · ～っぱなし vs ～てある

Rule of thumb - てある says somebody did it on purpose and left it that way for a reason. っぱなし says somebody did it and couldn't be bothered to undo it. Same physical scene, opposite verdicts.

空気(くうき)を入(い)れかえるため、窓(まど)が開(あ)けてある。
The window has been left open to air the room out.

誰(だれ)も閉(し)めないので、窓(まど)が開(あ)けっぱなしだ。
Nobody ever shuts it, so the window just stays open.

Both sentences describe a window that is open because a person opened it. てある supplies a purpose - and can be followed by one, as here. っぱなし supplies negligence, and it cannot take a purpose clause without sounding sarcastic.

～っぱなし · ～っぱなし vs ～てある (cont.)

This is the single most useful thing to know about the pattern, because the English "left open" covers both and gives you no clue which to choose.

～っぱなし · ～っぱなし vs ～たまま

Rule of thumb - たまま is the neutral version. っぱなし is the same state with a complaint attached, and it is limited to verb stems.

靴(くつ)をはいたまま畳(たたみ)に上(あ)がってはいけない。
You mustn't step onto the tatami with your shoes on.

玄関(げんかん)に靴(くつ)が脱(ぬ)ぎっぱなしで、足(あし)の踏(ふ)み場(ば)もない。
Shoes have been kicked off and left all over the entryway - there's nowhere to stand.

まま will also attach to nouns and adjectives（昔(むかし)のまま, 熱(あつ)いまま）, which っぱなし never does.

～っぱなし · ～っぱなし vs ～たまま (cont.)

And only っぱなし has the "without a break" sense. 立(た)ったまま食(た)べる means eating in a standing posture; 立(た)ちっぱなしだった means being on your feet for hours. Substituting one for the other changes the sentence completely.

～っぱなし · ～っぱなし vs ～ておく

Rule of thumb - ておく is leaving something as it is because that is the useful thing to do. っぱなし is leaving it as it is because nobody thought about it.

午後(ごご)もまた使(つか)うので、パソコンはつけておきます。
I'll leave the computer on, since I'll be using it again this afternoon.

三日(みっか)もつけっぱなしにしていたら、電気代(でんきだい)が大変(たいへん)なことになる。
Leave it on for three days and the electricity bill will be horrifying.

Notice that both can appear with the same verb in the same room. The choice is entirely about how the speaker wants the action judged - which makes っぱなし a useful thing to avoid when you are talking about your own deliberate decisions.

～っぱなし · Common mistakes

  • ❌ つけるっぱなし → ✅ つけっぱなし
    The suffix takes the ます-stem. Dictionary form in front of it is not possible.

  • ❌ 立(た)ったっぱなし → ✅ 立(た)ちっぱなし
    The た-form belongs to まま, not to っぱなし.

  • ❌ 開(あ)けっぱなしな窓(まど) → ✅ 開(あ)けっぱなしの窓(まど)
    The result is a noun, so a following noun is linked with の.

  • ❌ お客(きゃく)さんのために、入口(いりぐち)のドアを開(あ)けっぱなしにしておきました。 → ✅ お客(きゃく)さんのために、入口(いりぐち)のドアを開(あ)けておきました。
    You did it on purpose and for a good reason, so ておく. っぱなし would tell your boss you were careless.

～っぱなし · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ ご飯(はん)を食(た)べっぱなしで出(で)かけた → ✅ 食器(しょっき)を出(だ)しっぱなしにして出(で)かけた
    Eating leaves no object in a state, so the verb does not take the suffix. Name the thing that was actually left - the dishes.

～てばかりいる

Meaning · do nothing but ～; be forever ～ing

～てばかりいる says a person spends their time on one activity to the exclusion of everything else - and that the speaker thinks they should be doing something different. It is a complaint dressed as a description of a schedule.

～てばかりいる · Formation

ばかり slots between the て-form and いる. The whole thing is an ordinary る-verb, so it conjugates exactly like いる.

Attaches to Form Example
Verb て-form + ばかり + いる 遊(あそ)ぶ → 遊(あそ)んでばかりいる
Polite て-form + ばかり + います 寝(ね)てばかりいます
Past て-form + ばかり + いた 泣(な)いてばかりいた
Joining a clause て-form + ばかり + いて / いても 見(み)てばかりいても
Without いる て-form + ばかり + だ 謝(あやま)ってばかりだ

～てばかりいる · Formation (cont.)

Note - ～てばかりいる and ～てばかりだ are close, and both are common. The version with いる presents the behaviour as going on right now or over a stretch of time; the version with だ is flatter, closer to a verdict on the person. Do not mix them into ❌～てばかりする, which is not a form.

～てばかりいる · Examples

弟(おとうと)は遊(あそ)んでばかりいる。
My little brother does nothing but play.

彼(かれ)は文句(もんく)を言(い)ってばかりいて、自分(じぶん)では何(なに)もしない。
He does nothing but complain and never does anything himself.

泣(な)いてばかりいても何(なに)も変(か)わらないよ。
Crying about it isn't going to change anything.

息子(むすこ)はスマホを見(み)てばかりいて、話(はな)しかけても返事(へんじ)をしない。
My son is forever staring at his phone and doesn't answer when I speak to him.

休(やす)みの日(ひ)に寝(ね)てばかりいると、かえって疲(つか)れが取(と)れない。
If you do nothing but sleep on your days off, you actually end up less rested.

～てばかりいる · Examples (cont.)

新(あたら)しい職場(しょくば)では失敗(しっぱい)してばかりで、毎日(まいにち)落(お)ち込(こ)んでいる。
At my new job I do nothing but make mistakes, and I'm down about it every day.

昔(むかし)のことを思(おも)い出(だ)してばかりいても前(まえ)に進(すす)めないので、何(なに)か新(あたら)しいことを始(はじ)めたい。
Dwelling on the past won't get me anywhere, so I want to start something new.

部長(ぶちょう)は会議(かいぎ)で反対(はんたい)してばかりいたが、自分(じぶん)の案(あん)は一(ひと)つも出(だ)さなかった。
The manager did nothing but object throughout the meeting, yet didn't offer a single idea of his own.

～てばかりいる · Nuance & register

The pattern is almost always critical. Even when the activity is one you would normally praise, てばかりいる turns it into an imbalance: 勉強(べんきょう)してばかりいる is not "such a hard worker", it is "he never comes out of his room" - worry, not admiration. If you want the approving version, use a plain progressive: よく勉強(べんきょう)しています.

Aimed at yourself it becomes self-reproach, and this is extremely common: 謝(あやま)ってばかりですみません, 迷惑(めいわく)をかけてばかりで. In that use it functions almost as an apology formula.

The casual contraction is ばっかり, and in fast speech it shortens further to ばっか: 遊(あそ)んでばっかりいないで手伝(てつだ)ってよ. That is friends and family register; keep ばかり for anything else.

～てばかりいる · Nuance & register (cont.)

One genuine ambiguity to be aware of. ばかり has a second life after the た-form meaning "just did" - 着(つ)いたばかりです, "I've only just arrived". The two are different patterns with nothing in common but the word, and the て-form versus た-form is what tells them apart. Confusing them produces sentences that are grammatical and mean the wrong thing, which is the worst kind of error.

Register is otherwise neutral: てばかりいる is at home in speech, in fiction and in a newspaper column about people who spend too long online.

～てばかりいる · ～てばかりいる vs Noun + ばかり

Rule of thumb - after a noun, ばかり restricts what you do it to. After a て-form, ばかり restricts what you do - the whole activity.

弟(おとうと)は肉(にく)ばかり食(た)べて、野菜(やさい)を残(のこ)す。
My brother eats nothing but meat and leaves the vegetables.

弟(おとうと)は朝(あさ)から食(た)べてばかりいる。
My brother has done nothing but eat since this morning.

In the first, eating is fine; the problem is the menu. In the second, eating itself is the problem - he should be doing something else with his day. The noun version is also neutral enough to be used approvingly（野菜(やさい)ばかり食(た)べている can be a compliment）, which the て-form version essentially never is.

～てばかりいる · ～てばかりいる vs ～ている

Rule of thumb - ている reports what is happening. てばかりいる reports the same thing plus "and nothing else, and I object".

娘(むすめ)は部屋(へや)でゲームをしている。
My daughter is playing a game in her room.

娘(むすめ)は部屋(へや)でゲームをしてばかりいる。
My daughter does nothing but play games in her room.

ている covers this moment or a neutral habit. てばかりいる needs a stretch of repeated behaviour, so it cannot describe a single instance: pointing at someone right now and saying 見(み)てばかりいます about one glance is wrong the way "he's forever looking" would be wrong about one look.

～てばかりいる · ～てばかりいる vs ～がち

Rule of thumb - がち says it happens often, leaving room for other things. てばかりいる says it is all that happens.

忙(いそが)しい時期(じき)は外食(がいしょく)が多(おお)くなりがちだ。
When things get busy I tend to eat out more.

一人(ひとり)暮(ぐ)らしを始(はじ)めてから、外(そと)で食(た)べてばかりいる。
Since I started living alone I've done nothing but eat out.

Both disapprove, but they measure different things - frequency against exclusivity. They also attach differently: がち takes the ます-stem and builds a な-adjective, while てばかりいる takes the て-form and stays a verb. That means only てばかりいる can carry tense and aspect of its own: 食(た)べてばかりいた, 食(た)べてばかりいるだろう.

～てばかりいる · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 遊(あそ)ぶばかりいる → ✅ 遊(あそ)んでばかりいる
    ばかり in this pattern follows the て-form, not the dictionary form.

  • ❌ 食(た)べてばかりする → ✅ 食(た)べてばかりいる
    The verb at the end is いる. している belongs to the noun pattern 心配(しんぱい)ばかりしている.

  • ❌ お昼(ひる)を食(た)べてばかりいます。（meaning "I've just eaten lunch"） → ✅ お昼(ひる)を食(た)べたばかりです。
    "Just did" is た-form + ばかり. The て-form version says you have been eating all day.

  • ❌ 娘(むすめ)は毎日(まいにち)練習(れんしゅう)してばかりいて、えらいですね。 → ✅ 娘(むすめ)さんは毎日(まいにち)よく練習(れんしゅう)していて、えらいですね。
    The pattern carries disapproval, so it cannot be used to praise someone.

～てばかりいる · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 今(いま)、彼(かれ)はこちらを見(み)てばかりいます。（about one moment） → ✅ 今(いま)、彼(かれ)はこちらを見(み)ています。
    てばかりいる needs behaviour repeated over time; it cannot report a single instant.

～てばかりいる · Practice 1

見(み)る goes into its て-form 見(み)て, then ばかり, then いる. The のに in the first half tells you a complaint is coming, which is exactly the tone this pattern supplies.

～てばかりいる · Practice 4

～てばかりいる · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 彼女(かのじょ)は最近(さいきん)よく本(ほん)を読(よ)んでいる。 (b) 彼女(かのじょ)は最近(さいきん)本(ほん)を読(よ)んでばかりいる。

(b) says reading has crowded everything else out. Even though reading is a respectable activity, the pattern makes it sound like an escape or a neglect of something: she is not going out, not studying, not talking to anyone. The speaker is worried or exasperated, and that comes entirely from てばかりいる.

～かけ / ～かける

Meaning · started and not finished; on the point of ～ing

～かけ marks an action that got under way and then stopped short of its end. With an action you chose to start, that means half-done: a half-eaten sandwich, a half-written email. With a change that happens to you or to the world, it means almost happened: nearly fell, nearly forgot, sun starting to set.

～かけ / ～かける · Formation

The suffix has a noun form, かけ, and a verb form, かける (a る-verb, from 掛(か)ける).

Attaches to Form Example
Verb (ます-stem) → noun ます-stem + かけ 読(よ)みます → 読(よ)みかけ
Before a noun ～かけ + の + Noun 読(よ)みかけの本(ほん)
Predicate ～かけ + だ / です この本(ほん)は読(よ)みかけだ
Verb (ます-stem) → verb ます-stem + かける 言(い)います → 言(い)いかける
Past - began and stopped ～かけた 言(い)いかけた
Progressive - is on the verge ～かけている 沈(しず)みかけている
Before a noun (verb form) ～かけた + Noun 消(き)えかけた字(じ)

～かけ / ～かける · Formation (cont.)

Note - the verb has to name something with an endpoint to fall short of. 読(よ)む, 食(た)べる, 書(か)く, 言(い)う, 落(お)ちる, 消(き)える, 沈(しず)む all qualify. States and open-ended activities do not: ❌住(す)みかけ, ❌遊(あそ)びかけ, ❌知(し)りかけ. Note also that ます-stem + かける has a separate, purely lexical life in words like 話(はな)しかける ("speak to someone") and 出(で)かける ("go out") - those are vocabulary, not this pattern.

～かけ / ～かける · Examples

食(た)べかけのパンがテーブルに置(お)いてある。
There's a half-eaten piece of bread sitting on the table.

読(よ)みかけの本(ほん)が何(なん)冊(さつ)もたまっている。
I've got several books piled up that I started and never finished.

何(なに)か言(い)いかけて、彼(かれ)は急(きゅう)に黙(だま)ってしまった。
He started to say something and then suddenly went quiet.

階段(かいだん)で転(ころ)びかけたが、手(て)すりをつかんで助(たす)かった。
I nearly fell on the stairs but grabbed the handrail and was fine.

書(か)きかけのメールを送(おく)ってしまい、あわてて謝(あやま)った。
I accidentally sent a half-written email and hurried to apologise.

～かけ / ～かける · Examples (cont.)

日(ひ)が沈(しず)みかけているので、そろそろ山(やま)を下(お)りましょう。
The sun is starting to go down, so let's head off the mountain.

祖父(そふ)の声(こえ)を忘(わす)れかけていたことに気(き)づいて、少(すこ)し悲(かな)しくなった。
I realised I'd nearly forgotten what my grandfather's voice sounded like, and it made me sad.

やりかけの仕事(しごと)を机(つくえ)に置(お)いたまま帰(かえ)ってしまったので、朝(あさ)一番(いちばん)で片(かた)づけます。
I went home leaving unfinished work on my desk, so I'll deal with it first thing in the morning.

～かけ / ～かける · Nuance & register

The noun form is a plain, everyday word - you can point at a plate and say 食(た)べかけ with no more ceremony than saying "leftovers". It does carry a faint sense of someone else's: a 飲(の)みかけのコーヒー is a cup you would rather not drink from, which is why offering one is slightly awkward.

The verb form is where the two senses separate cleanly, and the split follows the verb. With a volitional verb, かけた means the speaker began and was interrupted: 言(い)いかけた, 立(た)ち上(あ)がりかけた. With a non-volitional change, it means the process was under way and did not complete - or is still under way: 消(き)えかけた火(ひ), 倒(たお)れかけた木(き), 沈(しず)みかけている船(ふね). Fiction leans on the second heavily.

Register is neutral throughout. Nothing about かけ is casual or formal; it turns up in conversation, in instructions and in literature alike.

～かけ / ～かける · Nuance & register (cont.)

One shape worth memorising because it is so useful: 死(し)にかける → 死(し)にかけた, "came close to dying". It is the standard way to say a near-miss with something serious, and it is much more common than any construction with "almost".

～かけ / ～かける · ～かけ vs ～ている途中(とちゅう)

Rule of thumb - 途中(とちゅう) means you are in the middle of it right now. かけ means you stopped partway and are not doing it at the moment.

今(いま)読(よ)んでいる**途中(とちゅう)**なので、あとで話(はな)してもいいですか。
I'm in the middle of reading it, so can we talk later?

去年(きょねん)買(か)った小説(しょうせつ)が、まだ読(よ)みかけのままだ。
The novel I bought last year is still sitting there half-read.

The first sentence has a person actively engaged. The second has a book on a shelf and nobody reading it. This is why かけ so often appears with まま and そのまま - the state has been abandoned and left standing.

～かけ / ～かける · ～かける vs ～そうだ

Rule of thumb - そうだ predicts from appearances: it looks like it will happen. かける reports that the process actually began.

棚(たな)の本(ほん)が落(お)ちそうだから、少(すこ)し奥(おく)に入(い)れて。
The books on the shelf look like they're going to fall - push them back a bit.

棚(たな)の本(ほん)が落(お)ちかけたので、あわてて手(て)を出(だ)した。
The books started to fall, so I grabbed at them.

Nothing has moved in the first sentence; it is a judgement about a risk. In the second, the books were on their way down. Learners who reach for かける when they mean "looks like" end up reporting events that never occurred.

～かけ / ～かける · ～かけ vs ～たばかり

Rule of thumb - opposite ends of the same action. かけ = begun, not finished. たばかり = finished a moment ago.

そのケーキ、まだ食(た)べかけだから捨(す)てないで。
Don't throw that cake out - I haven't finished it.

ケーキなら、さっき食(た)べたばかりです。
If you mean cake, I've only just had some.

The forms make the difference visible: かけ sits on the ます-stem, ばかり in this sense sits on the た-form. If you can see a た, the action is over.

～かけ / ～かける · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 読(よ)むかけの本(ほん) → ✅ 読(よ)みかけの本(ほん)
    かけ attaches to the ます-stem.

  • ❌ 読(よ)みかけな本(ほん) → ✅ 読(よ)みかけの本(ほん)
    The noun form links to a following noun with の.

  • ❌ もう少(すこ)しで事故(じこ)にあいかけだった。 → ✅ もう少(すこ)しで事故(じこ)にあいかけた。
    The bare noun かけ is for objects left unfinished. The "on the verge" sense needs the verb かける, conjugated.

  • ❌ 昼(ひる)ごはんは食(た)べかけです。（meaning "I've just eaten"） → ✅ 昼(ひる)ごはんは食(た)べたばかりです。
    食(た)べかけ means the plate is still half full.

  • ❌ 三年(さんねん)この町(まち)に住(す)みかけている。 → ✅ 三年(さんねん)この町(まち)に住(す)んでいる。
    住(す)む has no endpoint to stop short of, so it does not take かける at all.

～かけ / ～かける · Practice 1

1. 机(つくえ)の上(うえ)に、書(か)き____の手紙(てがみ)が置(お)いてあった。

書(か)きかけの手紙(てがみ)が置(お)いてあった。

The letter was started and abandoned, which is the noun form on the ます-stem, linked to 手紙(てがみ) with の. 書(か)きかけ is one of the commonest examples of this pattern.

～かけ / ～かける · Practice 2

2. Choose: 自転車(じてんしゃ)が倒(たお)れ（そうだった・かけた）ので、手(て)で支(ささ)えた。

自転車(じてんしゃ)が倒(たお)れかけたので、手(て)で支(ささ)えた。

The second half says the speaker physically caught it, so the bike must already have begun to go over - that is かける. 倒(たお)れそうだった would only say it looked unstable, and would not explain the sudden catch.

～かけ / ～かける · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "Please don't leave your food half-eaten."

食(た)べかけ is the standard noun for food left partway through, and it joins the following clause with で exactly as any noun does. You could also say 食(た)べかけのまま残(のこ)さないで, which adds the sense of the plate being left standing.

～かけ / ～かける · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 彼(かれ)は何(なに)か言(い)いかけのに、途中(とちゅう)でやめてしまった。

～かけ / ～かける · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 火(ひ)が消(き)えそうだ。 (b) 火(ひ)が消(き)えかけている。

(a) is a prediction from how the fire looks - small, low, unsteady. It might still recover, and the speaker is judging from the outside.

(b) says the going-out has already started: the flame is in the process of dying, and without intervention it will finish. It reports a change in progress rather than a risk, which makes it the more urgent of the two.

～たて

Meaning · freshly ～ed; only just ～ed

～たて says a thing came out of some process moments ago and is still in that condition. Bread out of the oven, rice out of the cooker, paint still wet, a graduate still blinking at the world. The freshness is presented as a quality of the thing, and it is a quality that will fade.

～たて · Formation

たて attaches to the ます-stem and produces a noun.

Attaches to Form Example
Verb (ます-stem) ます-stem + たて 焼(や)きます → 焼(や)きたて
する-verb Noun + し + たて 卒業(そつぎょう)します → 卒業(そつぎょう)したて
Before a noun ～たて + の + Noun 焼(や)きたてのパン
Predicate ～たて + だ / です ご飯(はん)は炊(た)きたてです
Joining a clause ～たて + で 習(なら)いたてで、まだ下手(へた)だ

～たて · Formation (cont.)

Note - this is the most lexically restricted suffix in the group. Two small families use it and nothing else does. Things coming out of a process: 焼(や)きたて・揚(あ)げたて・炊(た)きたて・入(い)れたて・塗(ぬ)りたて・できたて・とれたて・生(う)まれたて. People who have just entered a status: 習(なら)いたて・覚(おぼ)えたて・卒業(そつぎょう)したて・入社(にゅうしゃ)したて・なりたて. Outside those, it simply is not said: ❌買(か)いたて, ❌読(よ)みたて, ❌見(み)たて. (The existing words 見立(みた)て, 仕立(した)て and 組(く)み立(た)て are separate vocabulary, not this suffix.)

～たて · Examples

焼(や)きたてのパンはやっぱりおいしい。
Freshly baked bread really is delicious.

ご飯(はん)は炊(た)きたてが一番(いちばん)だ。
Rice is best straight out of the cooker.

ペンキ塗(ぬ)りたてなので、さわらないでください。
The paint is still wet, so please don't touch.

市場(いちば)でとれたての魚(さかな)を買(か)って帰(かえ)った。
I bought some freshly caught fish at the market and took it home.

入(い)れたてのコーヒーのにおいで目(め)が覚(さ)めた。
The smell of freshly brewed coffee woke me up.

～たて · Examples (cont.)

彼(かれ)は運転(うんてん)免許(めんきょ)をとりたてで、まだ高速(こうそく)道路(どうろ)が怖(こわ)いらしい。
He's only just got his driving licence and is apparently still nervous of the motorway.

覚(おぼ)えたての敬語(けいご)を使(つか)おうとして、かえって変(へん)な日本語(にほんご)になってしまった。
I tried to use the keigo I'd just learned and ended up with odder Japanese than before.

この店(みせ)は揚(あ)げたてのコロッケを一(ひと)つずつ紙(かみ)に包(つつ)んでくれるので、帰(かえ)り道(みち)でつい食(た)べてしまう。
This shop wraps each freshly fried croquette in paper for you, so I always end up eating one on the way home.

～たて · Nuance & register

たて is a warm, appetising word, and it is the only suffix in this group that is reliably positive. Shops and menus lean on it - 焼(や)きたてパン, できたてのお弁当(べんとう) - because it promises the best version of the thing. The two combinations 焼(や)きたて and できたて are so entrenched that they function as ordinary nouns.

The freshness has a short shelf life built in, and this is what makes it different from "new". Bread is 焼(や)きたて for an hour, not a week. A person is 卒業(そつぎょう)したて for a few months. If the interval you mean is long, the word is wrong.

Applied to people, the tone is affectionate but a little indulgent: 新入社員(しんにゅうしゃいん)なりたて and 習(なら)いたて both carry "and it shows". It is not an insult, but it is not a compliment on competence either.

～たて · Nuance & register (cont.)

There is no register restriction - 塗(ぬ)りたて appears on public signs, 揚(あ)げたて on menus, 覚(おぼ)えたて in casual conversation. Some combinations take the kanji 立(た)て in writing（焼(や)き立(た)て）, but kana is far more usual.

～たて · ～たて vs ～たばかり

Rule of thumb - たばかり works with any verb and measures time since an event. たて works with a short fixed list and describes the thing as being in a fresh condition.

この車(くるま)は先週(せんしゅう)買(か)ったばかりだ。
I only bought this car last week.

朝(あさ)六時(ろくじ)の店(みせ)には、焼(や)きたてのパンが並(なら)んでいた。
At six in the morning the shop had freshly baked bread lined up.

Where both are available, they say slightly different things. 焼(や)いたばかりのパン tells you when the baking happened; 焼(や)きたてのパン tells you what the bread is like. The second is what you want on a sign, and the first is what you want in a sentence about your morning.

～たて · ～たて vs ～たばかり (cont.)

The safe rule for production: if the combination is not one of the fixed set, use たばかり. ❌買(か)いたての車(くるま) is not Japanese; 買(か)ったばかりの車(くるま) is.

～たて · ～たて vs 新(あたら)しい

Rule of thumb - 新(あたら)しい means not old. たて means straight out of the process that made it, with the heat still in it.

新(あたら)しいパンを二(ふた)つください。
Two of the fresh loaves, please.

昼(ひる)すぎに行(い)くと、もう焼(や)きたては残(のこ)っていない。
If you go after midday, there's nothing left that's just out of the oven.

新(あたら)しい covers anything baked today, and it would still be true tomorrow morning relative to last week's loaf. たて is a claim about minutes. This is also why 新(あたら)しい can describe things that were never made - 新(あたら)しい山(やま)の道(みち) - while たて needs a process and an agent.

～たて · ～たて vs ～かけ

Rule of thumb - the two ends of one action. かけ = begun and abandoned partway; たて = just completed.

飲(の)みかけのお茶(ちゃ)が、まだテーブルに残(のこ)っていた。
There was a half-drunk cup of tea still on the table.

入(い)れたてのお茶(ちゃ)を、来客(らいきゃく)に出(だ)した。
I served the visitors tea I'd just made.

Structurally they are twins: both sit on the ます-stem, both produce a noun, both take の before a following noun. The difference is entirely one of where in the action they point - and, as a result, of mood. A 飲(の)みかけ cup is a small domestic mess; an 入(い)れたて cup is hospitality.

～たて · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 焼(や)くたてのパン → ✅ 焼(や)きたてのパン
    たて attaches to the ます-stem, not the dictionary form.

  • ❌ 焼(や)きたてなパン → ✅ 焼(や)きたてのパン
    The result is a noun and links with の.

  • ❌ 買(か)いたてのパソコン → ✅ 買(か)ったばかりのパソコン
    Buying is not a process that leaves a thing freshly made, and 買(か)いたて is not used. The productive pattern is たばかり.

  • ❌ 去年(きょねん)卒業(そつぎょう)したてなので、まだ仕事(しごと)に慣(な)れていません。 → ✅ 先月(せんげつ)卒業(そつぎょう)したてなので、まだ仕事(しごと)に慣(な)れていません。
    The freshness must be genuinely recent. A year is too long for たて; use 去年(きょねん)卒業(そつぎょう)したばかり if you really mean a year ago.

～たて · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ この服(ふく)は洗(あら)いたてで古(ふる)いです。 → ✅ この服(ふく)は洗(あら)いたてでいいにおいがします。
    たて is a positive word about a fresh state. Pairing it with a negative assessment of the same thing pulls the sentence in two directions.

焼(や)く gives the stem 焼(や)き, and 焼(や)きたて is the most standard member of this small family. Note the の linking it to パン.

He has joined - the process is complete and he is fresh in the role, which is たて. 入社(にゅうしゃ)しかけ would mean he started joining and stopped partway, which is not a situation that exists.

～たて · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) できたてのカレーを出(だ)してくれた。 (b) 作(つく)ったばかりのカレーを出(だ)してくれた。

～きる

Meaning · do all of it, right to the end; be completely in a state

～きる (from 切(き)る, "to cut") takes a verb and adds completion with nothing left over. Not merely finishing, but getting through the whole quantity: the last page, the last spoonful, the last yen. A second use pushes a state to its limit - utterly exhausted, thoroughly cold, perfectly obvious.

～きる · Formation

きる attaches to the ます-stem and produces a verb. Because it is 切(き)る, it conjugates as an う-verb (a Group 1 / 五段(ごだん) verb), despite ending in -る.

Attaches to Form Example
Verb (ます-stem) ます-stem + きる 食(た)べます → 食(た)べきる
する-verb Noun + し + きる 整理(せいり)します → 整理(せいり)しきる
Past ～きった 読(よ)みきった

Negative (う-verb!) ～きらない 食(た)べきらない

～きる · Formation (cont.)

Note - the completion sense needs a quantity or a course to run to the end of: a plateful, a book, a savings account, a race. The intensifying sense needs a verb of change of state: 疲(つか)れる, 冷(ひ)える, 弱(よわ)る, 澄(す)む, 困(こま)る. Verbs with neither - ❌見(み)きる in the sense "look completely", ❌住(す)みきる - do not take it.

～きる · Examples

大(おお)きなカレーを一人(ひとり)で食(た)べきった。
I finished off a huge plate of curry by myself.

今月(こんげつ)のお金(かね)を、もう使(つか)いきってしまった。
I've already spent all of this month's money.

長(なが)い小説(しょうせつ)を三日(みっか)で読(よ)みきった。
I got through a long novel in three days.

最後(さいご)まで走(はし)りきった選手(せんしゅ)に、大(おお)きな拍手(はくしゅ)が送(おく)られた。
The runners who made it all the way to the finish got a big round of applause.

引(ひ)っ越(こ)しの片(かた)づけで疲(つか)れきって、夕(ゆう)ご飯(はん)も食(た)べずに寝(ね)た。
I was completely worn out from unpacking after the move and went to bed without dinner.

～きる · Examples (cont.)

そんなことは分(わ)かりきった話(はなし)だから、わざわざ言(い)わなくていい。
That's blindingly obvious, so there's no need to spell it out.

冬(ふゆ)の朝(あさ)、冷(ひ)えきった部屋(へや)でストーブをつけるのは、なかなかつらい。
On winter mornings, lighting the heater in a room that's gone stone cold is pretty grim.

三年(さんねん)分(ぶん)の資料(しりょう)を一(いっ)週間(しゅうかん)で整理(せいり)しきるのは、さすがに無理(むり)だと思(おも)います。
Getting three years of documents completely sorted in one week honestly isn't going to happen.

～きる · Nuance & register

The completion sense carries a note of achievement or effort. 読(よ)みきった is not a neutral "I finished reading"; it says the book was long and you got to the end of it. This is why the pattern is common in sports reporting and in talking about anything demanding - 走(はし)りきる, やりきる, 戦(たたか)いきる.

The intensifying sense produces a small set of very common ～きった adjectives. 疲(つか)れきった, 困(こま)りきった, 冷(ひ)えきった, 澄(す)みきった空(そら), 分(わ)かりきったこと. These feel a shade literary before a noun and are entirely ordinary in speech as predicates （もう疲(つか)れきっています）.

～きる · Nuance & register (cont.)

Register is neutral. Several combinations have hardened into everyday vocabulary and no longer feel like grammar: 売(う)り切(き)れる ("sell out"), 締(し)め切(き)り ("deadline"), 思(おも)い切(き)って ("taking the plunge"). You already know those as words; this page is about the productive pattern behind them.

The conjugation trap is worth stating plainly. 切(き)る is an う-verb, so the negative is 食(た)べきらない, not ❌食(た)べきない, and the polite past is 食(た)べきりました. Learners who see the -る and treat it as a る-verb get every form wrong at once.

～きる · ～きる vs ～てしまう

Rule of thumb - てしまう is about the action being over and irreversible, often with regret. きる is about the quantity being exhausted, often with satisfaction.

冷蔵庫(れいぞうこ)のケーキを食(た)べてしまった。
I've gone and eaten the cake that was in the fridge.

大(おお)きなケーキだったが、みんなで食(た)べきった。
It was a big cake, but between us we finished the lot.

The first invites "…and I shouldn't have". The second invites "…and we're rather pleased". They stack, too, and the combination is very common: 使(つか)いきってしまった - used it all up, and now there is none left.

～きる · ～きる vs ～終(お)わる

Rule of thumb - 終(お)わる means the activity stopped because you reached the end of it. きる means the whole amount was got through.

昨日(きのう)やっとレポートを書(か)き終(お)わった。
I finally finished writing the report yesterday.

三(さん)時間(じかん)かけて、残(のこ)っていた問題(もんだい)を全部(ぜんぶ)やりきった。
I spent three hours on it and got through every one of the remaining questions.

終(お)わる is the neutral, always-available choice and attaches to almost any verb. きる adds difficulty and completeness, and it needs something countable to be complete of. That is why 書(か)き終(お)わる is unremarkable while 書(か)ききる sounds like the report nearly defeated you.

～きる · ～きる vs ～抜(ぬ)く

Rule of thumb - both mean "to the very end". きる stresses that nothing was left; 抜(ぬ)く stresses that you kept going despite hardship.

苦(くる)しかったが、最後(さいご)まで走(はし)りきった。
It hurt, but I made it all the way to the end.

どんなに反対(はんたい)されても、最後(さいご)までやり抜(ぬ)くつもりだ。
However much people object, I intend to see it through to the end.

With a race, either works and they shade differently: 走(はし)りきる is about covering the whole distance, 走(はし)り抜(ぬ)く about enduring it. With a quantity - money, food, paper - only きる fits, because there is nothing to endure. 抜(ぬ)く belongs to a later level, but the pair is worth seeing now so that きる does not get stretched to cover perseverance.

～きる · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 食(た)べるきる → ✅ 食(た)べきる
    きる attaches to the ます-stem.

  • ❌ 食(た)べきない → ✅ 食(た)べきらない
    切(き)る is an う-verb, so its negative is きらない. The same goes for きります, きって, きった.

  • ❌ 財布(さいふ)をなくしきった。 → ✅ 財布(さいふ)をなくしてしまった。
    A wallet is one object, not a quantity to work through, so there is nothing for きる to exhaust. Regret over a single completed action is てしまう.

  • ❌ この町(まち)に三十年(さんじゅうねん)住(す)みきった。 → ✅ この町(まち)に三十年(さんじゅうねん)住(す)んでいる。
    住(す)む has no endpoint, so it takes neither sense of きる.

  • ❌ 疲(つか)れきる顔(かお) → ✅ 疲(つか)れきった顔(かお)
    In the intensifying sense, the state has already been reached, so the form before a noun is the past きった.

～きれない

Meaning · there is too much of it to get through; more than one can manage

～きれない is the negative potential of 切(き)る in its "do it all" sense. It does not say you are incapable of the action - it says the amount defeats you. You can eat; there is simply more food than you can eat. You can count; there are simply more stars than you can count.

～きれない · Formation

きれない attaches to the ます-stem. It is already a negative potential, so it never takes a second potential ending.

～きれない · Formation (cont.)

Note - the affirmative きれる exists but is far less common than the negative. When you mean "I managed to finish it", the ordinary 食(た)べきった is what people actually say; 食(た)べきれた is reserved for contexts where the capacity is the point（一人(ひとり)でも食(た)べきれる量(りょう)です）. The pattern is lopsided, and it is worth learning it as a negative first.

～きれない · Examples

料理(りょうり)が多(おお)すぎて、食(た)べきれない。
There's too much food - I can't finish it.

漢字(かんじ)が多(おお)くて、一日(いちにち)では覚(おぼ)えきれない。
There are so many kanji that I can't learn them all in one day.

空(そら)には数(かぞ)えきれないほどの星(ほし)が光(ひか)っていた。
There were countless stars shining in the sky.

来週(らいしゅう)の旅行(りょこう)が楽(たの)しみで、待(ま)ちきれない。
I'm so looking forward to next week's trip that I can hardly wait.

荷物(にもつ)が多(おお)くて一度(いちど)では運(はこ)びきれないので、二回(にかい)に分(わ)けます。
There's too much to carry in one trip, so I'll split it into two.

～きれない · Examples (cont.)

お世話(せわ)になった方々(かたがた)への感謝(かんしゃ)は、言葉(ことば)では伝(つた)えきれません。
I can't put into words how grateful I am to everyone who looked after me.

資料(しりょう)が多(おお)すぎて会議(かいぎ)までに読(よ)みきれなかったので、要点(ようてん)だけまとめてもらった。
There was too much material to get through before the meeting, so I had someone summarise the key points.

一人(ひとり)では抱(かか)えきれない問題(もんだい)を、何年(なんねん)も誰(だれ)にも言(い)わずにいたそうだ。
Apparently he kept a problem too big for one person to handle to himself for years, without telling anyone.

～きれない · Nuance & register

The core meaning is capacity overrun, and it is the amount that overruns, not your skill. That distinction does real work in a sentence: 読(よ)みきれない means the book was too long, while 読(よ)めない would mean you cannot read it at all - bad eyesight, wrong language, handwriting you cannot decipher. Learners who use きれない for lack of ability end up claiming that a two-line note is too long for them.

きれない is register-neutral and it turns up constantly in one particular shape: ～きれないほど, "so many/much that you couldn't ～". 数(かぞ)えきれないほど is the standard Japanese for "countless", and 食(た)べきれないほどの料理(りょうり) is what you say about a table that groans.

～きれない · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two idiomatic uses do not follow the "too much" template, and both are worth knowing. 待(ま)ちきれない is eager anticipation - the waiting exceeds what you can bear - and it is a warm, positive expression. 信(しん)じきれない and 割(わ)りきれない (in its emotional sense) describe a feeling you cannot fully settle into; they are more literary.

In writing you will also meet the て-form きれず(に): 我慢(がまん)しきれずに笑(わら)ってしまった. That is standard written Japanese and sounds slightly formal in speech.

～きれない · ～きれない vs the plain potential negative

Rule of thumb - られない denies the ability. きれない grants the ability and denies that it is enough for the quantity.

辛(から)い物(もの)が苦手(にがて)なので、このカレーは食(た)べられない。
I'm no good with spicy food, so I can't eat this curry.

おいしいけれど量(りょう)が多(おお)くて、このカレーは食(た)べきれない。
It's delicious, but there's so much of it that I can't finish this curry.

In the first sentence the speaker cannot eat one spoonful. In the second they have been eating happily and have simply run out of room. Notice that きれない presupposes the action is under way or possible - which is why it fits so naturally with a half-empty plate.

～きれない · ～きれない vs ～すぎる

Rule of thumb - すぎる describes the thing: there is too much of it. きれない describes the person: they cannot get to the end of it.

今日(きょう)の宿題(しゅくだい)は多(おお)すぎる。
There's far too much homework today.

今日(きょう)の宿題(しゅくだい)は夜(よる)までにやりきれない。
I won't get through today's homework by tonight.

They are two views of the same problem and they combine constantly - the commonest frame in the whole pattern is ～すぎて～きれない: 多(おお)すぎて食(た)べきれない. Using both is not redundant; the first names the cause and the second the consequence.

～きれない · ～きれない vs ～きった

Rule of thumb - these are the two halves of one pattern. きった = got through the whole lot. きれない = could not.

大(おお)きなピザだったが、三人(さんにん)で食(た)べきった。
It was a big pizza, but the three of us polished it off.

大(おお)きなピザで、二人(ふたり)では食(た)べきれなかった。
It was a big pizza, and the two of us couldn't finish it.

The asymmetry to remember is that the affirmative side normally uses the plain form 食(た)べきる / 食(た)べきった, not the potential 食(た)べきれる, while the negative side almost always uses the potential 食(た)べきれない rather than the plain 食(た)べきらない. Both plain negatives exist, but they sound deliberate - "doesn't finish it" rather than "can't finish it".

～きれない · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 食(た)べるきれない → ✅ 食(た)べきれない
    The suffix goes on the ます-stem.

  • ❌ 食(た)べきれられない → ✅ 食(た)べきれない
    きれない is already the potential negative. Adding another potential ending doubles it.

  • ❌ この字(じ)は小(ちい)さくて読(よ)みきれない。 → ✅ この字(じ)は小(ちい)さくて読(よ)めない。
    Small print is a problem of ability, not of quantity. きれない needs an amount to fall short of.

  • ❌ 数(かぞ)えきれないの星(ほし) → ✅ 数(かぞ)えきれないほどの星(ほし)
    きれない is a negative い-adjective form, so it modifies a noun directly（数(かぞ)えきれない星(ほし)）or through ほどの. It never takes a bare の.

～きれない · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ 来週(らいしゅう)のライブが待(ま)ちきれる! → ✅ 来週(らいしゅう)のライブが待(ま)ちきれない!
    The excited "I can't wait!" is the negative. The affirmative would mean you are perfectly capable of waiting, which rather kills the mood.

～がたい

Meaning · hard to bring oneself to ～; almost impossible to ～

～がたい (from 難(かた)い, voiced to がたい as a suffix) marks a difficulty that lives in the mind, not in the hands. Something is hard to believe, hard to accept, hard to part with, hard to put into words. It is not about a task being physically awkward - for that Japanese has a different suffix entirely.

～がたい · Formation

がたい attaches to the ます-stem and produces an い-adjective: 信(しん)じがたい → 信(しん)じがたかった → 信(しん)じがたく → no だ.

Note - this suffix is not productive. It attaches to a short list of verbs of thinking, feeling, saying and judging: 信(しん)じる・理解(りかい)する・想像(そうぞう)する・忘(わす)れる・捨(す)てる・言(い)う・認(みと)める・近寄(ちかよ)る・動(うご)かす・得(え)る・耐(た)える. His story is hard to believe.

The year abroad turned into an experience I'll never forget.

Both proposals have something going for them, so let me think a bit longer.

なぜ彼(かれ)があんな嘘(うそ)をついたのか、どうしても理解(りかい)しがたい。
I really cannot understand why he told a lie like that.

The manager comes across as a bit unapproachable, but he's kind once you talk to him.

～がたい · Examples (cont.)

今(いま)の段階(だんかい)では、この計画(けいかく)が成功(せいこう)したとは言(い)いがたい。
At this stage it would be hard to call the project a success.

信頼(しんらい)できる同僚(どうりょう)は得(え)がたい存在(そんざい)だと、転職(てんしょく)して初(はじ)めて気(き)づいた。
It took changing jobs for me to see how rare a colleague you can trust really is.

数字(すうじ)がこれだけそろっているのだから、この結論(けつろん)は動(うご)かしがたいだろう。
With this many figures lined up, this conclusion is going to be hard to shift.

～がたい · Nuance & register

がたい is noticeably formal. It belongs to written Japanese, prepared speech, news copy and careful argument. In everyday conversation the same idea comes out as 信(しん)じられない, ちょっとわからない, 捨(す)てるのはもったいない. Saying 信(しん)じがたいですね in a chat with a friend is not wrong, but it lands like "that strains credulity" in English - correct, and a little theatrical.

The kind of difficulty it names is psychological or principled. A conclusion that is 動(うご)かしがたい is not heavy; it is well supported. A person who is 近寄(ちかよ)りがたい is not physically distant; they are intimidating. Note that this is a comment on how they come across and not necessarily a criticism - 近寄(ちかよ)りがたい often shades into "commands respect".

The suffix is also useful for hedged negatives, and this is where business Japanese likes it. 成功(せいこう)したとは言(い)いがたい means "I would not go so far as to call it a success" - a way of saying it failed without saying it failed. 賛成(さんせい)しがたい softens a refusal in the same way.

～がたい · Nuance & register (cont.)

Two lexical footnotes. ありがたい ("grateful, welcome") comes from this suffix historically but is now simply a word, and it is not built the same way. And the reading voices: がたい, not かたい. 固(かた)い and 硬(かた)い are unrelated adjectives about hardness.

～がたい · ～がたい vs ～にくい

Rule of thumb - にくい is practical difficulty and attaches to almost any verb. がたい is psychological or moral difficulty and attaches to a short fixed list. When in doubt, にくい is nearly always the right answer.

この靴(くつ)は小(ちい)さすぎて歩(ある)きにくい。
These shoes are too small and hard to walk in.

事故(じこ)のことは家族(かぞく)には言(い)いがたい。
It's hard to bring myself to tell my family about the accident.

Where a verb takes both, the difference is exactly this. 言(い)いにくい is awkward to pronounce or awkward to raise - a practical obstacle. 言(い)いがたい is something you cannot in conscience assert. Likewise 信(しん)じにくい ("hard to credit, on the evidence") is milder than 信(しん)じがたい ("beyond belief").

～がたい · ～がたい vs ～にくい (cont.)

Register follows the same line: にくい is at home everywhere, がたい is formal. Learners producing ❌使(つか)いがたいアプリ for "an app that's hard to use" are picking the wrong suffix twice over - wrong kind of difficulty and wrong level of formality.

～がたい · ～がたい vs ～ようがない

Rule of thumb - がたい says it is hard and you would rather not. ようがない says there is no method at all, so the question of willingness never arises.

彼(かれ)の言(い)い訳(わけ)は信(しん)じがたいが、証拠(しょうこ)もない。
His excuse is hard to believe, though there's no evidence either way.

連絡先(れんらくさき)を知(し)らないので、知(し)らせようがない。
I don't have his contact details, so there's no way to let him know.

The first speaker could believe the excuse and is declining to. The second speaker would happily make the call and cannot. That is why がたい combines naturally with が and けれど - it admits the other side - while ようがない tends to close a topic down.

～がたい · ～がたい vs ～られない

Rule of thumb - られない reports that you cannot. がたい reports that it is hard to, leaving the possibility open.

そんな話(はなし)、とても信(しん)じられない。
I simply can't believe a story like that.

報告(ほうこく)の内容(ないよう)には、いくつか信(しん)じがたい点(てん)がある。
There are several points in the report that are hard to credit.

The first is an emotional reaction, spoken, and it slams the door. The second is an assessment, written, and it leaves room for the report to turn out right after all. Choosing がたい where られない was wanted makes you sound as though you are drafting a memo in the middle of an argument; choosing られない in a formal document sounds hot-headed.

～がたい · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 信(しん)じるがたい → ✅ 信(しん)じがたい
    がたい attaches to the ます-stem.

  • ❌ 忘(わす)れがたいだ → ✅ 忘(わす)れがたい
    The result is an い-adjective. The polite form is 忘(わす)れがたいです.

  • ❌ この席(せき)は狭(せま)くて座(すわ)りがたい。 → ✅ この席(せき)は狭(せま)くて座(すわ)りにくい。
    Physical awkwardness is にくい. がたい is for difficulty of the mind.

  • ❌ しんじかたい → ✅ しんじがたい
    The suffix voices. Unvoiced かたい belongs to 固(かた)い and 硬(かた)い, which mean "hard" in the physical sense.

  • ❌ この漢字(かんじ)は読(よ)みがたい。 → ✅ この漢字(かんじ)は読(よ)みにくい。
    A character that is hard to make out is a practical problem, and 読(よ)みがたい is not a combination Japanese uses.

～ようがない / ～ようもない

Meaning · there is no way of doing it; no method exists

よう here is 様(よう), "manner, way of doing". ～ようがない therefore says that the means are missing: not that you lack the skill, not that you are unwilling, but that there is nothing available to do it with. No address, so no way to send it. No pieces left, so no way to mend it.

I don't even know his phone number, so there's no way to get in touch.

The name is written in big letters, so there's no way you can get it wrong.

There aren't any ingredients, so how exactly am I supposed to make anything?

I cannot even begin to imagine how she felt at the time.

データがすべて消(き)えてしまったので、報告書(ほうこくしょ)を書(か)きようがなく、締(し)め切(き)りを延(の)ばしてもらった。
All the data was wiped, so there was simply no way to write the report and I had the deadline pushed back.

～ようがない / ～ようもない · Nuance & register

The feeling is resignation without excuse-making. Because the sentence claims that no method exists, it removes blame from everybody: 直(なお)しようがない is not "I failed to fix it" but "it was not fixable". That makes the pattern useful for declining responsibility gracefully, and it also makes it a strong claim - if a method did exist, the sentence is simply untrue.

There is a mirror-image use that surprises learners: ようがない on a verb of error or failure produces a guarantee. 間違(まちが)えようがない means "you couldn't get it wrong if you tried"; 負(ま)けようがない, "there's no way we lose". The logic is the same - no method available - but the effect is confident rather than defeated.

～ようがない / ～ようもない · Nuance & register (cont.)

Register is neutral, leaning slightly written for ようもない. The one thing that is not neutral is the contraction. 仕方(しかた)がない has a spoken cousin しょうがない, and しょうがない is a contraction of しようがない - so the two patterns share a form. In practice, しょうがない has drifted away into a fixed phrase meaning "oh well, nothing to be done", and it is quite casual; if you mean specifically "there is no way to do it", say しようがない with the full vowel.

Note also that しょうがない人(ひと) means "hopeless case, incorrigible" - an affectionate insult, not a comment on methods.

～ようがない / ～ようもない · ～ようがない vs the potential negative

Rule of thumb - られない says I can't. ようがない says nobody can, because there is nothing to do it with.

道具(どうぐ)がないので、私(わたし)には直(なお)せない。
I haven't got the tools, so I can't fix it.

部品(ぶひん)がもう作(つく)られていないので、直(なお)しようがない。
The parts aren't made any more, so it simply can't be repaired.

In the first sentence, a repair shop could still do it. In the second, nobody can. This is why ようがない is the right choice when you want to close off the possibility entirely - and the wrong choice when the obstacle is personal. ❌忙(いそが)しくて行(い)きようがない is a common learner error; being busy is your circumstance, not an absence of method, so it should be 忙(いそが)しくて行(い)けない.

～ようがない / ～ようもない · ～ようがない vs ～がたい

Rule of thumb - がたい is hard; ようがない is impossible. がたい concedes that the thing could be done and says you can hardly bring yourself to; ようがない says the question does not arise.

彼(かれ)の説明(せつめい)は理解(りかい)しがたいが、一応(いちおう)聞(き)いておこう。
His explanation is hard to make sense of, but let's hear him out anyway.

資料(しりょう)が一(いち)枚(まい)もなくては、理解(りかい)しようがない。
Without a single document to go on, there's no way to understand it.

The give-away is what can follow. がたい sits comfortably before が or けれど, because there is another side. ようがない normally ends the matter.

～ようがない / ～ようもない · ～ようがない vs 仕方(しかた)がない

Rule of thumb - 仕方(しかた)がない is about accepting a situation: it can't be helped. ようがない is about an action having no method: there's no way to do it.

電車(でんしゃ)が止(と)まってしまったのだから、仕方(しかた)がない。
The trains have stopped, so there's nothing for it.

地図(ちず)も電波(でんぱ)もないので、道(みち)を調(しら)べようがない。
With no map and no signal, there's no way to look up the route.

仕方(しかた)がない stands alone as a comment on the whole state of affairs and attaches to nothing. ようがない must attach to a verb stem and names a specific action that cannot be carried out. They often appear in the same breath: 調(しら)べようがないのだから、仕方(しかた)がない.

～ようがない / ～ようもない · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 直(なお)すようがない → ✅ 直(なお)しようがない
    The suffix takes the ます-stem.

  • ❌ 行(い)こうがない → ✅ 行(い)きようがない
    The volitional 行(い)こう is a different form entirely. よう here is the noun 様(よう), and it follows the stem.

  • ❌ 連絡(れんらく)するようがない → ✅ 連絡(れんらく)しようがない
    With a する-verb, the stem is し.

  • ❌ 直(なお)しようがないだ → ✅ 直(なお)しようがない
    It ends in ない, so it is an い-adjective and takes no だ. Polite is 直(なお)しようがありません.

  • ❌ 眠(ねむ)くて勉強(べんきょう)しようがない。 → ✅ 眠(ねむ)くて勉強(べんきょう)できない。
    Being sleepy is a personal state, not a missing method. ようがない needs the means themselves to be absent.

～ようがない / ～ようもない · Practice 1

1. メールアドレスも電話(でんわ)番号(ばんごう)も分(わ)からないので、知(し)らせ____。

知(し)らせようがない。

Every possible channel is missing, so there is literally no method - which is what ようがない claims. Note the ます-stem 知(し)らせ, and that the whole thing behaves as an い-adjective.

～ようがない / ～ようもない · Practice 2

2. Choose: 説明(せつめい)が丁寧(ていねい)すぎるくらいなので、作(つく)り方(かた)を（間違(まちが)えようがない・間違(まちが)えがたい）。

作(つく)り方(かた)を間違(まちが)えようがない。

This is the confident use: the instructions are so thorough that no route to a mistake exists. 間違(まちが)えがたい is not a combination Japanese uses, and がたい would in any case only mean "hard to", not "impossible".

～ようがない / ～ようもない · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "The shop has closed down, so there's no way to complain about it now."

店(みせ)がなくなってしまったので、今(いま)となっては文句(もんく)の言(い)いようがない。

The shop's disappearance removes the means, not the speaker's ability - exactly the distinction the pattern draws. 言(い)いようがない sits on the ます-stem of 言(い)う.

～ようがない / ～ようもない · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 時間(じかん)がなくて、昨日(きのう)は返事(へんじ)しようがなかったです。

✅ 時間(じかん)がなくて、昨日(きのう)は返事(へんじ)できませんでした。

Lack of time is a personal circumstance, so the potential negative is what is wanted. ようがない would claim that no channel for replying existed at all - which would be true if the phone were dead, but not merely because you were busy.

～ようがない / ～ようもない · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) この時計(とけい)は直(なお)せない。 (b) この時計(とけい)は直(なお)しようがない。

(a) says the speaker cannot repair it. Somebody with more skill, better tools or the right parts might well be able to, and the sentence quietly invites you to take it somewhere else.

(b) closes that door. There is no method - the parts do not exist, or the damage is past any repair - so taking it elsewhere would be pointless. It is a statement about the watch rather than about the speaker.

～込む・～合う・～直す・～慣れる

Meaning · four suffixes that bolt a direction, a partner, a second attempt or a habit onto a verb

Japanese builds a great deal of its vocabulary by welding a second verb onto the ます-stem of a first. These four are the ones an N3 learner meets constantly: ～込(こ)む sends the action inward or deep, ～合(あ)う makes it mutual, ～直(なお)す makes it a second go, and ～慣(な)れる says you have done it often enough to be comfortable.

～込む・～合う・～直す・～慣れる · Formation

All four attach to the ます-stem and produce an ordinary verb. 込(こ)む, 合(あ)う and 直(なお)す are う-verbs; 慣(な)れる is a る-verb.

～込む・～合う・～直す・～慣れる · Formation (cont.)

Attaches to Form Example
Verb (ます-stem) - inward ます-stem + 込(こ)む 飛(と)びます → 飛(と)び込(こ)む
Verb (ます-stem) - deeply, at length ます-stem + 込(こ)む 考(かんが)えます → 考(かんが)え込(こ)む
Verb (ます-stem) - mutually ます-stem + 合(あ)う 話(はな)します → 話(はな)し合(あ)う
Verb (ます-stem) - do over ます-stem + 直(なお)す 書(か)きます → 書(か)き直(なお)す
Verb (ます-stem) - get used to doing ます-stem + 慣(な)れる 使(つか)います → 使(つか)い慣(な)れる
Usual shape of the last one ～慣(な)れて + いる 使(つか)い慣(な)れている
Noun instead of a stem Noun + に + 慣(な)れる 生活(せいかつ)に慣(な)れる

～込む・～合う・～直す・～慣れる · Formation (cont.)

Note - these are word-formation, not free grammar. Each combination is a separate item of vocabulary, and only some combinations exist: 考(かんが)え込(こ)む is a word, ❌走(はし)り込(こ)む in the sense "run inward" mostly is not. 込(こ)む is the least predictable of the four - 使(つか)い込(こ)む means both "use for years" and "embezzle" - so treat new combinations as vocabulary to look up rather than forms to generate. 直(なお)す and 合(あ)う are the two you can most safely build yourself.

～込む・～合う・～直す・～慣れる · Examples

この漢字(かんじ)は間違(まちが)えたので、書(か)き直(なお)します。
I got this kanji wrong, so I'll write it again.

クラスのみんなで話(はな)し合(あ)った。
The whole class talked it over together.

子(こ)どもが水(みず)たまりに飛(と)び込(こ)んだ。
The child jumped into the puddle.

この道具(どうぐ)は十年(じゅうねん)使(つか)っているので、すっかり使(つか)い慣(な)れている。
I've been using this tool for ten years, so it's completely second nature by now.

部長(ぶちょう)は質問(しつもん)を聞(き)いて、しばらく考(かんが)え込(こ)んでいた。
The manager heard the question and sat there thinking hard for a while.

～込む・～合う・～直す・～慣れる · Examples (cont.)

留学(りゅうがく)をやめるかどうか、もう一度(いちど)考(かんが)え直(なお)してみることにした。
I decided to give the question of quitting the exchange programme another think.

困(こま)ったときに助(たす)け合(あ)える友(とも)だちが近(ちか)くにいるのは、ありがたいことだ。
It's a blessing to have friends nearby who can help each other out when things go wrong.

東京(とうきょう)の満員(まんいん)電車(でんしゃ)にもだんだん乗(の)り慣(な)れてきて、今(いま)では本(ほん)も読(よ)める。
I've gradually got used to Tokyo's packed trains, and these days I can even read on them.

駅(えき)で昔(むかし)の友人(ゆうじん)に会(あ)い、喫茶店(きっさてん)で二(に)時間(じかん)も話(はな)し込(こ)んでしまった。
I ran into an old friend at the station and ended up talking for two solid hours in a coffee shop.

～込む・～合う・～直す・～慣れる · Nuance & register

All four are ordinary, register-neutral vocabulary. Nothing about them is formal or casual; what varies is how freely you can invent new combinations.

～直(なお)す is the most productive and the most useful for a learner. Almost any deliberate action can be redone: 書(か)き直(なお)す, 読(よ)み直(なお)す, 考(かんが)え直(なお)す, 作(つく)り直(なお)す, やり直(なお)す. It always implies the first attempt was unsatisfactory, which is why it does not work for pleasant repetition - you do not 聞(き)き直(なお)す a song you love, you listen again.

～合(あ)う requires two or more parties acting on each other. That is a real constraint: 助(たす)け合(あ)う needs both sides helping, and if only one person helps, it is plain 助(たす)ける. This use produces a warm, cooperative tone, which is why 話(はな)し合(あ)い is the word for a proper discussion rather than an argument.

～込む・～合う・～直す・～慣れる · Nuance & register (cont.)

～込(こ)む splits into a physical direction（飛(と)び込(こ)む, 乗(の)り込(こ)む, 流(なが)れ込(こ)む）and an intensity or duration sense（考(かんが)え込(こ)む, 話(はな)し込(こ)む, 黙(だま)り込(こ)む, 教(おし)え込(こ)む）. The second group tends to suggest being absorbed to the point of losing track of the outside world.

～慣(な)れる almost always appears as ～慣(な)れている or ～慣(な)れてくる, because it names a state you have arrived at rather than an event. And keep it apart from its noun-taking twin: 日本(にほん)の生活(せいかつ)に慣(な)れる takes a noun and に; 日本語(にほんご)で話(はな)し慣(な)れる takes a stem.

～直(なお)す vs もう一度(いちど)～する

Rule of thumb - もう一度(いちど) is neutral repetition. ～直(なお)す says the first attempt was wrong or inadequate and this is the corrected version.

好(す)きな映画(えいが)なので、**もう一度(いちど)**見(み)たい。
It's a film I love, so I'd like to watch it again.

名前(なまえ)が抜(ぬ)けていたので、申込書(もうしこみしょ)を書(か)き直(なお)した。
The name was missing, so I filled the application form in again.

Swap them and the meanings shift audibly. 映画(えいが)を見(み)直(なお)す would mean watching it again to check something, or - in another sense - coming to think better of it. もう一度(いちど)書(か)く is simply writing a second copy, with no implication that the first was faulty.

～合(あ)う vs 一緒(いっしょ)に～する

Rule of thumb - ～合(あ)う means the action goes between the participants. 一緒(いっしょ)に means they do it side by side, usually to something or someone else.

三人(さんにん)で荷物(にもつ)を一緒(いっしょ)に運(はこ)んだ。
The three of us carried the luggage together.

困(こま)っている人(ひと)を、みんなで助(たす)け合(あ)おう。
Let's all help each other out when we're in trouble.

The luggage is a third party; nobody is carrying anybody. That is why ❌運(はこ)び合(あ)う is odd. With 助(たす)ける, the sentence only takes 合(あ)う if the helping flows in both directions - 一緒(いっしょ)に助(たす)ける would mean two people helping a third.

～込む・～合う・～直す・～慣れる · ～込(こ)む vs ～出(だ)す

Rule of thumb - as directional suffixes they are opposites: 込(こ)む points in, 出(だ)す points out. Note that 出(だ)す also has the unrelated "suddenly begin" sense you met at N4.

かばんから財布(さいふ)を取(と)り出(だ)した。
I took my wallet out of my bag.

ポケットに切符(きっぷ)を押(お)し込(こ)んだ。
I shoved the ticket into my pocket.

The same verb takes both and the pair is worth memorising: 飛(と)び出(だ)す ("dash out, leap out") against 飛(と)び込(こ)む ("dive in").

～込む・～合う・～直す・～慣れる · ～込(こ)む vs ～出(だ)す (cont.)

The trap is that 出(だ)す has a second job - marking the sudden start of an action, as in 泣(な)き出(だ)す - and that job has nothing to do with direction. Context tells them apart: 走(はし)り出(だ)す is "break into a run", not "run outwards".

～込む・～合う・～直す・～慣れる · Common mistakes

  • ❌ 書(か)く直(なお)す → ✅ 書(か)き直(なお)す
    All four attach to the ます-stem.

  • ❌ 先生(せんせい)に相談(そうだん)し合(あ)った。 → ✅ 先生(せんせい)に相談(そうだん)した。
    ～合(あ)う needs the action to run both ways. Consulting a teacher goes one way, so the plain verb is correct.

  • ❌ 日本(にほん)の生活(せいかつ)を住(す)み慣(な)れた。 → ✅ 日本(にほん)の生活(せいかつ)に慣(な)れた。
    With a noun, use に慣(な)れる. The stem form needs a verb in front of it: この町(まち)に住(す)み慣(な)れた.

  • ❌ この問題(もんだい)をもう一度(いちど)考(かんが)え込(こ)もう。 → ✅ この問題(もんだい)をもう一度(いちど)考(かんが)え直(なお)そう。
    考(かんが)え込(こ)む is to brood, absorbed and silent. To rethink something is 考(かんが)え直(なお)す.

～込む・～合う・～直す・～慣れる · Common mistakes (cont.)

  • ❌ このパソコンは使(つか)い慣(な)れる。 → ✅ このパソコンは使(つか)い慣(な)れている。
    The suffix names a state you have reached, so the ている form is what you almost always want.

～込む・～合う・～直す・～慣れる · Practice 1

1. 漢字(かんじ)を一(いち)文字(もじ)間違(まちが)えたので、はじめから書(か)き____。

はじめから書(か)き直(なお)した。

The first version was faulty, which is exactly what ～直(なお)す marks. 直(なお)す is an う-verb, so the past is 直(なお)した.

～込む・～合う・～直す・～慣れる · Practice 2

2. Choose: 会議(かいぎ)では全員(ぜんいん)で意見(いけん)を（言(い)い合(あ)った・一緒(いっしょ)に言(い)った）。

全員(ぜんいん)で意見(いけん)を言(い)い合(あ)った。

Opinions were exchanged between the participants, which is the mutual sense. 一緒(いっしょ)に言(い)った would mean everyone said the same thing in unison, which is not what a meeting is.

～込む・～合う・～直す・～慣れる · Practice 3

3. Translate into Japanese: "I've got used to cooking for myself, so it doesn't bother me any more."

自分(じぶん)で料理(りょうり)を作(つく)り慣(な)れたので、もう苦(く)にならない。

作(つく)り慣(な)れる takes the ます-stem because the thing you are used to is an action. Had the sentence been about being used to life alone, it would need the noun pattern 一人(ひとり)暮(ぐ)らしに慣(な)れる.

～込む・～合う・～直す・～慣れる · Practice 4

4. Find and fix the error: 電車(でんしゃ)が来(き)たので、いそいで乗(の)り出(だ)した。

✅ 電車(でんしゃ)が来(き)たので、いそいで乗(の)り込(こ)んだ。

Getting onto a train is movement inward, so the suffix is 込(こ)む. 乗(の)り出(だ)す exists but means "lean out" or "launch into a venture" - not boarding.

～込む・～合う・～直す・～慣れる · Practice 5

5. Explain the difference in nuance: (a) 彼(かれ)は黙(だま)って下(した)を見(み)ていた。 (b) 彼(かれ)は黙(だま)り込(こ)んでしまった。

(a) simply describes what he was doing: not speaking, eyes down. It is a neutral piece of stage direction and says nothing about why.

(b) says he shut down into silence and stayed there. The 込(こ)む supplies both depth and duration - he has gone somewhere inward - and しまった adds that this was not what anyone wanted. It is a much heavier silence than (a).
